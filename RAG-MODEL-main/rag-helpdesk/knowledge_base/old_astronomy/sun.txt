SUN

1. OVERVIEW
   The Sun is the star at the center of the Solar System.
   The Sun is the primary source of energy for Earth.
   The Sun is a G-type main-sequence star.
   The Sun is classified as a G2V star.
   The Sun formed approximately 4.6 billion years ago.
   The Sun contains most of the mass in the Solar System.
   The Sun is located at the center of the Solar System's gravitational system.
   All eight planets orbit the Sun.
   The Sun also gravitationally influences asteroids, comets, and other small bodies.
   The Sun is approximately 150 million kilometers from Earth on average.
   This average Earth-Sun distance is called one astronomical unit.
   The Sun is much larger than Earth.
   The Sun has a diameter of approximately 1.39 million kilometers.
   The Sun's mass is approximately 333,000 times the mass of Earth.
   The Sun is composed primarily of hydrogen and helium.
   Hydrogen is the most abundant element in the Sun.
   Helium is the second most abundant element in the Sun.
   The Sun is a nearly spherical object.
   The Sun does not have a solid surface like Earth.

2. COMPOSITION
   The Sun is composed mainly of hydrogen and helium.
   Hydrogen makes up most of the Sun's mass.
   Helium accounts for most of the remaining mass.
   The Sun also contains small amounts of heavier elements.
   Elements heavier than helium are collectively called heavier elements in stellar astronomy.
   The composition of the Sun reflects material present in the cloud from which it formed.
   Nuclear fusion changes the composition of the Sun's core over time.
   Hydrogen nuclei are converted into helium through nuclear fusion.
   The fusion process releases energy.
   The released energy ultimately reaches the solar surface.
   Spectroscopy is used to study the chemical composition of the Sun.
   Absorption lines in the solar spectrum provide information about elements.
   The solar spectrum contains characteristic signatures of different elements.

3. INTERNAL STRUCTURE
   The Sun has several major structural regions.
   The core is the central region of the Sun.
   Nuclear fusion occurs primarily in the core.
   The core has extremely high temperature and pressure.
   The radiative zone surrounds the core.
   Energy moves outward through the radiative zone mainly by radiation.
   The convection zone lies above the radiative zone.
   Energy is transported through the convection zone mainly by convection.
   Hot plasma rises while cooler plasma sinks in the convection zone.
   The movement of plasma contributes to solar magnetic activity.
   The photosphere is the visible layer associated with the solar surface.
   The chromosphere lies above the photosphere.
   The corona is the outer atmosphere of the Sun.
   The corona extends far into interplanetary space.

4. NUCLEAR FUSION
   The Sun produces energy through nuclear fusion.
   Fusion takes place primarily in the solar core.
   The main fusion process in the Sun is the proton-proton chain.
   Hydrogen nuclei participate in the proton-proton chain.
   The overall process converts hydrogen into helium.
   A small amount of mass is converted into energy.
   The energy is released according to the relationship between mass and energy.
   Fusion maintains the Sun's energy output.
   The energy generated in the core initially exists in energetic particles and radiation.
   Energy takes a long time to move from the interior toward the surface.
   The solar energy eventually leaves the photosphere as electromagnetic radiation.
   Earth receives only a small fraction of the Sun's total emitted energy.

5. PHOTOSPHERE
   The photosphere is the visible layer of the Sun.
   Most visible sunlight originates from the photosphere.
   The photosphere has a temperature of roughly 5,500 degrees Celsius.
   The photosphere has a granular appearance.
   Solar granules are associated with convection beneath the photosphere.
   Granules are relatively small bright regions surrounded by darker boundaries.
   The photosphere is not a solid surface.
   It is a layer of relatively dense solar plasma.
   Sunspots can appear on the photosphere.
   Sunspots appear darker because they are cooler than surrounding regions.
   Sunspots are associated with strong magnetic fields.
   The number and distribution of sunspots vary with solar activity.

6. CHROMOSPHERE
   The chromosphere is a layer of the Sun's atmosphere.
   It lies above the photosphere.
   The chromosphere can appear reddish during a total solar eclipse.
   The chromosphere contains structures influenced by magnetic fields.
   Spicules are narrow jets of plasma observed in the chromosphere.
   The temperature generally increases with altitude through the chromosphere.
   The chromosphere is less dense than the photosphere.
   Spectroscopic observations are useful for studying the chromosphere.
   Solar prominences can extend through regions of the solar atmosphere.

7. CORONA
   The corona is the outermost major layer of the solar atmosphere.
   The corona extends millions of kilometers from the visible solar surface.
   The corona is extremely hot compared with the photosphere.
   Coronal temperatures can reach millions of degrees Celsius.
   The low density of the corona allows it to extend far into space.
   The corona is visible during a total solar eclipse.
   Special instruments called coronagraphs can observe the corona by blocking direct sunlight.
   The corona contains plasma influenced by the Sun's magnetic field.
   Coronal holes are regions where magnetic field structures allow enhanced solar wind escape.
   The solar wind originates in the Sun's outer atmosphere.

8. SUNSPOTS
   Sunspots are dark regions visible on the photosphere.
   Sunspots are cooler than the surrounding photosphere.
   Strong magnetic fields are concentrated within sunspot regions.
   Sunspots can appear individually or in groups.
   Sunspot groups can become large and complex.
   Solar activity changes over an approximately 11-year cycle.
   Sunspot numbers generally increase and decrease during the solar cycle.
   Periods of high activity are called solar maximum.
   Periods of lower activity are called solar minimum.
   Sunspots help scientists track changes in solar magnetic activity.
   Large active regions can produce solar flares and other eruptions.

9. SOLAR FLARES
   A solar flare is a sudden release of energy from the Sun.
   Solar flares are associated with magnetic activity in the solar atmosphere.
   Flares can produce intense electromagnetic radiation.
   Solar flares can occur near sunspot groups and active regions.
   Flare radiation can include X-rays and ultraviolet radiation.
   Strong solar flares can affect Earth's upper atmosphere.
   Solar flares can interfere with some radio communications.
   Solar flares are different from coronal mass ejections.
   A flare is primarily a rapid energy release.
   A coronal mass ejection involves a large eruption of plasma and magnetic field.
   Both phenomena can be associated with active solar regions.

10. CORONAL MASS EJECTIONS
    A coronal mass ejection is a large eruption of magnetized plasma from the Sun.
    Coronal mass ejections are commonly abbreviated as CMEs.
    A CME can contain billions of tons of solar material.
    A CME can travel through interplanetary space.
    If directed toward Earth, a CME can interact with Earth's magnetosphere.
    Strong interactions can contribute to geomagnetic storms.
    Geomagnetic storms can affect satellites and communication systems.
    They can also produce enhanced auroras.
    Not every CME is directed toward Earth.
    The effects of a CME depend on its speed, direction, magnetic properties, and interaction with Earth's magnetosphere.

11. SOLAR WIND
    The solar wind is a continuous flow of charged particles from the Sun.
    The solar wind consists mainly of protons and electrons.
    The solar wind travels outward through the Solar System.
    The solar wind carries the Sun's magnetic field into interplanetary space.
    This extended magnetic field is called the interplanetary magnetic field.
    The solar wind interacts with planetary magnetic fields.
    Earth's magnetosphere provides significant protection from the solar wind.
    Solar wind interactions contribute to auroras near Earth's polar regions.
    The solar wind also affects the tails of comets.
    Solar wind conditions vary with solar activity.

12. SOLAR CYCLE
    Solar activity changes over a cycle lasting approximately 11 years.
    The solar cycle is associated with changes in the Sun's magnetic field.
    Sunspot numbers generally rise and fall during the cycle.
    Solar maximum is a period of relatively high solar activity.
    Solar minimum is a period of relatively low solar activity.
    The magnetic polarity of the Sun reverses as part of the solar cycle.
    Solar activity can influence space weather near Earth.
    Scientists monitor sunspots, flares, CMEs, and other indicators of solar activity.
    Solar observations help researchers understand changes in the Sun's magnetic environment.

13. SUN AND EARTH
    The Sun provides most of the energy that drives Earth's climate system.
    Solar radiation warms Earth's surface and atmosphere.
    The Sun influences Earth's space environment through the solar wind.
    Earth's magnetic field interacts with charged particles from the Sun.
    Solar activity can produce changes in Earth's upper atmosphere.
    Strong solar storms can affect satellites and radio communication.
    Solar activity can also increase radiation exposure for astronauts and spacecraft.
    The Sun's ultraviolet radiation contributes to atmospheric and biological processes on Earth.
    Earth's distance from the Sun allows liquid water to exist on its surface under suitable atmospheric conditions.

14. SOLAR OBSERVATION
    Scientists observe the Sun using ground-based and space-based instruments.
    Different wavelengths reveal different layers and processes.
    Visible-light observations show features such as sunspots.
    Ultraviolet observations reveal hot atmospheric structures.
    X-ray observations reveal extremely energetic solar regions.
    Spectroscopy provides information about solar composition and motion.
    Space-based solar observatories can observe wavelengths blocked by Earth's atmosphere.
    Solar telescopes are designed to safely observe the Sun.
    Directly looking at the Sun without proper protection can damage the eyes.
    Solar observations are important for understanding space weather.

15. SCIENTIFIC IMPORTANCE
    The Sun provides a nearby example of a typical main-sequence star.
    Studying the Sun helps scientists understand stellar physics.
    Solar observations help researchers study nuclear fusion indirectly through its effects.
    The Sun provides a natural laboratory for plasma physics.
    Solar magnetic activity helps scientists study magnetic fields in stars.
    Understanding the Sun improves prediction of space weather.
    Space weather forecasting is important for satellites and communication systems.
    The Sun also provides context for studying other stars.
    Comparing the Sun with other stars helps scientists understand stellar evolution.
    The Sun will eventually change as its core hydrogen supply is depleted.

16. KEY TERMS
    Solar core: The central region where nuclear fusion occurs.
    Photosphere: The visible layer of the Sun.
    Chromosphere: The atmospheric layer above the photosphere.
    Corona: The Sun's extended outer atmosphere.
    Sunspot: A relatively cool, dark region associated with strong magnetic fields.
    Solar flare: A sudden release of energy from the Sun.
    Coronal mass ejection: A large eruption of magnetized solar plasma.
    Solar wind: A continuous flow of charged particles from the Sun.
    Solar cycle: The approximately 11-year cycle of changing solar activity.
    Solar maximum: A period of relatively high solar activity.
    Solar minimum: A period of relatively low solar activity.
    Astronomical unit: The approximate average distance between Earth and the Sun.
    Nuclear fusion: A process in which atomic nuclei combine and release energy.
    Photosphere granulation: A visible pattern produced by convection in the solar atmosphere.
    Space weather: Changing environmental conditions in space caused partly by solar activity.
