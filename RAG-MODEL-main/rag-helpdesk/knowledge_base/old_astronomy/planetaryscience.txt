PLANETARY SCIENCE
Planetary science is the study of planets, moons, dwarf planets, asteroids, comets, rings, and other planetary bodies.
It examines how planetary systems form, evolve, and interact with their surrounding environments.
Planetary science combines astronomy, geology, atmospheric science, chemistry, physics, and space science.
Comparative planetology compares different planetary bodies to understand their similarities and differences.
A planetary system contains a star and the bodies that orbit it.
Planetary bodies can be studied through telescopic observations, spacecraft measurements, laboratory experiments, and computer models.

PLANETARY SYSTEM FORMATION
Planetary systems generally form from rotating disks of gas and dust surrounding young stars.
A protoplanetary disk contains gas, dust, and solid material around a newly forming star.
Dust grains can collide and stick together to form progressively larger solid particles.
Planetesimals are relatively large solid bodies that form during planetary accretion.
Protoplanets are growing planetary bodies formed through repeated accretion of material.
Accretion is the gradual growth of a body through the accumulation of matter.
Gravity becomes increasingly important as growing bodies attract additional material.
Collisions between planetary embryos can produce larger planets.
Differentiation occurs when a planetary body separates into layers according to material density and composition.
Dense materials such as iron can sink toward a planetary center during differentiation.
Lighter silicate materials can form mantles and crusts above denser interiors.
Planetary migration occurs when gravitational interactions change a planet's orbit.
Orbital resonances occur when orbital periods of bodies have simple numerical relationships.
Resonances can influence the long-term stability and evolution of planetary systems.
Volatiles such as water and carbon-bearing compounds can be incorporated into planetary bodies.
Impacts can transport material between different regions of a planetary system.

PLANET TYPES
Terrestrial planets are relatively small, rocky planets with solid surfaces.
Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars are the Solar System's terrestrial planets.
Gas giants are large planets dominated by hydrogen and helium.
Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants.
Ice giants contain substantial amounts of water, ammonia, methane, and other heavier compounds beneath their outer atmospheres.
Uranus and Neptune are ice giants.
Dwarf planets orbit the Sun and are massive enough for gravity to make them nearly round but have not cleared their orbital neighborhoods.
Small bodies include asteroids, comets, meteoroids, and other minor objects.
Planetary classification is based on physical properties, composition, orbital behavior, and formation history.

PLANETARY INTERIORS
A differentiated rocky planet commonly contains a core, mantle, and crust.
The core is the deepest major internal layer of a differentiated rocky planet.
Planetary cores can contain substantial amounts of metallic iron and other dense elements.
The mantle lies between the core and crust of a rocky planet.
The mantle is generally composed of silicate minerals and rocks.
The crust is the outer solid rocky layer of a terrestrial planet.
Planetary density provides information about internal composition and structure.
Gravity measurements can reveal variations in subsurface mass distribution.
Internal heat can originate from formation, radioactive decay, and other planetary processes.
A planetary dynamo can generate a global magnetic field when electrically conducting material moves inside a planet.
Not every planetary body has an active global magnetic field.
Planetary interiors can change as bodies cool, contract, differentiate, or experience geological activity.

PLANETARY SURFACES
Planetary surfaces record the effects of impacts, volcanism, tectonics, erosion, and weathering.
Impact craters form when asteroids, comets, or other bodies collide with a planetary surface.
Crater density can provide information about the relative ages of planetary surfaces.
Volcanism occurs when molten or partially molten material reaches or affects a planetary surface.
Lava flows can create extensive volcanic plains.
Tectonics involves the movement, deformation, or fracturing of planetary crust.
Faults are fractures in planetary crust along which movement can occur.
Mountain ranges can form through tectonic deformation or large impacts.
Regolith is the loose layer of fragmented material covering many planetary surfaces.
Regolith can contain dust, rock fragments, impact debris, and altered minerals.
Weathering changes surface materials through physical or chemical processes.
Erosion transports or removes surface material through processes such as wind or flowing liquid.
Some planetary bodies have surfaces strongly modified by atmospheric processes.
Airless bodies are generally dominated by impact-related surface processes and space weathering.
Space weathering changes exposed surface materials through radiation and impacts from small particles.

PLANETARY ATMOSPHERES
An atmosphere is a layer of gas gravitationally bound to a planetary body.
Atmospheric composition differs greatly among planets and moons.
Atmospheric pressure depends on the amount and distribution of gas above a surface.
Temperature varies with altitude, latitude, season, surface properties, and atmospheric composition.
Atmospheric circulation transports heat and chemical constituents around a planetary body.
Winds are large-scale movements of atmospheric gases.
Clouds form when atmospheric gases or vapors condense into particles or droplets.
The greenhouse effect occurs when atmospheric gases absorb and re-emit thermal radiation.
A strong greenhouse effect can substantially increase a planet's surface temperature.
Atmospheric escape occurs when particles leave a planet's gravitational influence.
Light gases generally escape more easily than heavier gases.
Solar radiation can alter atmospheric chemistry and contribute to atmospheric loss.
The ionosphere is a region of an atmosphere containing significant numbers of charged particles.
Auroral emissions can occur when energetic charged particles interact with an atmosphere.
Atmospheric observations can reveal composition, temperature, pressure, winds, and chemical processes.

MAGNETOSPHERES
A magnetosphere is the region around a planetary body influenced by its magnetic field.
Earth has a global magnetic field and a large magnetosphere.
The solar wind is a stream of charged particles flowing outward from the Sun.
The solar wind interacts with planetary magnetic fields.
Magnetospheres can deflect or redirect many charged particles from the solar wind.
Magnetic fields can influence the interaction between a planet and its space environment.
Charged particles can become trapped in planetary radiation belts.
Auroras occur when energetic charged particles produce light in an upper atmosphere.
Magnetospheric activity can change during variations in solar activity.
Magnetic-field measurements help scientists investigate planetary interiors and space environments.

WATER AND VOLATILES
Water can exist as ice, liquid, or vapor depending on environmental conditions.
Planetary water can be stored in surface ice, subsurface reservoirs, minerals, or atmospheres.
Water ice is common in the outer Solar System and in permanently shadowed regions of some bodies.
Liquid water requires suitable combinations of temperature and pressure.
Subsurface oceans are liquid-water reservoirs located beneath an outer crust or ice shell.
Hydrated minerals contain water or hydroxyl groups within their crystal structures.
Volatiles are substances that can readily vaporize under planetary surface conditions.
Important planetary volatiles include water, carbon dioxide, methane, ammonia, and nitrogen.
Volatile distribution provides clues about planetary formation and evolution.
Sublimation is the direct transition of a substance from solid to gas.
Condensation is the transition of a gas or vapor into a liquid or solid.
Volatile loss can alter the long-term composition and climate of a planetary body.

MOONS AND TIDAL PROCESSES
Natural satellites are bodies that orbit planets, dwarf planets, or other larger bodies.
Moons can have rocky, icy, or mixed compositions.
Some moons formed alongside their parent planets, while others were captured or produced by impacts.
Tides are gravitationally driven deformations of a planetary body or its oceans and atmosphere.
Tidal forces arise mainly from differences in gravitational pull across an extended body.
Tidal locking occurs when a moon's rotation period becomes synchronized with its orbital period.
A tidally locked moon keeps approximately the same hemisphere facing its parent body.
Tidal heating converts orbital and rotational energy into internal heat through deformation.
Tidal heating can drive geological or volcanic activity inside some moons.
Tidal interactions can gradually change the rotation and orbital properties of planetary bodies.
The study of moons provides information about planetary formation, evolution, and internal activity.

PLANETARY RINGS
Planetary rings are systems of particles orbiting a planet.
Ring particles can consist of ice, rock, dust, or mixtures of these materials.
Saturn has the most prominent ring system in the Solar System.
Jupiter, Uranus, and Neptune also have ring systems.
Ring particles can range from microscopic dust to larger solid fragments.
Gravitational interactions can create gaps, waves, and structures within rings.
Small moons can influence ring particles through gravitational interactions.
Shepherd moons can help confine or shape narrow ring structures.
The Roche limit describes a region where tidal forces can prevent a loosely bound body from remaining intact.
Ring systems can evolve through collisions, gravitational interactions, and particle loss.

SMALL BODIES
Asteroids are mostly rocky or metallic small bodies that orbit the Sun.
Comets contain substantial amounts of volatile ices mixed with dust and rocky material.
Meteoroids are relatively small natural objects moving through interplanetary space.
A meteor is the visible phenomenon produced when a meteoroid enters an atmosphere and heats surrounding gas.
A meteorite is a meteoroid fragment that survives atmospheric passage and reaches a planetary surface.
Asteroid composition can provide clues about conditions in the early Solar System.
Comet activity can produce a coma and tail when solar heating releases volatile material.
Small bodies preserve material that can provide evidence about early planetary-system conditions.

COMPARATIVE PLANETOLOGY
Mercury has a large metallic core relative to its planetary size.
Venus has a dense atmosphere and an extremely hot surface.
Earth has stable surface liquid water and a nitrogen-rich atmosphere with substantial oxygen.
Mars has a thin atmosphere and evidence of extensive past interaction with liquid water.
The Moon has a rocky surface with extensive impact cratering and very little atmosphere.
Jupiter is the largest planet in the Solar System and is dominated by hydrogen and helium.
Saturn is a gas giant distinguished by its extensive and bright ring system.
Uranus and Neptune are ice giants with hydrogen, helium, and methane in their outer atmospheres.
Comparing planets helps identify which processes are controlled by size, composition, distance from the Sun, or geological history.
Planetary similarities can reveal shared formation processes.
Planetary differences can reveal how environments evolve under different physical conditions.

PLANETARY EXPLORATION
A flyby spacecraft observes a planetary body during a close passage without entering orbit.
An orbiter travels around a planetary body and can make repeated observations.
A lander reaches a planetary surface and performs measurements from a fixed location.
A rover moves across a planetary surface while collecting scientific observations.
Sample-return missions collect material and transport it back to Earth for laboratory analysis.
Spectroscopy identifies materials by analyzing how they emit, absorb, or reflect electromagnetic radiation.
Radar can investigate surfaces and subsurface structures.
Gravimetry measures variations in a body's gravitational field.
Magnetometry measures magnetic fields around or on planetary bodies.
Seismology uses seismic waves to investigate internal planetary structure.
Thermal measurements reveal surface and subsurface temperature properties.
Imaging provides information about terrain, geology, morphology, and surface changes.
Spacecraft instruments often combine multiple measurement techniques to study the same planetary process.

HABITABILITY AND PLANETARY EVOLUTION
Planetary habitability refers to the potential of an environment to support conditions suitable for life.
Liquid water is considered an important factor in many habitability studies.
Habitability also depends on available energy sources and chemically useful elements.
A stable environment can help maintain potentially habitable conditions over long periods.
Habitability does not mean that life is actually present.
Scientists study atmospheric composition, surface chemistry, temperature, and water availability when assessing habitability.
Planetary environments can become less habitable through atmospheric loss, climate changes, impacts, or geological evolution.
Planetary evolution describes long-term changes in a body's interior, surface, atmosphere, and orbital environment.
Cooling can reduce geological activity in smaller planetary bodies.
Large impacts can significantly modify planetary surfaces and atmospheres.
Volcanism can release gases and reshape planetary surfaces.
Atmospheric escape can gradually transform a planet's surface environment.

KEY PLANETARY SCIENCE TERMS
Accretion is the growth of a body through accumulation of matter.
Differentiation is the separation of planetary material into layers based on density and composition.
Regolith is loose fragmented material covering a planetary surface.
Tectonics describes crustal deformation and movement.
Volcanism involves the movement or eruption of molten material.
Atmospheric escape is the loss of atmospheric particles into space.
A magnetosphere is a region controlled by a planetary magnetic field.
Tidal heating is internal heating caused by repeated gravitational deformation.
Comparative planetology uses similarities and differences between worlds to study planetary processes.
Planetary exploration combines spacecraft observations, remote sensing, and direct measurements to investigate planetary bodies.
