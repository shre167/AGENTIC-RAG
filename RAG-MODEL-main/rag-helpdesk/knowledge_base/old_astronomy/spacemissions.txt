SPACE MISSIONS
A space mission is an organized operation designed to perform activities beyond Earth's atmosphere.
Space missions can be robotic, crewed, orbital, planetary, lunar, or astronomical.
Mission objectives determine the spacecraft design, instruments, trajectory, and operations.
A mission can involve one spacecraft or multiple coordinated spacecraft.
Space missions are planned around scientific, technological, commercial, or exploration goals.
Mission planning includes launch, trajectory, communications, operations, and end-of-mission activities.
The spacecraft must survive the mechanical and thermal conditions of launch and spaceflight.
Mission success depends on both hardware reliability and accurate operational planning.
Space missions can last from minutes to several decades.
Data returned by spacecraft can provide information unavailable from ground-based observations.

MISSION TYPES
Earth-observation missions study Earth's atmosphere, land, oceans, and environment.
Communications missions place satellites into orbit to relay information between locations.
Navigation missions provide positioning, navigation, and timing services.
Weather missions monitor atmospheric conditions and support forecasting.
Astronomy missions observe astronomical objects from space.
Planetary missions investigate planets, moons, asteroids, and comets.
Lunar missions study the Moon's surface, interior, environment, and resources.
Solar missions investigate the Sun and its influence on the space environment.
Crewed missions transport astronauts to and from space and support human activities in orbit.
Technology-demonstration missions test new spacecraft systems or instruments.

MISSION PHASES
A typical mission begins with scientific or engineering requirements.
Mission designers select spacecraft systems that satisfy these requirements.
The spacecraft is integrated with its launch vehicle before launch.
Pre-launch testing verifies mechanical, electrical, thermal, and software systems.
The launch phase places the spacecraft onto an initial trajectory.
After launch, the spacecraft may perform deployment and commissioning activities.
Commissioning verifies that instruments and spacecraft subsystems operate correctly.
The operational phase begins when the spacecraft is ready to perform its primary mission.
Mission controllers monitor spacecraft health and send commands from ground stations.
The mission ends when operations are no longer possible or the planned objectives are completed.

LAUNCH VEHICLES
A launch vehicle is a rocket designed to carry a payload into space.
The payload can include satellites, probes, crewed spacecraft, or scientific instruments.
Rocket engines generate thrust by expelling high-speed exhaust gases.
Launch vehicles commonly use multiple stages to improve performance.
A stage can be discarded after its propellant is consumed.
Staging reduces the mass that later rocket stages must accelerate.
Liquid-propellant engines commonly use separate fuel and oxidizer systems.
Solid rocket motors contain propellant in a solid form.
Some launch vehicles combine liquid and solid propulsion technologies.
Launch vehicle selection depends on payload mass, destination, orbit, and mission requirements.

ORBIT
An orbit is a gravitational trajectory followed by an object around another body.
Earth satellites remain in orbit because their forward motion and gravitational acceleration continually interact.
Orbital velocity depends on the mass of the central body and the size of the orbit.
Low Earth orbit is commonly used for Earth observation and many crewed missions.
Geostationary orbit lies above Earth's equator at an altitude where the orbital period matches Earth's rotation.
A geostationary satellite appears fixed above a particular longitude when viewed from Earth.
Polar orbits pass approximately over Earth's polar regions.
Sun-synchronous orbits are designed so a satellite passes locations at similar local solar times.
Elliptical orbits have varying distances from the central body.
Orbital maneuvers change a spacecraft's velocity and therefore change its trajectory.

ESCAPE VELOCITY
Escape velocity is the minimum speed needed to escape the gravitational influence of a body without further propulsion in the idealized case.
Escape velocity depends on the body's mass and the distance from its center.
Earth's escape velocity near the surface is approximately 11.2 kilometers per second.
A spacecraft does not simply accelerate continuously at escape velocity throughout a mission.
Rocket missions use controlled changes in velocity to achieve desired trajectories.
A spacecraft can leave Earth using a launch trajectory with sufficient total energy.
The required energy depends on the launch site, target orbit, payload, and trajectory.
Interplanetary missions require additional velocity beyond a low Earth orbit.
Gravity assists can change spacecraft velocity without using equivalent amounts of onboard propellant.
Trajectory design balances energy, travel time, and mission constraints.

SPACECRAFT
A spacecraft is a vehicle or platform designed to operate in space.
Major spacecraft subsystems include power, communication, thermal control, guidance, navigation, and control.
Scientific spacecraft also carry instruments designed for specific measurements.
Solar panels commonly provide electrical power for missions near the Sun.
Batteries store electrical energy for periods when solar panels cannot provide sufficient power.
Thermal-control systems prevent spacecraft components from becoming excessively hot or cold.
Communication systems transmit commands to spacecraft and return mission data to Earth.
Attitude-control systems maintain or change spacecraft orientation.
Onboard computers execute software that controls spacecraft functions.
Redundancy is often used to improve reliability by providing backup components or systems.

PAYLOAD
The payload is the part of a mission that performs the primary mission objective.
A scientific payload can contain cameras, spectrometers, radar systems, detectors, or other instruments.
Payload instruments are designed around specific scientific measurements.
Payload mass and power requirements influence spacecraft and launch-vehicle design.
Different instruments can observe different wavelengths or physical properties.
A spacecraft may carry multiple instruments operating independently or together.
Calibration is necessary to convert instrument measurements into scientifically useful data.
Payload data can require substantial onboard storage.
Some instruments collect data continuously while others operate according to scheduled commands.
The scientific return of a mission depends strongly on payload capability and reliable operations.

GUIDANCE NAVIGATION AND CONTROL
Guidance determines the desired path or trajectory of a spacecraft.
Navigation determines the spacecraft's position and velocity.
Control systems generate commands that correct spacecraft motion and orientation.
Attitude refers to the spacecraft's orientation relative to a reference frame.
Gyroscopes and star trackers can help determine spacecraft orientation.
Reaction wheels can rotate spacecraft without using propellant.
Thrusters can change spacecraft attitude or orbital velocity.
Ground tracking stations provide measurements that help determine spacecraft trajectory.
Autonomous navigation allows spacecraft to perform some navigation functions without continuous ground commands.
Reliable guidance, navigation, and control are essential for precise planetary and orbital missions.

COMMUNICATION
Spacecraft communicate with Earth using radio-frequency or other electromagnetic signals.
Ground stations contain antennas and electronics for transmitting and receiving signals.
Deep-space missions require highly sensitive communication systems.
Communication delay increases with distance because electromagnetic signals travel at the speed of light.
A command sent to Mars can take several minutes to reach the spacecraft.
The spacecraft therefore cannot always be controlled in real time.
Mission teams use scheduled command sequences for distant spacecraft.
Telemetry contains information about spacecraft health and subsystem status.
Science data are transmitted through the spacecraft communication system.
Deep-space communication requires careful management of antenna pointing, data rates, and power.

GROUND SEGMENT
The ground segment consists of systems and personnel supporting a space mission from Earth.
Ground stations communicate with spacecraft.
Mission-control centers monitor spacecraft health and command operations.
Flight dynamics teams calculate spacecraft trajectories and orbital maneuvers.
Science operations teams plan observations and process scientific data.
Ground software converts received telemetry into useful operational information.
Mission teams schedule communication passes according to spacecraft visibility.
Deep-space networks use geographically separated ground stations to maintain communication coverage.
Ground systems can continue supporting a spacecraft even when it is millions of kilometers away.
A mission is therefore a coordinated system involving both space and ground infrastructure.

TRAJECTORY DESIGN
A trajectory is the path followed by a spacecraft through space.
Mission planners calculate trajectories using gravitational and propulsion effects.
The simplest interplanetary trajectories can be approximated using patched-conic methods.
More precise trajectory calculations account for multiple gravitational bodies and spacecraft maneuvers.
Launch windows are periods when planetary positions allow efficient trajectories.
The relative positions of planets strongly influence interplanetary mission design.
A Hohmann transfer is an energy-efficient idealized transfer between two circular orbits.
Gravity assists use a planet's motion to alter a spacecraft's heliocentric trajectory.
Gravity assists can reduce propellant requirements for some deep-space missions.
Trajectory optimization balances fuel consumption, travel time, scientific opportunities, and mission risk.

GRAVITY ASSIST
A gravity assist uses the gravitational field and orbital motion of a celestial body to change a spacecraft's velocity.
The spacecraft approaches a planet or moon along a planned trajectory.
The body's gravity bends the spacecraft's path.
The spacecraft can gain or lose heliocentric energy depending on the encounter geometry.
The spacecraft does not extract useful energy from the planet without affecting the planet's motion.
The change in the planet's velocity is extremely small because the planet is much more massive.
Gravity assists have been used by many interplanetary spacecraft.
They can enable missions that would otherwise require substantially more propellant.
Multiple gravity assists can be combined in complex trajectories.
Gravity-assist planning requires accurate knowledge of planetary positions and spacecraft state.

PLANETARY MISSIONS
Planetary missions investigate planets and their environments using spacecraft and scientific instruments.
Flyby missions pass a target without entering a long-term orbit around it.
Orbiter missions enter orbit around a target body.
Lander missions descend to a surface and perform measurements from a stationary or mobile platform.
Rover missions travel across a planetary or lunar surface.
Sample-return missions collect material and deliver it back to Earth.
Atmospheric probes descend through the atmosphere of a target planet or moon.
Multiple mission types can be combined in one exploration program.
Planetary missions often require autonomous operations because communication delays limit real-time control.
The choice of mission type depends on scientific objectives, environment, cost, and technical feasibility.

LUNAR MISSIONS
Lunar missions investigate Earth's Moon from orbit, the surface, or other trajectories.
Orbiters can map the Moon's surface and measure its gravitational and geological properties.
Landers can perform measurements directly on the lunar surface.
Rovers can investigate regions surrounding a landing site.
Lunar missions study craters, rocks, soil, minerals, topography, and the lunar environment.
The Moon has no substantial global atmosphere, creating a harsh surface environment.
Lunar temperatures can vary greatly between illuminated and shadowed regions.
Permanently shadowed polar regions can preserve volatile materials such as water ice.
Lunar missions contribute to understanding the Moon's origin and geological history.
The Moon is also an important target for developing technologies for future deep-space exploration.

MARS MISSIONS
Mars is a major target for robotic planetary exploration.
Orbiter missions study the Martian surface, atmosphere, climate, and interior.
Landers and rovers investigate rocks and soil directly on the surface.
Mars has a thin atmosphere dominated by carbon dioxide.
Evidence indicates that liquid water existed on ancient Mars.
Ancient water-related features include dried channels, valleys, and altered minerals.
Mars rovers can analyze rocks and soil to investigate geological history.
Some missions search for environments that could once have supported microbial life.
Mars missions require autonomous navigation because of communication delays.
Future exploration concepts include sample return and eventual human missions.

OUTER PLANET MISSIONS
Jupiter and Saturn have been studied extensively by robotic spacecraft.
The Voyager spacecraft performed historic flybys of the outer planets.
Galileo studied Jupiter and its major moons from orbit.
Cassini investigated Saturn, its rings, atmosphere, and moons.
Jupiter has a powerful magnetic field and extensive radiation environment.
Europa, one of Jupiter's major moons, is of interest because it has evidence for a subsurface ocean.
Saturn's moon Titan has a dense atmosphere and surface lakes of liquid hydrocarbons.
Outer-planet missions require long travel times and careful power management.
Radioisotope power systems can support spacecraft operating far from the Sun.
These missions provide information about giant planets and their diverse moons.

DEEP-SPACE MISSIONS
Deep-space missions travel far beyond Earth's immediate orbital environment.
Communication becomes increasingly difficult as spacecraft distance increases.
Solar energy becomes weaker farther from the Sun.
Radioisotope power systems are useful for missions to the outer Solar System.
Deep-space spacecraft often operate autonomously for significant periods.
Navigation can use ground tracking, optical observations, and onboard sensors.
Long mission durations require components capable of surviving radiation, thermal cycling, and aging.
Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 are examples of long-duration deep-space missions.
New Horizons performed a close flyby of Pluto in 2015.
Deep-space missions can continue producing scientific data long after their primary encounters.

SPACE TELESCOPES
A space telescope is an astronomical observatory placed above Earth's atmosphere.
Space telescopes avoid atmospheric absorption and atmospheric turbulence for many observations.
Different space telescopes are designed to observe different electromagnetic wavelengths.
The Hubble Space Telescope has produced observations across ultraviolet, visible, and near-infrared wavelengths.
The James Webb Space Telescope is optimized primarily for infrared astronomy.
Infrared observations can reveal cool objects and distant galaxies.
Space telescopes can observe wavelengths that are strongly absorbed by Earth's atmosphere.
Observatory missions can remain operational for many years.
Space telescopes expand the range and quality of astronomical observations.
They are important tools for studying stars, galaxies, planets, and the early universe.

MISSION DATA
Telemetry is engineering information sent from a spacecraft to ground systems.
Science data are measurements collected by mission instruments.
Raw data often require calibration and processing before scientific interpretation.
Calibration corrects known instrument responses and systematic effects.
Mission data are commonly stored in standardized scientific data formats.
Data processing can remove artifacts and convert instrument measurements into physical quantities.
Researchers use mission archives to analyze data after the original mission operations end.
Public archives can make scientific observations available to researchers and the wider community.
Data preservation allows future researchers to apply improved analysis methods.
Long-term archives increase the scientific value of space missions.

MISSION RISKS
Space missions face risks during launch, cruise, operations, and data transmission.
Launch failures can prevent a spacecraft from reaching its intended trajectory.
Spacecraft can experience radiation damage to electronics and sensors.
Thermal extremes can damage components if thermal control fails.
Micrometeoroids and orbital debris can impact spacecraft.
Communication failures can prevent ground teams from commanding a spacecraft.
Software errors can cause incorrect spacecraft behavior.
Redundant systems can reduce the impact of individual component failures.
Extensive testing is used to identify weaknesses before launch.
Mission teams continuously assess spacecraft health and operational risk.

MISSION SUCCESS
Mission success is evaluated against predefined technical and scientific objectives.
A mission can remain scientifically valuable even if some secondary objectives fail.
Partial mission success can occur when only some instruments or mission phases operate as planned.
Unexpected discoveries can increase a mission's scientific importance.
Reliable data return is one of the most important measures of scientific mission performance.
Mission duration can exceed the original design lifetime when spacecraft systems remain healthy.
Extended missions can provide additional observations without requiring a new launch.
Mission operations must balance scientific opportunities against spacecraft safety.
Lessons from completed missions improve the design of future missions.
Space exploration is therefore an iterative process involving technology development and scientific discovery.

KEY TERMS
Space mission means an organized operation involving activities in space.
Launch vehicle means a rocket used to place a payload into space.
Payload means the part of a spacecraft or launch system that performs the primary mission function.
Orbit means a gravitational trajectory around a celestial body.
Trajectory means the path followed by a spacecraft through space.
Orbital maneuver means a controlled change in spacecraft velocity or orbit.
Gravity assist means using a body's gravity and motion to alter spacecraft trajectory.
Flyby means a close passage past a target without entering a long-term orbit.
Orbiter means a spacecraft designed to operate around a target body.
Lander means a spacecraft designed to reach and operate on a planetary surface.
Rover means a mobile surface exploration vehicle.
Telemetry means engineering and health information transmitted from spacecraft.
Mission control means the ground facility responsible for monitoring and commanding a mission.
Ground station means a terrestrial facility used to communicate with spacecraft.
Deep-space mission means a mission operating far beyond Earth's immediate orbital environment.
