VENUS

1. OVERVIEW
   Venus is the second planet from the Sun.
   Venus is a terrestrial planet with a rocky composition.
   Venus is similar to Earth in size, mass, and overall structure.
   Venus has a solid surface beneath a dense atmosphere.
   Venus has the hottest planetary surface in the Solar System.
   Venus is hotter than Mercury despite being farther from the Sun.
   The high surface temperature is caused mainly by a powerful greenhouse effect.
   Venus has a thick atmosphere dominated by carbon dioxide.
   Clouds of sulfuric acid surround the planet.
   Venus has no natural moons.
   Venus rotates very slowly compared with Earth.
   Venus rotates in the opposite direction to most planets.
   Venus has one of the longest rotation periods among the planets.
   Venus takes about 225 Earth days to orbit the Sun.
   Venus takes about 243 Earth days to rotate once relative to distant stars.
   A Venusian solar day is about 117 Earth days long.
   Venus is an important example of how planetary climates can evolve differently.

2. FORMATION
   Venus formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago.
   Venus formed from material in the early Solar System.
   The planet developed through accretion of rocky and metallic material.
   Venus formed in the inner region of the Solar System.
   Its composition reflects the materials available near the young Sun.
   Venus developed a metallic core, rocky mantle, and rocky crust.
   The planet's present environment resulted from billions of years of geological and atmospheric evolution.
   The exact history of Venus's interior and surface remains under investigation.
   Comparing Venus with Earth helps scientists study different outcomes of terrestrial planet evolution.

3. ORBIT
   Venus follows an orbit around the Sun that is nearly circular.
   Venus has a smaller orbital eccentricity than many other planets.
   The average distance between Venus and the Sun is about 108 million kilometers.
   Venus completes one orbit in approximately 225 Earth days.
   Venus travels around the Sun at an average speed of about 35 kilometers per second.
   Venus receives more solar energy than Earth.
   Venus's orbit lies closer to the Sun than Earth's orbit.
   Venus is often visible from Earth because of its relatively close distance and bright cloud tops.
   Venus can appear as either a morning star or an evening star.
   Venus never appears very far from the Sun in Earth's sky.

4. ROTATION
   Venus rotates extremely slowly.
   One Venus rotation takes approximately 243 Earth days relative to distant stars.
   Venus rotates in a retrograde direction.
   Retrograde rotation means Venus spins opposite to the direction of its orbit.
   The cause of Venus's unusual rotation is not fully established.
   Large impacts and long-term tidal effects have been proposed as possible contributors.
   Because Venus rotates slowly, its day-night cycle is unusual.
   A Venusian solar day lasts approximately 117 Earth days.
   The slow rotation does not prevent strong atmospheric circulation.
   Venus's atmosphere rotates around the planet much faster than the solid surface.

5. ATMOSPHERE
   Venus has the densest atmosphere of the terrestrial planets.
   The atmosphere is composed mostly of carbon dioxide.
   Nitrogen is the second most abundant major atmospheric component.
   Venus's atmosphere is much thicker than Earth's atmosphere.
   The atmospheric pressure at the surface is roughly 92 times Earth's sea-level pressure.
   The thick atmosphere produces an extremely strong greenhouse effect.
   The atmosphere traps infrared radiation emitted by the surface.
   This trapped energy keeps the surface extremely hot.
   Venus has extensive cloud layers at high altitudes.
   The clouds contain sulfuric acid droplets.
   The atmosphere becomes denser toward the surface.
   Pressure and temperature increase rapidly with decreasing altitude.

6. GREENHOUSE EFFECT
   The greenhouse effect is the main reason Venus has such a high surface temperature.
   Sunlight enters Venus's atmosphere and warms the surface.
   The warm surface emits infrared radiation.
   Carbon dioxide absorbs and re-emits much of this infrared radiation.
   The dense atmosphere prevents efficient loss of heat to space.
   This process maintains temperatures far above those expected from sunlight alone.
   Venus demonstrates how atmospheric composition can strongly influence planetary climate.
   The greenhouse effect on Venus is much stronger than Earth's natural greenhouse effect.
   Studying Venus helps scientists understand planetary climate systems.

7. TEMPERATURE
   Venus has an average surface temperature of roughly 465 degrees Celsius.
   Surface temperatures remain extremely high across much of the planet.
   The thick atmosphere distributes heat around Venus.
   The slow rotation therefore does not produce the extreme day-night temperature contrast seen on Mercury.
   The surface is hot enough to melt some low-melting-point metals.
   Temperature and pressure increase substantially toward the surface.
   The high temperature makes surface operations difficult for spacecraft.
   Venus provides an example of an extreme high-pressure planetary environment.

8. CLOUDS
   Venus is covered by thick global cloud layers.
   The clouds are mainly composed of sulfuric acid droplets.
   Cloud layers occur high above the planet's surface.
   The upper clouds reflect a large fraction of incoming sunlight.
   Venus therefore appears extremely bright when viewed from Earth.
   The clouds obscure direct visible-light views of the surface.
   Ultraviolet observations can reveal patterns in the upper atmosphere.
   Atmospheric winds move cloud structures rapidly around the planet.
   The cloud system plays an important role in Venus's atmospheric circulation.

9. ATMOSPHERIC CIRCULATION
   Venus has powerful atmospheric circulation.
   The upper atmosphere can move around the planet much faster than the solid surface rotates.
   This rapid atmospheric rotation is called superrotation.
   Venusian winds can transport heat from the dayside toward the nightside.
   Atmospheric waves contribute to the circulation of the upper atmosphere.
   Solar heating drives many atmospheric processes.
   The atmosphere changes with altitude.
   Understanding Venusian circulation helps scientists study atmospheres on other planets.

10. SURFACE
    Venus has a rocky surface hidden beneath its atmosphere and clouds.
    The surface contains mountains, plains, valleys, ridges, and volcanic structures.
    Large areas of Venus consist of relatively smooth volcanic plains.
    Volcanism has played an important role in shaping the planet.
    Radar observations are used to map the surface because visible light cannot penetrate the clouds.
    The surface contains many volcanic features.
    Venus also has large elevated regions called highlands.
    The planet has fewer obvious impact craters than heavily cratered worlds such as Mercury.
    The surface may have undergone extensive geological resurfacing.
    The age and history of different surface regions remain active research topics.

11. VOLCANOES
    Volcanic features are widespread on Venus.
    Venus contains shield volcanoes and other volcanic structures.
    Shield volcanoes are broad volcanoes formed by relatively fluid lava flows.
    Large volcanic plains cover substantial parts of the planet.
    Some volcanic features are hundreds of kilometers across.
    Lava flows have modified parts of the Venusian surface.
    Volcanism may have influenced the atmosphere over geological time.
    Scientists continue to investigate whether volcanic activity occurs on Venus today.
    Recent spacecraft observations have provided evidence consistent with ongoing geological activity.
    Volcanism is important for understanding Venus's surface-atmosphere evolution.

12. TECTONICS
    Venus has many tectonic features.
    The surface contains fractures, faults, ridges, and deformation zones.
    Venus does not appear to have Earth-like global plate tectonics.
    The exact mechanism driving crustal deformation on Venus remains uncertain.
    Heat from the interior can contribute to geological deformation.
    Some regions show evidence of intense tectonic activity.
    Studying Venusian tectonics helps scientists compare different styles of planetary crustal evolution.
    The absence of Earth-like plate tectonics is an important difference between Venus and Earth.

13. INTERIOR
    Venus is expected to have a metallic core.
    A rocky mantle surrounds the core.
    A rocky crust forms the outer solid layer.
    The interior structure is broadly similar to that of Earth.
    The exact physical state of Venus's core is not completely known.
    Scientists use gravity measurements and geological observations to study Venus's interior.
    The planet's interior heat influences its geological activity.
    Understanding the interior helps explain volcanism and tectonic deformation.

14. MAGNETIC ENVIRONMENT
    Venus does not have an Earth-like internally generated global magnetic field.
    The planet interacts directly with the solar wind.
    Solar wind particles interact with Venus's upper atmosphere.
    This interaction produces an induced magnetosphere.
    The induced magnetic environment can protect some atmospheric particles temporarily.
    Charged particles can also contribute to atmospheric escape.
    Venus's interaction with the solar wind differs from Earth's interaction with the solar wind.
    Studying this environment helps scientists understand how planets lose atmospheric material.

15. WATER AND ATMOSPHERIC EVOLUTION
    Venus currently contains very little water compared with Earth.
    Evidence suggests Venus may have lost large amounts of water over its history.
    Ultraviolet radiation can break water molecules in the upper atmosphere.
    Hydrogen can escape more easily into space than heavier elements.
    Loss of hydrogen can contribute to long-term water depletion.
    The history of water on Venus is still being investigated.
    The planet's atmospheric evolution may have contributed to its present dry environment.
    Comparing Venus with Earth helps scientists study how planetary climates diverge.

16. EXPLORATION
    Venus has been visited by numerous robotic spacecraft.
    The Soviet Venera program achieved several major milestones at Venus.
    Venera spacecraft obtained some of the first direct measurements from the Venusian surface.
    Venera landers returned photographs and scientific measurements from the surface.
    The Magellan spacecraft mapped most of Venus using radar.
    Radar mapping revealed detailed information about Venusian geology.
    Akatsuki is a Japanese spacecraft designed to study Venus's atmosphere.
    Akatsuki observations have contributed to the study of Venusian clouds and atmospheric circulation.
    Future missions are planned to investigate Venus's atmosphere, surface, and geological history.
    Venus remains an important target for planetary exploration.

17. SCIENTIFIC IMPORTANCE
    Venus is often described as Earth's planetary neighbor and a geological comparison case.
    Its similar size to Earth makes the difference in climate especially significant.
    Venus demonstrates the effects of an extreme greenhouse atmosphere.
    Its atmosphere provides a natural laboratory for studying dense planetary atmospheres.
    Its volcanic surface provides evidence of long-term geological activity.
    Its unusual rotation provides an example of planetary spin evolution.
    Its interaction with the solar wind helps scientists study atmospheric escape.
    Understanding Venus can improve models of rocky planets around other stars.
    Venus is especially useful for comparative planetology.

18. KEY TERMS
    Venus: The second planet from the Sun and the hottest planetary surface in the Solar System.
    Terrestrial planet: A rocky planet with a solid surface.
    Retrograde rotation: Rotation opposite to the usual direction of planetary orbital motion.
    Greenhouse effect: Warming caused when an atmosphere absorbs and re-emits outgoing infrared radiation.
    Superrotation: Atmospheric circulation that moves much faster than the underlying planet rotates.
    Carbon dioxide: The dominant gas in Venus's atmosphere.
    Sulfuric acid: A corrosive chemical present in Venus's upper cloud droplets.
    Radar mapping: A method used to study Venus's surface through its thick clouds.
    Volcanism: Geological activity involving the movement or eruption of molten rock.
    Atmospheric escape: The loss of atmospheric particles into space.
    Induced magnetosphere: A magnetic environment created through interaction between the solar wind and a planet's atmosphere.
