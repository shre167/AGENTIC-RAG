SPACE OBSERVATION
Space observation is the study of astronomical objects and phenomena using instruments that collect electromagnetic radiation or other signals.
Astronomers observe planets, moons, stars, galaxies, nebulae, and distant cosmic events.
Observations provide evidence about the physical properties, motion, composition, and evolution of astronomical objects.
Space observation can be performed from Earth or from instruments placed above Earth's atmosphere.
Ground-based observatories use telescopes located on Earth.
Space observatories operate above most or all of Earth's atmosphere.
Different wavelengths reveal different physical processes in astronomical objects.
The electromagnetic spectrum includes radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, and gamma-ray radiation.
Astronomical instruments are designed to detect specific wavelength ranges.
Observations can be combined across multiple wavelengths to obtain a more complete picture of an object.

TELESCOPES
A telescope collects electromagnetic radiation from distant objects and forms measurable signals or images.
The primary function of a telescope is to collect and focus incoming radiation.
A telescope's aperture is the effective diameter of its main light-collecting element.
Larger apertures generally allow telescopes to collect more light.
Light-gathering power determines how faint an object can potentially be detected.
Angular resolution describes the ability to distinguish two closely separated sources.
Larger apertures can provide better angular resolution under suitable observing conditions.
Magnification enlarges the apparent angular size of an observed object.
Magnification alone does not determine the quality of an astronomical observation.
Optical telescopes detect visible light.
Radio telescopes detect radio-frequency radiation.
Infrared telescopes detect infrared radiation emitted or reflected by astronomical sources.
Ultraviolet telescopes observe radiation at wavelengths shorter than visible light.
X-ray telescopes detect high-energy radiation from energetic astronomical environments.
Gamma-ray telescopes detect some of the highest-energy electromagnetic radiation.

REFRACTING AND REFLECTING TELESCOPES
A refracting telescope uses lenses to collect and focus light.
A reflecting telescope uses mirrors to collect and focus light.
Large modern optical telescopes commonly use mirrors rather than very large objective lenses.
Mirrors can be supported from behind, making large apertures more practical.
A telescope's optical system directs incoming radiation toward a detector or observing instrument.
Secondary mirrors can redirect light toward instruments positioned away from the primary mirror.
Different telescope designs provide different fields of view and optical characteristics.
Telescope instruments can include cameras, spectrographs, and photometers.

DETECTORS AND CAMERAS
A detector converts incoming radiation into a measurable signal.
Digital astronomical cameras commonly use arrays of light-sensitive detector elements.
Detector sensitivity affects the ability to measure faint astronomical sources.
Exposure time determines how long a detector collects incoming radiation.
Longer exposures can reveal fainter sources but can also increase unwanted signal and noise.
Astronomical images may require calibration before scientific analysis.
Calibration can account for detector response, background signal, and instrumental effects.
Image processing can improve measurement quality without creating information that was not detected.
Astronomical imaging records spatial information about observed sources.
Time-series imaging records how an object's brightness or appearance changes over time.

PHOTOMETRY
Photometry is the measurement of the brightness or flux of astronomical objects.
Apparent brightness describes how bright an object appears from an observer's location.
Intrinsic luminosity describes the total energy emitted by an object per unit time.
An object's apparent brightness depends on its intrinsic luminosity and distance.
Photometric measurements can reveal changes in stellar or planetary brightness.
Variable stars change in brightness over time.
Transit photometry can detect a temporary decrease in a star's brightness when a planet crosses its disk.
Light curves show how measured brightness changes with time.
Light curves can reveal periodic events and changes in astronomical systems.
Photometry can be performed in different wavelength bands.

SPECTROSCOPY
Spectroscopy studies the distribution of radiation across wavelengths.
A spectrum can contain absorption lines, emission lines, or a continuous distribution.
Spectral lines arise from interactions between radiation and atoms, ions, or molecules.
Different elements and molecules produce characteristic spectral features.
Spectroscopy can identify chemical constituents in astronomical objects.
Spectral measurements can provide information about temperature and physical conditions.
Doppler shifts in spectral lines can reveal motion along the observer's line of sight.
A redshift occurs when observed wavelengths are shifted toward longer wavelengths.
A blueshift occurs when observed wavelengths are shifted toward shorter wavelengths.
Spectroscopy is widely used to study stars, planets, galaxies, and interstellar material.

INFRARED OBSERVATION
Infrared radiation has longer wavelengths than visible light.
Infrared observations can reveal relatively cool objects and thermal emission.
Infrared wavelengths can penetrate some dust that blocks visible light.
Infrared astronomy is useful for studying star-forming regions surrounded by dust.
Cool stars, planets, and dust clouds can emit strongly at infrared wavelengths.
Thermal infrared observations can provide information about surface or atmospheric temperatures.
Infrared spectroscopy can identify molecules in planetary and astronomical environments.

RADIO ASTRONOMY
Radio telescopes detect radio waves from astronomical sources.
Radio observations can study gas, magnetic fields, pulsars, and other energetic phenomena.
Radio waves can pass through some clouds of interstellar dust that obscure visible light.
Radio interferometry combines signals from multiple antennas to improve angular resolution.
A radio interferometer measures correlated signals received by separate antennas.
Radio observations can reveal structures that may not be visible at optical wavelengths.
Radio astronomy is important for studying neutral hydrogen in galaxies.
Radio observations can also detect molecular emission from interstellar clouds.

SPACE-BASED OBSERVATION
Earth's atmosphere absorbs or distorts portions of incoming electromagnetic radiation.
Space telescopes avoid many atmospheric absorption effects.
Space-based instruments can observe wavelengths that are difficult or impossible to observe from the ground.
Space telescopes require specialized systems for power, thermal control, communications, and pointing.
Space observatories can operate for years depending on their design and mission conditions.
Some space telescopes observe primarily in visible and infrared wavelengths.
Others are designed for ultraviolet, X-ray, or gamma-ray astronomy.

EARTH'S ATMOSPHERE AND ASTRONOMICAL OBSERVATION
Atmospheric turbulence can blur images from ground-based optical telescopes.
Atmospheric water vapor strongly affects some infrared observations.
Adaptive optics can compensate for certain effects of atmospheric turbulence.
Adaptive optics uses rapid adjustments to optical components to reduce image distortion.
Atmospheric absorption creates wavelength ranges that are difficult to observe from the ground.
Radio observations can also be affected by human-made electromagnetic interference.
Observatories are often placed at locations with favorable atmospheric and environmental conditions.
High-altitude sites can reduce some atmospheric effects.
Dark-sky locations reduce interference from artificial light.

REMOTE SENSING
Remote sensing studies objects without physically contacting them.
Most astronomical observations are forms of remote sensing.
Planetary remote sensing can measure reflected sunlight and emitted thermal radiation.
Spectral measurements can identify minerals, gases, and other materials remotely.
Radar remote sensing can investigate surfaces and some subsurface structures.
Remote sensing allows scientists to study distant or inaccessible environments.
Spacecraft can carry remote-sensing instruments around planets and moons.
Orbital observations provide repeated coverage of planetary surfaces.

PLANETARY OBSERVATION
Planetary telescopes can measure brightness, color, atmospheric features, and planetary motion.
Spacecraft cameras can provide high-resolution images of planetary surfaces.
Spectrometers can determine the composition of planetary surfaces and atmospheres.
Radar can observe surfaces hidden by clouds or darkness in some environments.
Thermal instruments measure emitted infrared radiation related to temperature.
Altimeters measure surface elevation or distance from a spacecraft to a surface.
Repeated observations can reveal seasonal or geological changes.
Planetary observations can be combined with laboratory measurements of returned samples.

STELLAR OBSERVATION
Stars can be studied through their brightness, spectra, temperature, and motion.
Stellar spectra provide information about chemical composition and physical conditions.
The color of a star provides clues about its surface temperature.
Stellar brightness measurements can reveal variability and periodic changes.
Proper motion describes a star's apparent movement across the sky.
Radial velocity describes motion toward or away from an observer.
Parallax measures the apparent positional shift of a nearby star caused by Earth's orbital motion.
Stellar distances can be estimated from measured parallax for sufficiently nearby stars.
Combining distance and brightness measurements helps determine stellar properties.

GALAXY OBSERVATION
Galaxies can be observed across multiple electromagnetic wavelengths.
Optical observations reveal stars and luminous structures in galaxies.
Infrared observations can reveal cool dust and regions of star formation.
Radio observations can trace neutral hydrogen and molecular gas.
X-ray observations can reveal hot gas and energetic sources.
Galaxy spectra can provide information about composition and motion.
Redshift measurements are used to study the motion and large-scale distribution of galaxies.
Galaxy morphology describes the visible structure and shape of a galaxy.
Repeated observations can reveal transient events such as supernovae.

TIME-DOMAIN ASTRONOMY
Time-domain astronomy studies objects and phenomena that change with time.
Variable stars are important targets for time-domain observations.
Supernovae produce rapid and substantial changes in brightness.
Transiting exoplanets produce periodic changes in stellar brightness.
Pulsars produce highly regular pulses of electromagnetic radiation.
Astronomers compare observations taken at different times to identify changes.
Automated surveys can monitor large regions of the sky repeatedly.
Alerts can be generated when transient or variable objects are detected.

ASTROMETRY
Astrometry measures the positions and motions of astronomical objects.
Precise positions can be used to calculate proper motion.
Parallax provides a geometric method for estimating distances to nearby stars.
Astrometric measurements contribute to accurate astronomical catalogs.
Small positional changes can reveal the gravitational influence of unseen companions.
Astrometry can complement photometric and spectroscopic observations.

OBSERVATIONAL DATA
Astronomical data can include images, spectra, brightness measurements, positions, and time-series signals.
Raw observations often contain instrumental noise and background signals.
Calibration converts raw detector measurements into scientifically useful data.
Data reduction removes or corrects known instrumental effects.
Signal-to-noise ratio compares useful astronomical signal with unwanted noise.
Higher signal-to-noise measurements generally allow more reliable scientific analysis.
Astronomers use statistical methods to estimate uncertainties in measurements.
Independent observations can help confirm an astronomical result.
Observational data are often stored in standardized scientific formats for analysis.

OBSERVATIONAL SCIENCE
Astronomical observations are interpreted using physical models and measured data.
A scientific observation should be distinguishable from an assumption or theoretical prediction.
Multiple independent observations can strengthen confidence in a scientific conclusion.
Different instruments can provide complementary information about the same object.
Multi-wavelength observations can connect different physical processes occurring in one system.
Observational astronomy supports research into planetary systems, stellar evolution, galaxies, and cosmology.
Advances in detectors, telescopes, and data processing continue to increase the sensitivity of astronomical observations.
Modern astronomy increasingly combines observations from ground-based facilities, space observatories, and spacecraft.
