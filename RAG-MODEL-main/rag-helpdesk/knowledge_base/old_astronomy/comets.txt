COMETS — OVERVIEW
Comets are small Solar System bodies that contain significant amounts of ice.
They orbit the Sun and can develop visible activity when heated.
A comet's nucleus is its solid central body.
The nucleus contains a mixture of ice, dust, and rocky material.
Common cometary ices include water, carbon dioxide, and carbon monoxide.
When a comet approaches the Sun, some surface ices begin to sublimate.
Sublimation converts solid ice directly into gas.
Released gas carries dust away from the nucleus.
This produces a surrounding atmosphere called the coma.
A comet can develop one or more tails when sufficiently active.
Comet tails can extend millions of kilometers into space.

COMET STRUCTURE
A comet consists primarily of a nucleus and, when active, a coma.
The nucleus is the solid part of the comet.
The nucleus is generally irregular rather than perfectly spherical.
Its surface can contain dust, rocks, cliffs, pits, and fractures.
The coma forms from gas and dust released from the nucleus.
The coma can become much larger than the nucleus itself.
A comet's tails develop from material escaping from the coma.
The nucleus remains relatively compact compared with the surrounding coma.
Comet structure changes as the comet moves closer to or farther from the Sun.
Comets are therefore dynamic bodies rather than permanently unchanging objects.

NUCLEUS
The nucleus contains most of a comet's solid mass.
It is composed of frozen volatile compounds mixed with dust and rock.
Water ice is generally an important component of comet nuclei.
Carbon-bearing compounds are also present in many comets.
The surface can become covered by a dark layer of dust and organic material.
This surface can reduce how much sunlight penetrates the nucleus.
Jets can form where localized regions release gas and dust.
The activity of individual jets can change as the nucleus rotates.
Different regions of a nucleus can have different compositions.
Spacecraft observations have provided direct measurements of several comet nuclei.

COMA
The coma is a cloud of gas and dust surrounding an active nucleus.
It forms when solar heating causes volatile materials to escape.
The coma can expand to hundreds of thousands of kilometers.
Its size depends on the comet's activity and distance from the Sun.
The coma contains molecules released directly from the nucleus.
Solar radiation can break these molecules into smaller fragments.
The coma is not a solid structure.
Gas and dust continuously move through the coma.
The coma can become brighter as cometary activity increases.
Earth-based telescopes can observe the coma when a comet becomes sufficiently active.

DUST TAIL
A dust tail consists mainly of solid particles released from the nucleus.
Sunlight pushes dust particles away from the Sun through radiation pressure.
The dust tail generally curves along the comet's orbital path.
Larger dust particles are influenced more strongly by the comet's motion.
The appearance of a dust tail changes as the viewing geometry changes.
Dust tails can appear broad and curved in photographs.
They can contain particles ranging from very fine dust to larger grains.
The brightness of a dust tail depends on particle size and illumination.
Dust released during previous passages can remain along the comet's orbit.
This material can contribute to meteor showers when Earth crosses the debris stream.

ION TAIL
An ion tail is composed primarily of charged gas molecules.
Solar ultraviolet radiation and charged particles help ionize cometary gases.
The solar wind carries ions away from the comet.
The ion tail points approximately away from the Sun.
Unlike the dust tail, the ion tail is usually straighter.
Its direction can change with variations in the solar wind.
Ion tails can extend extremely long distances.
The tail can contain ionized molecules such as carbon monoxide and water products.
Magnetic fields carried by the solar wind influence ion-tail structure.
Ion tails demonstrate the interaction between cometary material and the solar environment.

COMET ACTIVITY
Cometary activity increases as a comet approaches the Sun.
Solar heating raises temperatures at the surface and below it.
Volatile ices can then sublimate into gas.
Escaping gas can drag dust particles away from the surface.
Jets and localized outbursts can produce irregular activity.
Activity usually decreases as the comet moves away from the Sun.
Not all comets become equally active.
Activity depends on composition, rotation, surface properties, and solar distance.
Some comets can experience sudden increases in brightness.
Repeated passages near the Sun can gradually alter a comet's surface.

ORBITAL CHARACTERISTICS
Comets generally travel around the Sun on elliptical orbits.
Some comet orbits are highly elongated.
Highly elliptical comets can spend most of their time far from the Sun.
Their activity becomes strongest during relatively close solar approaches.
Comet orbital periods can range from years to thousands of years.
Short-period comets generally return to the inner Solar System more frequently.
Long-period comets can take thousands or millions of years to return.
Planetary gravitational encounters can significantly change comet orbits.
Jupiter can strongly influence the trajectories of some comets.
Orbital calculations allow astronomers to predict future comet appearances.

SHORT-PERIOD COMETS
Short-period comets have orbital periods generally shorter than 200 years.
Many are associated with reservoirs of icy bodies beyond Neptune.
The Kuiper Belt is an important source region for many short-period comets.
The Jupiter-family comets are strongly influenced by Jupiter's gravity.
Their orbits can be changed through close encounters with giant planets.
Repeated solar approaches cause progressive loss of volatile material.
Some short-period comets eventually become less active.
Their surfaces can develop insulating layers of dust and organic material.
Halley's Comet is an example of a short-period comet.
Halley's Comet returns to the inner Solar System roughly every 76 years.

LONG-PERIOD COMETS
Long-period comets have orbital periods longer than about 200 years.
Many have extremely elongated orbits.
They can originate from distant reservoirs of icy bodies.
The Oort Cloud is considered a major source of long-period comets.
Passing stars and gravitational interactions can disturb distant cometary objects.
A disturbed object can enter an orbit that approaches the inner Solar System.
Long-period comets may be observed only once within a human lifetime.
Their distant origins make their orbital histories difficult to reconstruct.
Some long-period comets can approach the Sun from nearly any direction.
Their unpredictable appearances can make them important targets for surveys.

KUIPER BELT
The Kuiper Belt is a region beyond Neptune containing many icy bodies.
It extends roughly from Neptune's orbital region to several tens of astronomical units.
Many trans-Neptunian objects are found in the Kuiper Belt.
The region contains objects with different sizes and compositions.
Some Kuiper Belt objects can become comets.
Gravitational interactions can move icy bodies into comet-like orbits.
The Kuiper Belt preserves material from the outer Solar System.
Pluto is one of the best-known objects associated with this region.
Kuiper Belt objects help scientists study the early Solar System.
The region is an important source of short-period comet populations.

OORT CLOUD
The Oort Cloud is a proposed distant reservoir of icy bodies.
It is expected to surround the Solar System at enormous distances.
The Oort Cloud has not been directly imaged as a collection of individual objects.
Its existence is inferred from the orbits of long-period comets.
Gravitational disturbances can send Oort Cloud objects toward the Sun.
Passing stars may influence the orbits of distant objects.
The Galactic environment can also affect the outer comet reservoir.
The Oort Cloud is much farther away than the Kuiper Belt.
Its outer boundary is not known precisely.
It represents an important concept in models of Solar System formation.

COMET COMPOSITION
Comets contain water and other volatile ices.
Carbon dioxide and carbon monoxide are common volatile components.
Methane and ammonia can also occur in cometary material.
Dust contains silicate minerals and carbon-rich compounds.
Organic molecules have been detected in cometary material.
The exact composition differs between individual comets.
Spectroscopy allows astronomers to identify molecules in comet atmospheres.
Spacecraft can directly sample gas and dust released by a comet.
Laboratory analysis of returned comet samples can provide additional information.
Comet composition preserves clues about conditions in the early Solar System.

COMETS AND THE EARLY SOLAR SYSTEM
Comets formed from material present in the young Solar System.
Many remained in cold regions where volatile compounds could survive.
Their low-temperature histories can preserve primitive material.
Comets therefore act as natural records of early Solar System chemistry.
Their isotopic compositions can be compared with material found on Earth.
Scientists investigate whether comets contributed water or organic compounds to early Earth.
The exact contribution of comets to Earth's water remains an active research question.
Comet samples can reveal chemical compounds that formed before the planets.
Studying comets helps distinguish between primitive and processed Solar System material.
Comets connect planetary science with astronomy and organic chemistry.

METEOR SHOWERS
Meteor showers can occur when Earth passes through debris left along a comet's orbit.
Small particles enter Earth's atmosphere at high speed.
Friction and atmospheric interactions heat the particles and surrounding gas.
The resulting streaks of light are called meteors.
A meteor shower appears to originate from a particular region of the sky.
This apparent origin is called the radiant.
The radiant is determined by the direction of the debris stream.
Different cometary debris streams produce different meteor showers.
The Perseid meteor shower is associated with Comet Swift-Tuttle.
The Orionid meteor shower is associated with debris from Halley's Comet.

SPACECRAFT EXPLORATION
Spacecraft have visited several comets at close range.
ESA's Giotto spacecraft made a close encounter with Halley's Comet in 1986.
NASA's Stardust mission collected particles from the coma of Comet Wild 2.
The Stardust sample capsule returned cometary material to Earth.
NASA's Deep Impact mission released an impactor into Comet Tempel 1.
The impact exposed material beneath the comet's surface.
ESA's Rosetta mission orbited Comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko.
Rosetta studied the comet over an extended period.
Its Philae lander performed the first controlled landing on a comet nucleus.
These missions greatly improved knowledge of comet structure and activity.

ROSETTA AND COMET 67P
Comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko was the target of ESA's Rosetta mission.
Rosetta entered orbit around the comet in 2014.
The mission observed the comet as it approached the Sun.
Its observations showed that cometary activity can vary strongly with solar distance.
The nucleus of 67P has a distinctive two-lobed shape.
The two lobes are connected by a narrower region.
Rosetta detected complex organic molecules in the comet's environment.
Philae landed on the comet in November 2014.
The mission continued studying 67P until September 2016.
Rosetta provided detailed evidence about comet surfaces, jets, dust, and gases.

COMET LIFECYCLE
A comet may remain inactive while far from the Sun.
Solar heating increases as the comet approaches the inner Solar System.
Volatile ices begin to sublimate at appropriate distances.
The coma and tails become more prominent during increased activity.
The comet reaches its strongest activity near its perihelion in many cases.
After perihelion, solar heating decreases as the comet moves away.
Activity gradually weakens and the coma may disappear.
Repeated passages can remove volatile material from near-surface regions.
A comet can eventually become depleted or dormant.
Comet evolution depends on composition, orbit, rotation, and surface structure.

KEY TERMS
Comet: An icy small Solar System body that can become active near the Sun.
Nucleus: The solid central body of a comet.
Coma: The cloud of gas and dust surrounding an active comet nucleus.
Sublimation: Direct conversion of a solid into a gas.
Dust tail: A tail made mainly of solid particles pushed by sunlight and shaped by orbital motion.
Ion tail: A tail of charged particles carried away by the solar wind.
Perihelion: The point in an orbit closest to the Sun.
Kuiper Belt: A distant region containing many icy bodies beyond Neptune.
Oort Cloud: A proposed distant reservoir of icy bodies surrounding the Solar System.
Meteor shower: A group of meteors produced when Earth crosses a debris stream.
