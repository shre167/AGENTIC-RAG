HUMAN SPACEFLIGHT
Human spaceflight is the exploration of space by people aboard spacecraft.
Human spaceflight requires systems that protect astronauts from the hazards of the space environment.
Astronauts operate in environments with microgravity, vacuum, radiation, and extreme temperatures.
Spacecraft used for human missions require life-support, navigation, communication, power, and propulsion systems.
Human spaceflight includes orbital missions, space-station operations, spacecraft testing, and exploration beyond low Earth orbit.
Crewed missions can perform scientific experiments that require direct human participation.
Astronauts can also operate instruments, maintain spacecraft, and respond to unexpected situations.

SPACE ENVIRONMENT
Space is an extremely low-pressure environment with no breathable atmosphere.
A spacecraft must maintain a controlled internal atmosphere for its crew.
Microgravity is the condition in which objects appear to experience very little gravitational acceleration.
Astronauts in orbit experience microgravity because they and their spacecraft are continuously falling around Earth.
Microgravity affects human muscles, bones, balance, fluid distribution, and cardiovascular function.
Long-duration missions require exercise and medical monitoring to reduce some effects of microgravity.
Space radiation includes energetic particles from the Sun and sources outside the Solar System.
Radiation exposure is an important consideration for long-duration human missions.
Spacecraft structures and specialized materials can provide some radiation shielding.
Extravehicular activity exposes astronauts directly to the external space environment.

LIFE-SUPPORT SYSTEMS
A life-support system maintains environmental conditions required for human survival inside a spacecraft.
Oxygen supply is necessary for crew respiration.
Carbon dioxide must be removed because it accumulates as humans breathe.
Temperature and humidity must be controlled for crew comfort and equipment operation.
Water is required for drinking, food preparation, hygiene, and other spacecraft operations.
Waste-management systems collect and process human waste.
Air circulation distributes gases and helps control temperature and humidity.
Life-support systems can recycle portions of air and water to reduce resupply requirements.
Redundancy is important because failure of a critical life-support component can threaten the crew.
Environmental sensors continuously monitor important spacecraft conditions.

SPACE SUITS
A spacesuit is a wearable spacecraft that provides protection during operations outside a pressurized cabin.
Spacesuits maintain pressure around an astronaut's body.
Spacesuits provide oxygen and remove carbon dioxide during extravehicular activity.
Thermal-control systems help protect astronauts from extreme temperatures.
Spacesuits protect astronauts from some forms of radiation and micrometeoroid hazards.
The suit must remain flexible enough to allow astronauts to perform tasks.
Gloves are designed to provide hand protection while allowing controlled movement.
Space suits can include communication systems for contact with crew members and mission control.
Tethers and other safety systems can help prevent astronauts from drifting away from a spacecraft.
Spacesuit design depends on the environment and type of mission.

CREWED SPACECRAFT
A crewed spacecraft is designed to transport and support humans during spaceflight.
Crew modules provide a pressurized environment for astronauts.
Crew spacecraft include systems for propulsion, navigation, communication, power, and thermal control.
A spacecraft may contain separate sections for crew living, equipment, propulsion, or cargo.
Crew seats and restraints protect astronauts during launch, landing, and other high-acceleration events.
Windows or external cameras can provide astronauts with views of the surrounding environment.
Crew spacecraft require emergency systems for situations such as launch or spacecraft malfunctions.
An abort system can help separate a crew capsule from a failing launch vehicle during certain emergencies.
Reentry systems protect spacecraft from intense heating during atmospheric descent.
Parachutes or other landing systems can reduce speed before reaching the surface.

ROCKETS AND HUMAN LAUNCHES
A launch vehicle provides the thrust required to place a spacecraft into space.
Rocket engines generate thrust by expelling high-speed exhaust.
Rockets carry both fuel and an oxidizer because there is no atmospheric oxygen in space.
Multistage rockets use separate stages that are discarded after their propellant is consumed.
Staging reduces the mass that later stages must accelerate.
The launch vehicle must provide enough velocity for the spacecraft to enter its intended trajectory.
Launch trajectories are planned to satisfy requirements for altitude, velocity, safety, and orbital geometry.
Crewed launches require additional safety systems compared with many uncrewed missions.
Launch escape systems can move a crew capsule away from a malfunctioning rocket.
Launch windows are selected based on mission trajectory, orbital mechanics, and operational constraints.

ORBITAL FLIGHT
An orbit is a curved trajectory produced by gravitational motion around a larger body.
A spacecraft in low Earth orbit travels at several kilometers per second relative to Earth.
Astronauts aboard an orbiting spacecraft experience continuous free fall.
Orbital altitude affects orbital speed, period, atmospheric drag, and radiation exposure.
Low Earth orbit is commonly used for crewed spacecraft and space stations.
Spacecraft can perform orbital maneuvers by changing their velocity.
Small changes in velocity can produce significant changes in an orbit.
Rendezvous is the process of bringing two spacecraft into nearby compatible trajectories.
Docking occurs when two spacecraft physically connect in space.
Precise navigation is required for safe rendezvous and docking.

SPACE STATIONS
A space station is a crewed spacecraft or group of spacecraft designed for extended operations in orbit.
Space stations provide laboratories, living quarters, storage, and spacecraft-control systems.
Crew members can conduct experiments over periods ranging from days to months.
Microgravity research can investigate physical, biological, chemical, and technological processes.
Space stations can test equipment and procedures for future exploration missions.
Regular resupply missions can deliver food, equipment, experiments, and replacement components.
Crew rotations allow astronauts to stay aboard a station for extended periods.
Space stations require continuous power, thermal control, communication, and environmental support.
Station maintenance includes inspections, repairs, equipment replacement, and external operations.
Long-duration station missions provide information about human adaptation to spaceflight.

EXTRAVEHICULAR ACTIVITY
An extravehicular activity is an operation performed by an astronaut outside a spacecraft or space station.
EVA is commonly called a spacewalk.
Astronauts use spacesuits during spacewalks outside a pressurized spacecraft.
Spacewalk tasks can include equipment installation, maintenance, inspection, and scientific operations.
Astronauts are connected to the spacecraft through safety systems during many EVAs.
Tools are designed to remain manageable while wearing pressurized gloves.
EVA procedures are rehearsed extensively before flight.
Mission control coordinates many aspects of an EVA with the crew.
Astronauts monitor suit pressure, oxygen, carbon dioxide, temperature, and other parameters.
EVA operations are limited by suit resources, crew workload, and environmental conditions.

HUMAN FACTORS
Human factors research studies how spacecraft systems interact with human physical and cognitive capabilities.
Crew interfaces must provide information clearly and allow reliable control of spacecraft systems.
Astronauts require training to respond to normal and emergency procedures.
Fatigue can affect attention, decision-making, and physical performance.
Sleep schedules are important during long-duration missions.
Crew members must manage work, exercise, rest, communication, and personal activities.
Isolation and confinement can influence crew psychology and team dynamics.
Crew selection and training consider technical skills, physical health, communication, and teamwork.
Simulation helps astronauts practice complex operations before flight.
Mission planners consider human workload when assigning tasks.

SPACE MEDICINE
Space medicine studies the effects of spaceflight on human health.
Microgravity causes body fluids to shift toward the upper body and head.
Reduced mechanical loading can contribute to loss of bone mineral density.
Reduced muscle loading can cause muscle atrophy during prolonged exposure to microgravity.
Exercise equipment is used aboard spacecraft to help maintain muscle and bone condition.
Balance and spatial orientation can be temporarily affected after entering or leaving microgravity.
Astronauts can experience changes in cardiovascular function during spaceflight.
Medical monitoring helps identify health changes during missions.
Nutrition provides energy and essential nutrients required for crew health and performance.
Medical procedures must account for limited space, equipment, and communication delays.

SPACE RADIATION
Radiation exposure is a major environmental challenge for human spaceflight.
Sources include solar particle events and galactic cosmic rays.
Solar particle events can produce high-energy charged particles during periods of solar activity.
Galactic cosmic rays originate from energetic processes outside the Solar System.
Radiation shielding can reduce exposure but cannot eliminate all radiation hazards.
Mission duration and trajectory affect cumulative radiation exposure.
Earth's magnetic field provides substantial protection for astronauts in low Earth orbit.
Beyond Earth's protective magnetic environment, radiation becomes a greater mission consideration.
Spacecraft and spacesuit designs can incorporate materials intended to reduce radiation exposure.
Radiation monitoring is used to measure the environment experienced by astronauts.

SPACE COMMUNICATION
Crewed spacecraft require communication systems for contact with ground stations and other spacecraft.
Communication links can transmit voice, video, commands, telemetry, and scientific data.
Telemetry consists of measurements transmitted from spacecraft systems to ground controllers.
Tracking systems determine spacecraft position and motion.
Ground stations receive spacecraft signals and transmit commands.
Communication delays increase with distance between spacecraft and Earth.
Long-distance missions require procedures that account for communication delays or interruptions.
Mission control centers monitor spacecraft systems and coordinate crew activities.
Communication redundancy improves reliability during critical operations.

CREW TRAINING
Astronaut training prepares crew members for normal operations and emergency situations.
Training can include spacecraft systems, robotics, navigation, science, medicine, and survival procedures.
Neutral-buoyancy facilities can simulate some aspects of underwater movement during EVA training.
Virtual and physical simulators reproduce spacecraft controls and mission scenarios.
Crew members practice emergency procedures repeatedly to develop reliable responses.
Training can include launch and landing simulations.
Astronauts may train in spacecraft mock-ups that reproduce the interior layout of their vehicles.
International missions can require crew members to train in multiple countries and facilities.
Team training helps astronauts coordinate tasks under stressful conditions.
Mission-specific training continues throughout preparation for launch.

HUMAN SPACEFLIGHT MISSIONS
Crewed missions can be designed for scientific research, technology demonstrations, exploration, or station operations.
Mission planning includes launch, orbital operations, crew activities, and return or disposal.
Mission duration strongly affects life-support, food, medical, and psychological requirements.
Short missions can focus on specific experiments or spacecraft testing.
Long-duration missions require extensive planning for maintenance and crew health.
Exploration missions may require greater autonomy because communication delays can limit real-time control.
Surface missions require additional systems for landing, mobility, communication, power, and life support.
Future crewed missions may involve extended operations beyond low Earth orbit.
Human missions can work alongside robotic spacecraft to investigate environments before crew arrival.
Robotic systems can perform hazardous or repetitive tasks to support human explorers.

HUMAN SPACEFLIGHT AND ROBOTICS
Robotic spacecraft can perform reconnaissance before human missions.
Robots can operate in environments that are too hazardous for humans.
Human astronauts can make rapid decisions and adapt to unexpected physical situations.
Robotic systems can provide remote sensing and direct measurements.
Human and robotic exploration can complement each other.
Robotic precursor missions can identify landing sites and environmental hazards.
Crewed missions can collect samples or conduct complex experiments with direct human oversight.
Robotic assistants can transport equipment or perform repetitive operations.
Autonomous systems can reduce crew workload during long missions.
Human-robot collaboration is an important area of future space exploration.

SPACECRAFT SAFETY
Safety engineering identifies and reduces risks throughout a crewed mission.
Critical spacecraft systems commonly include redundant components.
Fault detection systems can identify abnormal spacecraft conditions.
Emergency procedures provide predefined responses to dangerous situations.
Crew escape systems can provide additional protection during launch emergencies.
Fire detection and suppression are important inside pressurized spacecraft.
Cabin pressure monitoring can detect possible leaks.
Power systems require protection against failures that could disable essential equipment.
Thermal-control failures can threaten both crew safety and spacecraft hardware.
Mission planners use simulations and risk analysis to prepare for possible failures.

FUTURE HUMAN EXPLORATION
Future human exploration may include longer missions beyond low Earth orbit.
The Moon provides a nearby environment for testing technologies needed for deeper-space missions.
Mars presents challenges involving distance, radiation, communication delay, landing, and surface operations.
Long-duration missions require efficient life-support and resource-recycling systems.
In-situ resource utilization uses locally available materials to support exploration.
Potential resources include water ice, atmospheric gases, and surface materials.
Reliable power systems are essential for sustained operations away from Earth.
Autonomous systems can support crews when communication with Earth is delayed.
Human exploration requires integration of transportation, habitats, life support, science, robotics, and safety systems.
Research from current missions contributes to technologies and procedures for future exploration.
