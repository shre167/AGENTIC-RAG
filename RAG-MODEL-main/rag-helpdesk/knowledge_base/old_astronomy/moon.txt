MOON

1. OVERVIEW
   The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite.
   The Moon is the fifth-largest natural satellite in the Solar System.
   The Moon is the largest natural satellite relative to the size of its host planet.
   The Moon is a rocky body with a solid surface.
   The Moon has no substantial atmosphere.
   The Moon has a very thin exosphere.
   The average distance between Earth and the Moon is about 384,400 kilometers.
   The Moon's diameter is about 3,475 kilometers.
   The Moon's mass is about 1.2 percent of Earth's mass.
   The Moon's surface is covered by craters, mountains, valleys, and plains.
   The Moon has water ice in permanently shadowed polar regions.
   The Moon's gravity is about one-sixth of Earth's surface gravity.
   The Moon has a major influence on Earth's ocean tides.
   The Moon also contributes to the long-term stability of Earth's axial orientation.
   The Moon is one of the most extensively studied objects beyond Earth.

2. FORMATION
   The leading explanation for the Moon's origin is the giant-impact hypothesis.
   According to this hypothesis, a large body collided with the early Earth.
   The impact produced debris that entered orbit around Earth.
   The debris eventually accumulated to form the Moon.
   The Moon formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago.
   The exact details of the giant impact remain under scientific investigation.
   Early in its history, the Moon was much hotter than it is today.
   A global or extensive magma ocean may have existed during the Moon's early evolution.
   Cooling allowed minerals to crystallize and form the lunar crust.
   The Moon's formation is closely connected to the early history of Earth.

3. ORBIT
   The Moon orbits Earth in an elliptical orbit.
   The Moon's average orbital distance from Earth is approximately 384,400 kilometers.
   The Moon completes one orbit relative to the stars in about 27.3 days.
   This period is called the sidereal month.
   The Moon's orbit is inclined by about 5 degrees relative to Earth's orbital plane.
   The Moon's distance from Earth changes during its orbit.
   The closest point in the Moon's orbit is called perigee.
   The farthest point is called apogee.
   The Moon's orbital motion determines the timing of lunar phases.
   The Moon also orbits the Sun while orbiting Earth.
   The Earth-Moon system travels together around the Sun.

4. ROTATION AND TIDAL LOCKING
   The Moon rotates around its axis.
   The Moon takes approximately 27.3 days to complete one rotation relative to the stars.
   The Moon's rotation period matches its orbital period around Earth.
   This condition is called synchronous rotation.
   Synchronous rotation causes the same lunar hemisphere to generally face Earth.
   The hemisphere facing Earth is commonly called the near side.
   The opposite hemisphere is commonly called the far side.
   The far side is sometimes incorrectly called the dark side.
   The far side receives sunlight just as the near side does.
   Tidal forces from Earth caused the Moon's synchronous rotation over time.
   Small orbital effects allow Earth observers to see slightly more than half of the lunar surface over time.
   This apparent motion is called lunar libration.

5. LUNAR PHASES
   The Moon does not produce its own visible light.
   The Moon appears bright because it reflects sunlight.
   The lunar phase depends on the relative positions of the Sun, Earth, and Moon.
   A new Moon occurs when the Moon is approximately between Earth and the Sun.
   A full Moon occurs when Earth is approximately between the Sun and Moon.
   A first-quarter Moon occurs when about half of the visible lunar disk is illuminated.
   A third-quarter Moon also shows approximately half of the visible disk illuminated.
   Waxing describes the increase of the illuminated portion.
   Waning describes the decrease of the illuminated portion.
   The complete sequence of lunar phases takes about 29.5 days.
   This period is called a synodic month.
   The lunar phases are caused by changing illumination geometry, not Earth's shadow.

6. SURFACE
   The Moon has a solid rocky surface.
   The surface is covered by a layer of loose material called regolith.
   Regolith consists of fragmented rock, mineral grains, and impact debris.
   The Moon has many impact craters.
   The largest craters were produced by collisions with asteroids and other bodies.
   The Moon's surface also contains mountains, ridges, valleys, and volcanic plains.
   Large parts of the surface preserve evidence of ancient impacts.
   The lack of substantial atmospheric weathering helps preserve surface features.
   The Moon does not have liquid oceans or flowing rivers on its present surface.
   Surface temperatures vary strongly between illuminated and dark regions.
   Lunar geology provides a record of events in the early Solar System.

7. HIGHLANDS AND MARIA
   The lunar highlands are generally bright and heavily cratered.
   The highlands form much of the Moon's ancient crust.
   Many highland regions are older than the dark lunar maria.
   Maria are broad, relatively dark plains on the Moon.
   The word maria means seas, although the maria are not seas of water.
   Most maria formed from ancient volcanic lava flows.
   Large impacts created basins that later filled with basaltic lava.
   The basaltic plains appear darker than the surrounding highlands.
   Maria are concentrated more heavily on the near side.
   The difference between the near side and far side is called lunar hemispheric asymmetry.

8. IMPACT CRATERS
   Impact craters are among the most common lunar surface features.
   Craters form when asteroids, meteoroids, or other bodies strike the surface.
   Small impacts can produce small bowl-shaped craters.
   Large impacts can create complex craters with central peaks.
   Very large impacts can produce enormous impact basins.
   The Moon has no thick atmosphere to burn up most incoming small objects.
   Impact ejecta can be distributed around a crater.
   Some craters overlap older craters.
   Crater superposition can help scientists determine relative surface ages.
   The density of craters is useful for studying the geological history of lunar regions.
   Impact cratering has been an important process throughout lunar history.

9. INTERNAL STRUCTURE
   The Moon has a crust, mantle, and core.
   The lunar crust is the outermost major solid layer.
   The mantle lies beneath the crust.
   A small metallic core exists at the center of the Moon.
   The Moon's core is much smaller relative to the Moon than Earth's core is relative to Earth.
   The Moon's interior contains both solid and partially molten regions.
   Seismic measurements from lunar missions have provided information about the Moon's interior.
   The Moon has experienced internal cooling throughout its history.
   The Moon's geological activity has decreased as its interior lost heat.
   Studying the lunar interior helps scientists understand the evolution of rocky planetary bodies.

10. LUNAR MAGNETIC ENVIRONMENT
    The Moon does not currently possess a strong global magnetic field like Earth.
    Some lunar rocks preserve evidence of ancient magnetization.
    This suggests the Moon may have generated a stronger magnetic field in its early history.
    The Moon interacts directly with the solar wind.
    The solar wind consists of charged particles released by the Sun.
    Solar wind particles can interact with the lunar surface.
    The Moon's lack of a thick atmosphere provides little protection from space radiation.
    The lunar magnetic environment varies across different regions.
    Studying lunar magnetism helps scientists investigate the Moon's early interior.

11. WATER ICE
    Water exists on the Moon in very small amounts compared with Earth.
    Scientists have detected evidence of water molecules and hydroxyl on the lunar surface.
    Water ice is concentrated in some permanently shadowed polar regions.
    These regions receive little or no direct sunlight.
    Their extremely low temperatures allow volatile materials to remain preserved.
    Water may have been delivered by comets, asteroids, or solar-wind-related chemical processes.
    Lunar water is scientifically important for understanding the Moon's history.
    Water deposits could also become useful resources for future lunar exploration.
    The amount, distribution, and accessibility of lunar water remain active research topics.

12. TEMPERATURE
    The Moon experiences large surface temperature variations.
    The absence of a thick atmosphere contributes to extreme temperature changes.
    Sunlit lunar surfaces can become extremely hot.
    Unilluminated surfaces can become extremely cold.
    Temperatures depend strongly on location, illumination, and local terrain.
    Permanently shadowed craters near the poles can remain extremely cold.
    Lunar temperature measurements are important for mission planning.
    Temperature conditions affect spacecraft systems and surface equipment.
    The extreme thermal environment is one of the challenges of lunar exploration.

13. GRAVITY
    The Moon's surface gravity is approximately one-sixth that of Earth.
    A person on the Moon would weigh much less than on Earth.
    The lower gravity affects how objects move and how astronauts walk.
    Lunar gravity is strong enough to retain some surface material.
    The Moon cannot retain a thick atmosphere under present conditions.
    Lunar gravity affects the trajectories of spacecraft around the Moon.
    The Moon's gravity also contributes to the motion of the Earth-Moon system.
    The difference between lunar and terrestrial gravity is important for spacecraft operations.

14. TIDES
    The Moon is the main contributor to Earth's ocean tides.
    The Moon's gravitational pull creates tidal bulges in Earth's oceans.
    Earth's rotation causes these tidal effects to move relative to locations on Earth.
    The Sun also contributes to Earth's tides.
    When the Sun, Earth, and Moon are aligned, stronger tides can occur.
    These stronger tides are called spring tides.
    When the Sun and Moon are at approximately right angles relative to Earth, weaker tides occur.
    These weaker tides are called neap tides.
    Tidal interactions transfer energy between Earth's rotation and the Moon's orbit.
    The Moon is slowly moving away from Earth because of tidal evolution.

15. ECLIPSES
    A solar eclipse occurs when the Moon passes between Earth and the Sun.
    During a solar eclipse, the Moon can temporarily block the Sun from view.
    A total solar eclipse occurs when the Sun is completely covered from a location in the Moon's shadow.
    A partial solar eclipse occurs when only part of the Sun is covered.
    A lunar eclipse occurs when Earth passes between the Sun and Moon.
    During a lunar eclipse, the Moon enters Earth's shadow.
    Not every new Moon produces a solar eclipse.
    Not every full Moon produces a lunar eclipse.
    The Moon's orbital inclination prevents eclipses from occurring every month.
    Eclipses provide useful opportunities for scientific observations.

16. EXPLORATION
    The Moon was the first extraterrestrial body visited by humans.
    The Soviet Luna program conducted early robotic lunar missions.
    Luna 2 became the first human-made object to reach the lunar surface.
    Luna 9 achieved the first successful soft landing on the Moon.
    The Apollo program was a NASA human lunar exploration program.
    Apollo 11 achieved the first human landing on the Moon in 1969.
    Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin became the first humans to walk on the lunar surface.
    Michael Collins remained in lunar orbit during the Apollo 11 surface mission.
    Several Apollo missions returned lunar rock and soil samples to Earth.
    Lunar samples have provided information about the Moon's composition and geological history.
    Modern missions continue to map and study the Moon using orbiters, landers, and rovers.

17. SCIENTIFIC IMPORTANCE
    The Moon preserves evidence from the early Solar System.
    Its heavily cratered surface provides information about ancient impacts.
    Lunar samples allow scientists to study extraterrestrial rocks directly.
    The Moon is a natural laboratory for studying airless rocky bodies.
    Its geological relationship with Earth provides information about planetary formation.
    Lunar water deposits are important for understanding volatile materials in the inner Solar System.
    The Moon is an important target for future robotic and human exploration.
    Lunar observations help scientists prepare for exploration of other worlds.
    The Earth-Moon system provides an important example of planetary-satellite evolution.

18. KEY TERMS
    Moon: Earth's natural satellite.
    Natural satellite: A naturally occurring body that orbits a planet or another larger body.
    Near side: The lunar hemisphere that generally faces Earth.
    Far side: The lunar hemisphere that generally faces away from Earth.
    Synchronous rotation: Rotation in which an object's rotation period matches its orbital period.
    Libration: Apparent oscillation that allows observers to see slightly more than half of the lunar surface over time.
    Maria: Large dark basaltic plains on the Moon.
    Regolith: Loose fragmented material covering the lunar surface.
    Perigee: The point in the Moon's orbit closest to Earth.
    Apogee: The point in the Moon's orbit farthest from Earth.
    Sidereal month: The Moon's orbital period measured relative to distant stars.
    Synodic month: The approximately 29.5-day cycle of lunar phases.
    Solar eclipse: An event in which the Moon passes between Earth and the Sun.
    Lunar eclipse: An event in which the Moon passes through Earth's shadow.
    Tidal locking: A condition in which an object's rotation becomes synchronized with its orbital period.
