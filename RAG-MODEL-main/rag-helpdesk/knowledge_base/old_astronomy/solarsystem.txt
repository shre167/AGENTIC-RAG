SOLAR SYSTEM

1. OVERVIEW

The Solar System is the planetary system centered on the Sun.
The Sun is the central star of the Solar System.
The Solar System formed approximately 4.6 billion years ago.
The Solar System is located in the Milky Way galaxy.
The Solar System lies in one of the spiral arms of the Milky Way.
The planets orbit the Sun because of gravitational attraction.
The Solar System contains eight recognized planets.
The eight planets are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune.
Mercury is the closest planet to the Sun.
Neptune is the farthest recognized planet from the Sun.
The planets are divided into terrestrial and giant planets.
Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars are terrestrial planets.
Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants.
Uranus and Neptune are ice giants.
The Solar System also contains dwarf planets.
Asteroids are small rocky bodies found throughout the Solar System.
Comets are bodies composed mainly of ice, dust, and rock.
Meteoroids are relatively small objects traveling through space.
The Solar System contains numerous moons.
Moons are natural satellites that orbit planets or other bodies.
The Solar System also contains interplanetary dust and gas.

2. THE SUN

The Sun is a star at the center of the Solar System.
The Sun contains most of the mass of the Solar System.
The Sun is primarily composed of hydrogen and helium.
Hydrogen is the most abundant element in the Sun.
Helium is the second most abundant element in the Sun.
The Sun produces energy through nuclear fusion.
Nuclear fusion occurs mainly in the Sun's core.
Hydrogen nuclei combine to form helium during fusion.
The energy produced in the core eventually reaches the solar surface.
The visible surface of the Sun is called the photosphere.
Above the photosphere are the chromosphere and corona.
The corona is the outermost major layer of the solar atmosphere.
The Sun's magnetic field influences solar activity.
Sunspots are relatively dark regions associated with strong magnetic fields.
Solar flares are powerful bursts of energy from the Sun.
Coronal mass ejections can release large quantities of solar plasma.
Solar activity can influence conditions throughout the Solar System.
The Sun provides most of the energy received by Earth.

3. FORMATION OF THE SOLAR SYSTEM

The Solar System formed from a large cloud of gas and dust.
This cloud is commonly called the solar nebula.
Gravity caused material in the solar nebula to collapse.
As the cloud collapsed, it began rotating more rapidly.
Most of the material accumulated at the center of the collapsing cloud.
The central concentration eventually became the young Sun.
A rotating disk of material formed around the young Sun.
This disk is called the protoplanetary disk.
Dust particles in the disk collided and stuck together.
Small particles gradually formed larger bodies.
These early bodies are called planetesimals.
Planetesimals collided and merged during planetary formation.
Some planetesimals eventually became planetary embryos.
Planetary embryos continued growing through collisions and accretion.
The inner Solar System was relatively rich in rock and metal.
The outer Solar System contained greater quantities of volatile materials.
Temperature differences influenced the composition of forming planets.
The terrestrial planets formed mainly in the warmer inner region.
The giant planets formed farther from the Sun.
The formation of planets occurred over millions of years.

4. PLANETARY ORBITS

A planet follows an orbit around the Sun.
Planetary orbits are governed primarily by gravity.
Most planetary orbits are elliptical rather than perfectly circular.
An ellipse is an oval-shaped geometric curve.
The Sun is located at one focus of a planet's elliptical orbit.
A planet's distance from the Sun changes slightly during its orbit.
The closest orbital point to the Sun is called perihelion.
The farthest orbital point from the Sun is called aphelion.
Earth completes one orbit around the Sun in approximately one year.
Mercury has the shortest orbital period of the eight planets.
Neptune has the longest orbital period of the eight planets.
Planetary orbital periods generally increase with distance from the Sun.
Planets generally orbit the Sun in the same direction.
The orbital paths of the major planets are relatively close to one plane.
This broad plane is associated with the ecliptic.
The Solar System's organized orbital structure reflects its formation from a disk.
Gravitational interactions can modify the orbits of smaller bodies.
Comets can have much more elongated or inclined orbits than planets.

5. TERRESTRIAL PLANETS

Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars are terrestrial planets.
Terrestrial planets have predominantly rocky compositions.
They have solid surfaces.
Terrestrial planets are smaller and denser than the giant planets.
Mercury is the smallest recognized planet.
Earth is the largest of the four terrestrial planets.
Venus has a dense carbon dioxide atmosphere.
Earth has a nitrogen-rich atmosphere with abundant oxygen.
Mars has a thin atmosphere dominated by carbon dioxide.
Mercury has an extremely thin exosphere.
The terrestrial planets have metal-rich interiors.
Their structures generally include a core, mantle, and crust.
Impact craters are common on the surfaces of terrestrial planets.
Volcanic activity has shaped several terrestrial planets.
Tectonic processes have strongly shaped Earth's surface.
Mars contains evidence of extensive geological activity in its past.
Venus has widespread volcanic features.
Mercury has a heavily cratered surface.
Earth has extensive liquid water on its surface.

6. GIANT PLANETS

Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune are giant planets.
Giant planets are much larger than terrestrial planets.
Jupiter is the largest planet in the Solar System.
Saturn is the second-largest planet.
Uranus and Neptune are smaller than Jupiter and Saturn.
Jupiter and Saturn are classified as gas giants.
Uranus and Neptune are classified as ice giants.
Giant planets do not have solid surfaces like Earth.
Their atmospheres transition gradually into deeper layers.
Hydrogen and helium are abundant in the gas giants.
Uranus and Neptune contain larger proportions of heavier volatile compounds.
Giant planets possess extensive atmospheric systems.
Jupiter has a powerful magnetic field.
Saturn has a prominent ring system.
Uranus has a tilted rotation axis.
Neptune has extremely fast atmospheric winds.
All four giant planets have multiple moons.
All four giant planets have ring systems.
The giant planets formed in the outer regions of the Solar System.

7. DWARF PLANETS

A dwarf planet is a distinct category of Solar System object.
Dwarf planets orbit the Sun.
Dwarf planets have enough mass for gravity to make them approximately round.
Dwarf planets have not cleared their orbital neighborhoods.
Dwarf planets are different from moons because they orbit the Sun.
Pluto is the most famous dwarf planet.
Pluto is located in the Kuiper Belt region.
Ceres is a dwarf planet located in the asteroid belt.
Eris is a distant dwarf planet beyond Neptune.
Haumea is known for its unusual elongated shape and rapid rotation.
Makemake is another recognized dwarf planet.
Dwarf planets vary greatly in size and composition.
Many distant Solar System objects remain difficult to study.
The classification of distant objects depends on observations and orbital properties.

8. ASTEROID BELT

The asteroid belt is located primarily between Mars and Jupiter.
It contains numerous rocky and metallic objects.
The asteroid belt is not a solid ring of closely packed rocks.
Large distances separate most asteroids from one another.
Ceres is the largest object in the main asteroid belt.
Gravitational interactions with Jupiter influenced the asteroid belt.
Asteroids are remnants of material left over from planetary formation.
Some asteroids contain significant amounts of metals.
Other asteroids contain large quantities of rocky or carbon-rich material.
Asteroid surfaces can contain impact craters.
Asteroids can have irregular shapes.
Some asteroids have their own small moons.
Spacecraft have visited and studied several asteroids.
Asteroid samples have also been returned to Earth by robotic missions.
Asteroids provide information about the early Solar System.

9. KUIPER BELT

The Kuiper Belt is a distant region beyond Neptune.
It contains many small icy bodies.
Pluto is one of the best-known objects associated with the Kuiper Belt.
The Kuiper Belt contains remnants from the formation of the Solar System.
Objects in the Kuiper Belt are generally rich in frozen volatile materials.
The region extends far beyond the orbit of Neptune.
The Kuiper Belt is different from the main asteroid belt.
The main asteroid belt is primarily rocky and lies between Mars and Jupiter.
The Kuiper Belt is primarily populated by icy bodies.
Some Kuiper Belt objects have unusual orbital properties.
Short-period comets can originate from the Kuiper Belt region.
Studying Kuiper Belt objects helps scientists understand planetary formation.
New Horizons provided detailed observations of Pluto.
New Horizons also flew past the Kuiper Belt object Arrokoth.
Distant Solar System observations continue to reveal new objects.

10. OORT CLOUD

The Oort Cloud is a proposed distant reservoir of icy objects.
It is thought to surround the Solar System at enormous distances.
The Oort Cloud has not been directly imaged as a complete structure.
Its existence is inferred from the orbits of long-period comets.
Long-period comets can have extremely elongated orbits.
Some long-period comets take thousands or millions of years to orbit the Sun.
Gravitational interactions can disturb objects in the distant Oort Cloud.
A disturbed object may begin traveling toward the inner Solar System.
The Oort Cloud is much farther away than the Kuiper Belt.
It represents one of the outermost proposed structures of the Solar System.
The exact size and population of the Oort Cloud remain uncertain.

11. MOONS

Many planets in the Solar System have natural satellites.
Natural satellites are commonly called moons.
Earth has one natural moon.
Mars has two small moons named Phobos and Deimos.
Jupiter has numerous known moons.
Saturn has numerous known moons.
Uranus and Neptune also have multiple moons.
Some moons are nearly spherical because of their gravity.
Other small moons have irregular shapes.
Moons can have atmospheres or extremely thin exospheres.
Some moons contain subsurface oceans.
Europa is a moon of Jupiter with strong evidence for a subsurface ocean.
Enceladus is a moon of Saturn known for water-rich plumes.
Titan is Saturn's largest moon and has a dense atmosphere.
The Moon strongly influences Earth's tides.
Tidal forces can affect the rotation and orbital evolution of moons.
Studying moons can reveal information about planetary systems.

12. COMETS

Comets are small Solar System bodies containing ice, dust, and rocky material.
A comet's nucleus is its solid central body.
When a comet approaches the Sun, its ices can begin to vaporize.
The released gas and dust can form a coma.
Solar radiation and the solar wind influence the coma.
A comet can develop a tail when it approaches the Sun.
The ion tail generally points away from the Sun.
The dust tail can have a different appearance and orientation.
Comets can follow short-period or long-period orbits.
Some comets return to the inner Solar System relatively frequently.
Other comets may take extremely long periods to return.
Cometary material preserves clues about early Solar System conditions.
Robotic spacecraft have visited several comets.
The Rosetta mission studied comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko.
Comets are important targets for planetary science.

13. SMALL BODIES

The Solar System contains many small bodies besides planets and dwarf planets.
Meteoroids are relatively small pieces of rock or metal in space.
A meteoroid entering Earth's atmosphere can produce a meteor.
A meteor is the visible streak caused by atmospheric heating and excitation.
A meteorite is a meteoroid fragment that reaches the ground.
Small bodies can originate from asteroids or comets.
Collisions can break larger bodies into smaller fragments.
Some meteorites preserve material from the early Solar System.
Scientists analyze meteorites to study Solar System history.
Small bodies can also interact gravitationally with planets.
Near-Earth objects are objects whose orbits bring them relatively close to Earth.
Monitoring near-Earth objects helps scientists understand impact hazards.
Space agencies conduct surveys to identify potentially hazardous objects.

14. SOLAR SYSTEM EXPLORATION

Humans have explored the Solar System mainly through robotic spacecraft.
Spacecraft have visited every major planet.
Flyby missions pass near a target and collect observations.
Orbiters enter orbit around a target body.
Landers descend to the surface of a planetary body.
Rovers move across the surface and conduct scientific investigations.
Sample-return missions collect material and deliver it to Earth.
Voyager spacecraft explored the outer Solar System.
Pioneer spacecraft contributed early observations of the outer planets.
Mariner missions studied several inner planets.
The Galileo mission studied Jupiter and its moons.
The Cassini mission extensively studied Saturn and its system.
The New Horizons mission studied Pluto and the Kuiper Belt.
Mars has been explored by numerous orbiters, landers, and rovers.
Spacecraft observations have transformed understanding of planetary surfaces.
Remote sensing allows scientists to study objects without landing on them.

15. IMPORTANCE OF STUDYING THE SOLAR SYSTEM

Studying the Solar System helps scientists understand planetary formation.
The Solar System provides examples of different types of planets.
Comparing planets reveals how environments evolve under different conditions.
The study of moons provides insight into planetary geology.
Asteroids preserve material from the early stages of planetary formation.
Comets contain volatile materials that can reveal early Solar System chemistry.
The Sun provides an example of stellar activity and evolution.
Solar activity affects space weather near Earth.
Planetary exploration improves understanding of Earth's own environment.
Observations of other planets help scientists test models of atmospheric evolution.
The Solar System is also a natural laboratory for planetary science.
Space exploration contributes to advances in robotics and remote sensing.
Studying the Solar System helps identify environments that may support life.
Understanding planetary environments is important for future exploration.
The Solar System remains an active area of astronomical research.

16. KEY TERMS

Solar System: The system centered on the Sun and containing planets and smaller bodies.
Planet: A large body that orbits a star and meets the accepted planetary criteria.
Dwarf planet: A round Solar System body that orbits the Sun but has not cleared its orbital neighborhood.
Moon: A natural satellite orbiting a planet or another Solar System body.
Asteroid: A relatively small rocky or metallic Solar System body.
Comet: An icy Solar System body that can develop a coma and tail near the Sun.
Meteoroid: A relatively small natural object traveling through space.
Meteor: The visible atmospheric phenomenon produced by a meteoroid.
Meteorite: A meteoroid fragment that survives atmospheric passage and reaches the ground.
Orbit: The path followed by an object under gravitational influence.
Perihelion: The point in an orbit closest to the Sun.
Aphelion: The point in an orbit farthest from the Sun.
Gravity: The attractive interaction associated with mass.
Accretion: The gradual growth of a body through the accumulation of material.
Planetesimal: A relatively small body involved in the formation of planets.
Solar nebula: The cloud of gas and dust from which the Solar System formed.
Ecliptic: The apparent annual path of the Sun and the approximate plane of planetary orbits.
Kuiper Belt: A distant region containing many icy bodies beyond Neptune.
Oort Cloud: A proposed distant reservoir of icy bodies surrounding the Solar System.
Solar wind: A continuous flow of charged particles from the Sun.
Solar flare: A sudden release of energy associated with solar magnetic activity.
Coronal mass ejection: A large eruption of magnetized plasma from the Sun.
Photosphere: The visible layer of the Sun.
Planetary science: The scientific study of planets, moons, and other planetary bodies.
Astrobiology: The study of the origin, evolution, distribution, and potential for life in the universe.
