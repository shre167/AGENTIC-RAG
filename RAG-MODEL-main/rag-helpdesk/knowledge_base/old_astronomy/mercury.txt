MERCURY

1. OVERVIEW
   Mercury is the smallest of the eight recognized planets in the Solar System.
   Mercury is the planet closest to the Sun.
   Mercury is a terrestrial planet with a rocky composition.
   Mercury has a solid surface covered by numerous impact craters.
   Mercury has no substantial atmosphere like Earth.
   Mercury has a very thin exosphere.
   Mercury is smaller than Earth's Moon.
   Mercury has a diameter of approximately 4,879 kilometers.
   Mercury has a mass of approximately 5.5 percent of Earth's mass.
   Mercury's average distance from the Sun is approximately 58 million kilometers.
   Mercury receives much more solar radiation than Earth.
   Mercury has the shortest orbital period of any planet.
   Mercury completes one orbit around the Sun in about 88 Earth days.
   Mercury rotates slowly compared with its orbital motion.
   One solar day on Mercury lasts about 176 Earth days.
   Mercury has a strong metallic core relative to its overall size.
   Mercury's unusual internal structure makes it important for planetary science.

2. FORMATION
   Mercury formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago.
   Mercury formed from material in the early Solar System.
   The planet developed through accretion of rocky and metallic material.
   Mercury formed in the hot inner region of the Solar System.
   The high temperatures near the young Sun influenced the materials available during formation.
   Mercury contains a relatively large metallic core compared with its mantle and crust.
   Several explanations have been proposed for Mercury's unusually large core.
   Large impacts may have removed some of Mercury's original rocky material.
   The exact sequence of events that produced Mercury's present structure remains an area of research.
   Studying Mercury helps scientists understand the formation of planets close to their stars.

3. ORBIT
   Mercury follows an elliptical orbit around the Sun.
   Mercury's orbit is more eccentric than the orbits of the other major planets.
   Its distance from the Sun changes significantly during its orbit.
   Mercury reaches perihelion when it is closest to the Sun.
   Mercury reaches aphelion when it is farthest from the Sun.
   Mercury travels around the Sun at a high orbital speed.
   Its average orbital speed is about 47 kilometers per second.
   Mercury's short orbital period results from its close distance to the Sun.
   Mercury's orbit is also slightly inclined relative to Earth's orbital plane.
   The planet experiences strong gravitational effects from the Sun.
   Mercury's orbit has been studied precisely using spacecraft and astronomical observations.

4. ROTATION
   Mercury rotates around its axis relatively slowly.
   Mercury takes about 59 Earth days to complete one rotation.
   Mercury's rotational period is related to its orbital period.
   Mercury is in a 3:2 spin-orbit resonance with the Sun.
   Mercury rotates three times for every two orbits around the Sun.
   The 3:2 resonance means Mercury does not keep the same side permanently facing the Sun.
   A location on Mercury experiences unusual cycles of daylight and darkness.
   One complete solar day on Mercury lasts approximately 176 Earth days.
   Mercury's slow rotation contributes to large temperature differences between day and night.
   The relationship between Mercury's rotation and orbit is an important example of tidal evolution.

5. SURFACE
   Mercury has a rocky solid surface.
   Its surface resembles the heavily cratered regions of Earth's Moon.
   Impact craters cover large portions of Mercury.
   The surface also contains cliffs, plains, valleys, and other geological features.
   Large impacts have shaped Mercury throughout its history.
   Some regions of Mercury are relatively smooth plains.
   Volcanic activity in Mercury's early history contributed to the formation of smooth plains.
   The planet has experienced contraction as its interior cooled.
   This contraction produced large fault scarps across the surface.
   Mercury's surface records evidence of its geological history.
   The absence of a thick atmosphere allows small impact features to remain preserved.
   Mercury's surface is an important record of impacts in the inner Solar System.

6. CRATERS
   Impact craters are among Mercury's most common surface features.
   Craters formed when asteroids and other objects struck the planet.
   Some Mercury craters are extremely large.
   The largest impact structures can extend hundreds of kilometers across.
   Craters can contain central peaks formed during large impacts.
   Fresh craters can have bright deposits around their interiors.
   Older craters can become degraded through later impacts and geological processes.
   Mercury's heavily cratered terrain indicates extensive impact history.
   The Caloris Basin is one of Mercury's largest known impact features.
   The Caloris Basin formed after a major impact early in Mercury's history.
   The impact that formed the basin affected regions on the opposite side of Mercury.
   Mercury's crater record provides information about the history of impacts in the inner Solar System.

7. CALORIS BASIN
   The Caloris Basin is a giant impact basin on Mercury.
   It is approximately 1,550 kilometers across.
   The basin formed from a massive collision early in Mercury's history.
   The impact produced extensive geological disturbances.
   The interior of the basin contains relatively smooth volcanic plains.
   The basin is surrounded by terrain influenced by the original impact.
   The Caloris Basin is one of the most prominent features on Mercury.
   Studying the basin helps scientists understand the effects of large planetary impacts.

8. INTERNAL STRUCTURE
   Mercury has a large metallic core.
   The core occupies a substantial fraction of Mercury's interior.
   The core is surrounded by a rocky mantle.
   A rocky crust forms the outermost solid layer.
   Mercury's core contains a significant amount of iron.
   The large core contributes to Mercury's relatively high average density.
   Mercury has a global magnetic field.
   The magnetic field provides evidence that at least part of the core remains active.
   Scientists study Mercury's interior using spacecraft measurements and gravitational data.
   Understanding Mercury's core helps explain how small terrestrial planets evolve.

9. MAGNETIC FIELD
   Mercury has a global magnetic field.
   Its magnetic field is weaker than Earth's magnetic field.
   Mercury's magnetic field is approximately aligned with its rotation axis.
   The magnetic field interacts with the solar wind.
   The solar wind is a stream of charged particles from the Sun.
   Mercury's magnetic environment is strongly influenced by its proximity to the Sun.
   The planet has a small magnetosphere.
   Solar wind conditions around Mercury can change rapidly.
   Mercury's magnetic field is unusual for a small rocky planet.
   The existence of the field provides information about Mercury's interior.

10. EXOSPHERE
    Mercury does not have a thick atmosphere.
    Instead, Mercury has an extremely thin exosphere.
    The exosphere contains atoms released from the surface.
    Solar radiation can knock atoms from Mercury's surface.
    Micrometeorite impacts can also release material into the exosphere.
    The solar wind contributes to interactions between the surface and surrounding space environment.
    The exosphere contains elements including sodium, oxygen, hydrogen, helium, and potassium.
    Because the exosphere is extremely thin, it does not behave like Earth's atmosphere.
    Mercury's exosphere cannot retain heat effectively.
    The lack of a substantial atmosphere contributes to extreme surface temperature variations.

11. TEMPERATURE
    Mercury experiences extreme temperature differences.
    The dayside can become extremely hot because Mercury is close to the Sun.
    Surface temperatures on the sunlit side can reach roughly 430 degrees Celsius.
    The nightside can become extremely cold.
    Nighttime temperatures can fall to approximately minus 180 degrees Celsius.
    The thin exosphere provides little insulation.
    Mercury's slow rotation causes long periods of sunlight and darkness.
    Some permanently shadowed polar craters remain extremely cold.
    Temperature variations make Mercury useful for studying heat transfer on airless worlds.

12. WATER ICE
    Despite its proximity to the Sun, Mercury contains water ice in some polar craters.
    Some deep polar craters receive little or no direct sunlight.
    These permanently shadowed regions can remain extremely cold.
    Radar observations provided evidence for reflective deposits in polar craters.
    Spacecraft observations support the presence of water ice in permanently shadowed areas.
    The water may have been delivered by comets, asteroids, or other processes.
    Water ice can survive where temperatures remain low enough.
    Mercury's polar ice demonstrates that volatile materials can persist on airless bodies.
    Studying Mercury's polar deposits helps scientists understand volatile transport in the inner Solar System.

13. SOLAR ENVIRONMENT
    Mercury is exposed to intense solar radiation.
    The planet receives significantly more sunlight than Earth.
    The solar wind interacts directly with Mercury's magnetosphere.
    Solar activity can affect Mercury's surface environment.
    Mercury has no thick atmosphere to shield its surface from the space environment.
    The planet's close distance to the Sun makes it an important target for studying solar interactions.
    Spacecraft operating near Mercury must withstand strong solar heating.
    Mercury provides an environment for studying planetary magnetospheres close to stars.

14. EXPLORATION
    Mercury has been studied by several spacecraft.
    Mariner 10 was the first spacecraft to visit Mercury.
    Mariner 10 conducted multiple flybys of Mercury during the 1970s.
    The mission used a gravity-assist technique involving Venus.
    Mariner 10 photographed a significant portion of Mercury's surface.
    MESSENGER was a NASA spacecraft that studied Mercury extensively.
    MESSENGER entered orbit around Mercury in 2011.
    MESSENGER mapped Mercury's surface in much greater detail.
    The spacecraft studied Mercury's composition, magnetic field, exosphere, and interior.
    MESSENGER discovered evidence supporting the presence of water ice in polar regions.
    BepiColombo is a joint European-Japanese mission studying Mercury.
    BepiColombo consists of the Mercury Planetary Orbiter and Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter.
    The mission is designed to investigate Mercury's interior, surface, exosphere, and magnetosphere.

15. SCIENTIFIC IMPORTANCE
    Mercury provides an example of a small terrestrial planet.
    Its large metallic core makes it unusual among rocky planets.
    Its proximity to the Sun allows scientists to study a strongly irradiated planetary environment.
    Mercury's magnetic field provides information about planetary interiors.
    Its cratered surface records billions of years of impacts.
    Its polar ice demonstrates that volatile materials can survive near the Sun.
    Mercury helps scientists test models of terrestrial planet formation.
    Comparisons between Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars reveal differences in planetary evolution.
    Spacecraft observations continue to improve understanding of Mercury's geology.
    Mercury is an important target for comparative planetary science.

16. KEY TERMS
    Mercury: The smallest recognized planet and the closest planet to the Sun.
    Terrestrial planet: A rocky planet with a solid surface.
    Exosphere: An extremely thin outer atmospheric region.
    Caloris Basin: A very large impact basin on Mercury.
    Spin-orbit resonance: A relationship between an object's rotation and orbital periods.
    3:2 resonance: Mercury's rotation-orbit relationship in which three rotations occur for every two orbits.
    Perihelion: The point in Mercury's orbit closest to the Sun.
    Aphelion: The point in Mercury's orbit farthest from the Sun.
    Magnetosphere: The region around a planet influenced by its magnetic field.
    Solar wind: A continuous flow of charged particles from the Sun.
    Regolith: A layer of loose material covering a planetary surface.
    Impact crater: A depression formed by the collision of a space object with a planetary surface.
