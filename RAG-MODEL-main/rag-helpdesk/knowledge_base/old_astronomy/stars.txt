STARS — OVERVIEW
A star is a massive astronomical object made primarily of hot plasma.
Stars generate energy through processes occurring in their interiors.
Most stars produce energy through nuclear fusion.
Hydrogen fusion is the main energy source for stars during most of their lifetimes.
The Sun is the closest star to Earth.
Stars vary greatly in mass, size, temperature, luminosity, and age.
Their properties depend strongly on their mass and chemical composition.
Stars can exist individually or as members of binary and multiple-star systems.
Large groups of stars can form open clusters or globular clusters.
Stars are fundamental building blocks of galaxies.

STAR FORMATION
Stars form inside cold, dense regions of interstellar clouds.
These regions contain gas and microscopic dust grains.
Gravity causes sufficiently dense parts of a cloud to collapse.
As a collapsing region contracts, its density and temperature increase.
The collapsing material can form a protostar.
A protostar is a young stellar object that has not yet begun stable hydrogen fusion.
Material can continue falling onto the protostar from its surrounding disk.
Conservation of angular momentum causes the material to rotate faster as it contracts.
A circumstellar disk can form around the young star.
Planets can eventually form from material remaining in such disks.

MOLECULAR CLOUDS
Molecular clouds are cold regions containing large amounts of molecular gas.
Hydrogen molecules are an important component of these clouds.
Molecular clouds can contain dust, carbon monoxide, and other molecules.
Dust grains help shield molecules from energetic radiation.
Dense regions inside molecular clouds can collapse under gravity.
Turbulence can influence the structure of molecular clouds.
Magnetic fields can also affect the movement of gas.
Star formation commonly occurs in the densest parts of molecular clouds.
A single cloud can produce many stars.
Groups of newly formed stars can remain associated for some time.

PROTOSTARS
A protostar forms during the gravitational collapse of dense gas.
It gains mass as material falls toward it.
The gravitational energy of infalling material is converted into heat.
A protostar can be surrounded by an accretion disk.
Jets and outflows can remove some material from the system.
The protostellar stage ends when the object becomes a stable hydrogen-fusing star.
The duration of protostellar evolution depends strongly on stellar mass.
Massive stars evolve through early stages much faster than low-mass stars.
Protostars are often observed using infrared and radio wavelengths.
Dust can block visible light from many young stellar systems.

NUCLEAR FUSION
Nuclear fusion combines light atomic nuclei into heavier nuclei.
Fusion releases energy because of differences in nuclear binding energy.
In Sun-like stars, hydrogen nuclei ultimately combine to form helium.
The proton-proton chain is the dominant hydrogen-fusion process in the Sun.
Higher-mass stars mainly use the carbon-nitrogen-oxygen cycle for hydrogen fusion.
Fusion requires extremely high temperatures and pressures.
These conditions occur naturally in stellar cores.
Energy produced in the core moves outward through the star.
Fusion provides pressure that helps support a star against gravitational collapse.
When fuel changes, the structure and evolution of a star also change.

STELLAR CORE
The core is the central region where nuclear fusion occurs.
Core temperature depends strongly on stellar mass and evolutionary stage.
The Sun's core reaches temperatures of roughly 15 million degrees Celsius.
Pressure in stellar cores is extremely high.
Hydrogen is gradually converted into helium during main-sequence evolution.
The core becomes enriched in helium as hydrogen is consumed.
The changing chemical composition affects the star's internal structure.
Energy generated in the core eventually reaches the stellar surface.
Neutrinos produced during nuclear reactions can escape directly from the core.
Solar neutrino observations provide evidence about fusion inside the Sun.

HYDROSTATIC EQUILIBRIUM
A stable star can exist in hydrostatic equilibrium.
Gravity pulls stellar material inward.
Pressure generated by hot gas and radiation pushes outward.
The balance between these effects supports the star.
Changes in energy production can disturb this balance.
The star then adjusts its structure until a new equilibrium is established.
Hydrostatic equilibrium does not mean that material inside the star is stationary.
Gas and energy continue to move through the stellar interior.
The balance can change significantly during later stages of stellar evolution.
Mass is one of the most important factors controlling stellar equilibrium.

STELLAR MASS
Stellar mass strongly influences almost every major stellar property.
Massive stars have higher core temperatures and pressures.
They generally fuse fuel at much higher rates than low-mass stars.
High-mass stars are therefore more luminous.
Massive stars usually have shorter lifetimes.
Low-mass stars consume their fuel more slowly.
A low-mass star can remain on the main sequence for billions or even trillions of years.
Mass determines which nuclear-fusion stages a star can undergo.
It also strongly influences the star's final remnant.
Stellar mass is therefore central to understanding stellar evolution.

MAIN SEQUENCE
The main sequence is the longest major stage of a normal star's life.
During this stage, hydrogen is fused into helium in the core.
The Sun is currently a main-sequence star.
Main-sequence stars form a diagonal band on the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram.
Massive main-sequence stars are generally hotter and more luminous.
Low-mass main-sequence stars are cooler and less luminous.
A star's main-sequence lifetime depends strongly on its mass.
Hydrogen gradually becomes depleted in the core.
When core hydrogen becomes insufficient, the star begins evolving away from the main sequence.

STELLAR TEMPERATURE
Stellar temperature affects the color and spectrum of a star.
Hot stars generally appear blue or blue-white.
Intermediate-temperature stars can appear white or yellow.
Cooler stars generally appear orange or red.
The surface temperature of the Sun is about 5,500 degrees Celsius.
Astronomers can estimate temperature from stellar spectra.
Spectral lines provide information about temperature and chemical composition.
Temperature can vary significantly between a star's core and surface.
The term effective temperature refers to the temperature associated with the star's total emitted radiation.
Temperature is one of the main parameters used to classify stars.

STELLAR LUMINOSITY
Luminosity is the total amount of energy a star emits per unit time.
It is different from apparent brightness.
A star can appear bright because it is close to Earth.
A distant star can be intrinsically much more luminous but appear faint.
Luminosity depends on a star's radius and surface temperature.
A larger star can be very luminous even if its surface is relatively cool.
A small hot star can also have high luminosity.
Astronomers compare luminosities using solar luminosity as a reference.
Luminosity is an important parameter for placing stars on the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram.
Accurate distance measurements help determine stellar luminosity.

STELLAR SPECTRUM
A stellar spectrum is the distribution of a star's radiation across wavelengths.
Spectra can contain absorption or emission lines.
Absorption lines occur when atoms or molecules absorb specific wavelengths.
Spectral lines reveal information about chemical composition.
The strength and shape of spectral lines also provide information about temperature and pressure.
Doppler shifts can reveal stellar motion.
Rotational broadening can provide information about stellar rotation.
Spectroscopy is one of the most important tools in stellar astronomy.
Spectral observations can also reveal magnetic activity and atmospheric conditions.
A star's spectrum contains information about its physical environment.

SPECTRAL CLASSIFICATION
Stars are commonly classified using the spectral sequence O, B, A, F, G, K, M.
O-type stars are the hottest of these major classes.
M-type stars are the coolest of these major classes.
The Sun is classified as a G-type star.
Spectral class is related mainly to surface temperature.
Each major spectral class is divided into numerical subclasses.
A lower numerical subclass generally indicates a hotter star within the same class.
Spectral classification is based on characteristic absorption lines and overall spectral properties.
Modern stellar classification can include additional information beyond the basic OBAFGKM sequence.
Spectral classification helps astronomers compare large populations of stars.

HERTZSPRUNG-RUSSELL DIAGRAM
The Hertzsprung-Russell diagram compares stellar luminosity with surface temperature.
Temperature usually increases toward the left side of the diagram.
Luminosity generally increases upward.
Most stars lie along a diagonal region called the main sequence.
Red giants occupy a region above the main sequence.
White dwarfs are hot but relatively faint and occupy a lower-left region.
Supergiants occupy regions of very high luminosity.
The diagram is useful for studying stellar evolution.
A star's position changes as its temperature and luminosity change.
Clusters of stars can be studied using the diagram to estimate their ages.

RED GIANTS
A red giant forms when a low- or intermediate-mass star exhausts hydrogen in its core.
Hydrogen fusion can continue in a shell surrounding the helium-rich core.
The outer layers expand significantly.
The expanded surface becomes cooler.
The star therefore becomes larger and redder.
Its luminosity can increase substantially.
The core continues to contract and heat.
Eventually, helium fusion can begin in sufficiently massive cores.
The Sun is expected to become a red giant in the distant future.
Red giant evolution can strongly affect surrounding planetary systems.

HELIUM FUSION
Helium fusion occurs at temperatures much higher than those required for ordinary hydrogen fusion.
The triple-alpha process converts helium nuclei into carbon.
Some carbon can also capture helium to form oxygen.
Helium fusion occurs in the cores of evolved low- and intermediate-mass stars.
The onset of helium fusion changes the structure of the star.
In some stars, helium ignition can occur rapidly.
After core helium is depleted, further evolution depends strongly on stellar mass.
The products of helium fusion become important sources of carbon and oxygen.
These elements can later become part of stars, planets, and other astronomical bodies.
Helium fusion is an important stage of stellar nucleosynthesis.

MASSIVE STARS
Massive stars are substantially more massive than the Sun.
Their high core temperatures allow them to fuse heavier elements.
After hydrogen and helium, they can process carbon, neon, oxygen, and silicon.
Advanced fusion stages occur progressively faster.
The final stages of massive-star evolution can produce an iron-rich core.
Fusion beyond iron does not provide net energy in the same way as lighter-element fusion.
The core can therefore become unstable when it reaches advanced stages.
Massive stars can end their lives in powerful stellar explosions.
Their deaths distribute newly formed elements into surrounding space.
Massive stars strongly influence the chemical evolution of galaxies.

STELLAR NUCLEOSYNTHESIS
Stellar nucleosynthesis is the production of atomic nuclei in stars.
Hydrogen fusion produces helium.
Helium fusion produces substantial amounts of carbon and oxygen.
Massive stars can synthesize heavier elements through successive fusion stages.
Supernova explosions can produce and distribute additional elements.
Neutron-capture processes can create many elements heavier than iron.
Stellar winds can also return processed material to interstellar space.
The elements released by stars can become part of future generations of stars and planets.
The chemical composition of the universe changes through stellar nucleosynthesis.
Many elements found on Earth were produced in earlier generations of stars.

STELLAR WINDS
Stellar winds are streams of charged particles flowing away from stars.
The strength of stellar winds varies among different types of stars.
Massive hot stars can have powerful radiation-driven winds.
The Sun produces a continuous solar wind.
Stellar winds can remove mass from stars over time.
They can also interact with surrounding interstellar gas.
Strong winds from massive stars can influence nearby star-forming regions.
Stellar winds contribute to the chemical enrichment of interstellar space.
They can affect the atmospheres of nearby planets.
Stellar mass loss is an important part of stellar evolution.

BINARY STAR SYSTEMS
A binary star system contains two stars gravitationally bound to each other.
The stars orbit their common center of mass.
Binary systems can have very different orbital separations.
Some binaries have stars close enough to exchange material.
Mass transfer can significantly change stellar evolution.
Eclipsing binaries periodically pass in front of one another from Earth's viewpoint.
Their brightness variations can reveal stellar properties.
Binary orbital measurements can provide direct estimates of stellar masses.
Multiple-star systems can contain three or more gravitationally associated stars.
Binary systems are important laboratories for testing stellar evolution models.

STELLAR DEATH
A star's final evolutionary stage depends strongly on its mass.
Low- and intermediate-mass stars can lose their outer layers.
The remaining core can become a white dwarf.
Massive stars can undergo core-collapse supernovae.
A massive stellar core can form a neutron star or black hole.
Supernova explosions can eject material into interstellar space.
Stellar remnants can remain detectable long after the original star disappears.
White dwarfs gradually cool over extremely long periods.
Neutron stars can produce powerful radiation and magnetic effects.
Stellar death contributes material and energy to the surrounding galaxy.

WHITE DWARFS
A white dwarf is the dense remnant of a low- or intermediate-mass star.
It forms after the star loses its outer layers.
White dwarfs no longer maintain normal hydrogen fusion in their cores.
They are supported mainly by electron degeneracy pressure.
A typical white dwarf is similar in size to Earth but contains a substantial fraction of the Sun's mass.
Their surfaces can initially be extremely hot.
White dwarfs gradually cool over time.
Most white dwarfs are composed mainly of carbon and oxygen.
Some white dwarfs can contain other compositions depending on their evolutionary history.
A white dwarf in a binary system can potentially gain mass from a companion.

NEUTRON STARS
A neutron star is an extremely dense stellar remnant.
It can form after the core-collapse supernova of a massive star.
Most of its matter is composed of extremely compressed nuclear material.
A neutron star typically has a radius of only about 10 to 15 kilometers.
Its mass can be greater than the mass of the Sun.
Neutron stars can rotate extremely rapidly.
Some neutron stars produce beams of radiation from their magnetic poles.
A rapidly rotating neutron star observed as a periodic source is called a pulsar.
Neutron stars can have extraordinarily strong magnetic fields.
They provide laboratories for studying matter under extreme conditions.

BLACK HOLE REMNANTS
Some massive stars can leave behind black holes after core collapse.
A black hole has gravity strong enough that light cannot escape from within its event horizon.
The event horizon marks a boundary beyond which information cannot return to an outside observer.
The mass of a stellar black hole is concentrated in a very compact region.
Material falling toward a black hole can form a hot accretion disk.
Black holes can also produce powerful jets when suitable conditions exist.
Not every massive star necessarily produces a black hole.
The final remnant depends on the star's mass, composition, mass loss, and explosion physics.
Stellar-mass black holes are much smaller than supermassive black holes found in galaxy centers.

STAR CLUSTERS
A star cluster is a group of stars held together by gravity or sharing a common origin.
Open clusters generally contain younger stars and are relatively loosely bound.
They are often found in the disks of galaxies.
Globular clusters are dense, roughly spherical systems containing many old stars.
Globular clusters are commonly found around galaxy halos.
Stars within a cluster often formed from the same molecular cloud.
Comparing stars in a cluster helps scientists study stellar evolution.
Cluster ages can be estimated using the main-sequence turnoff.
Star clusters provide useful laboratories for testing models of stellar populations.
Some clusters gradually lose stars because of gravitational interactions.

STELLAR EVOLUTION
Stellar evolution describes the physical changes a star undergoes during its lifetime.
Mass is the primary factor controlling the overall evolutionary path.
Chemical composition also affects stellar evolution.
A star begins as a collapsing region within a molecular cloud.
It becomes a protostar and eventually reaches the main sequence if it has sufficient mass.
Hydrogen fusion dominates the main-sequence stage.
Later evolution begins when the available core hydrogen becomes depleted.
Different masses lead to different final evolutionary outcomes.
The complete lifetime can range from millions to trillions of years depending on mass.
Studying stellar populations allows astronomers to reconstruct the history of galaxies.

KEY TERMS
Star: A massive astronomical object made primarily of hot plasma and powered for much of its life by nuclear fusion.
Protostar: A young stellar object forming through gravitational collapse before stable hydrogen fusion.
Main sequence: The evolutionary stage in which a star primarily fuses hydrogen in its core.
Luminosity: The total energy emitted by a star per unit time.
Effective temperature: A temperature describing the overall thermal radiation emitted by a star.
Spectroscopy: The analysis of electromagnetic radiation to determine physical and chemical properties.
Hydrostatic equilibrium: The balance between inward gravity and outward pressure within a star.
Stellar nucleosynthesis: The production of atomic nuclei through processes occurring in stars.
White dwarf: A dense stellar remnant left by a low- or intermediate-mass star.
Neutron star: An extremely dense stellar remnant formed from the collapsed core of a massive star.
