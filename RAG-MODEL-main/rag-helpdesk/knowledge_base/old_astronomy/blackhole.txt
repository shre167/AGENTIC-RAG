BLACK HOLES
A black hole is a region of spacetime where gravity is so strong that nothing can escape once it passes the event horizon.
Light cannot escape from inside a black hole's event horizon.
A black hole is not an empty hole or a cosmic vacuum cleaner.
Its gravitational influence depends on its mass, distance, and motion like other massive objects.
Black holes are predicted by Einstein's general theory of relativity.
The boundary surrounding a black hole is called the event horizon.
The event horizon is a causal boundary rather than a physical surface.
An object crossing the event horizon is not expected to encounter a solid barrier.
Black holes can interact gravitationally with stars, gas, dust, and other black holes.
Black holes are detected mainly through their effects on surrounding matter and spacetime.

KEY PROPERTIES
The three main physical parameters of an idealized black hole are mass, angular momentum, and electric charge.
Astrophysical black holes are expected to have extremely small net electric charge.
Mass determines the overall gravitational strength and characteristic size of a black hole.
Angular momentum describes the black hole's rotation.
A rotating black hole is commonly described by the Kerr solution.
A nonrotating, uncharged black hole is described by the Schwarzschild solution.
Black holes do not have a conventional solid surface.
The event horizon marks the region beyond which outward escape is impossible.
The Schwarzschild radius gives the event-horizon radius for a nonrotating, uncharged black hole.
The Schwarzschild radius is given by r_s = 2GM/c^2.
G represents the gravitational constant and c represents the speed of light.
For a given mass, a rotating black hole has a more complicated horizon structure than a Schwarzschild black hole.

TYPES BY MASS
Stellar-mass black holes typically have masses comparable to several to tens of times the Sun.
Stellar-mass black holes commonly form from the deaths of massive stars.
Intermediate-mass black holes are expected to lie between stellar and supermassive black holes in mass.
Strong evidence for individual intermediate-mass black holes is still an active research area.
Supermassive black holes contain millions to billions of solar masses.
Supermassive black holes are found at the centers of most large galaxies.
The Milky Way contains a supermassive black hole at its center.
Primordial black holes are hypothetical black holes that could have formed in the early universe.
There is currently no confirmed detection of primordial black holes.
Black holes can also grow through mergers with other black holes.

FORMATION OF STELLAR BLACK HOLES
A sufficiently massive star can end its life after exhausting the nuclear fuel supporting its core.
The core of a massive star can collapse under its own gravity.
Core collapse can produce a neutron star or a black hole depending on the remaining core mass and physical conditions.
Some stellar black holes form after supernova explosions.
Direct collapse can also produce a black hole with little or no bright supernova.
The exact boundary between neutron-star and black-hole formation depends on stellar evolution and the equation of state of dense matter.
Binary star systems can evolve into systems containing black holes.
A black hole can receive additional mass from a companion star.
Black holes can merge with other compact objects under suitable conditions.

GROWTH OF SUPERMASSIVE BLACK HOLES
Supermassive black holes can grow by accreting gas and other matter.
They can also grow through mergers with other black holes.
Galaxy mergers can bring central black holes together.
The earliest supermassive black holes require rapid growth from early black-hole seeds.
Possible seed mechanisms include remnants of massive early stars and direct-collapse scenarios.
Accretion can convert gravitational energy into intense radiation.
Rapidly accreting supermassive black holes can appear as active galactic nuclei.
Quasars are extremely luminous active galactic nuclei powered by accretion around supermassive black holes.
Black-hole growth is connected to the evolution of galaxies and their central regions.

ACCRETION DISKS
Matter falling toward a black hole usually forms an accretion disk when it has substantial angular momentum.
Gas in an accretion disk follows orbital paths while gradually losing angular momentum.
Viscosity and magnetic processes allow angular momentum to move outward through the disk.
Gravitational potential energy is converted into heat as matter moves inward.
Hot accretion disks can emit strongly across the electromagnetic spectrum.
Accretion around stellar black holes can produce powerful X-ray emission.
Accretion around supermassive black holes can produce optical, ultraviolet, X-ray, and radio emission.
The event horizon itself does not radiate like a normal physical surface.
Observed radiation usually comes from material and magnetic fields outside the event horizon.
The appearance of an accreting black hole depends strongly on the surrounding environment.

CORONAE AND JETS
Some accreting black holes have very hot regions called coronae above their accretion disks.
Black-hole coronae can produce high-energy X-rays.
Relativistic jets are narrow outflows of plasma moving at speeds close to the speed of light.
Jets can extend far beyond the immediate region around the black hole.
Strong magnetic fields can play a major role in launching and collimating jets.
Rotation of the black hole can contribute energy to jets through magnetic processes.
The Blandford-Znajek mechanism describes extraction of rotational energy through magnetic fields.
Not every black hole has a visible relativistic jet.
Jet formation depends on accretion conditions, magnetic fields, and black-hole properties.

EVENT HORIZON
The event horizon is the boundary separating regions that can communicate with distant observers from regions that cannot.
For a sufficiently distant observer, light emitted near the horizon becomes increasingly redshifted.
An infalling observer crossing the horizon of a sufficiently large black hole need not encounter a locally special physical boundary.
The experience near the horizon depends on the black hole's mass, spin, and the observer's trajectory.
The event horizon is not the same thing as the accretion disk.
The accretion disk exists outside the event horizon.
Matter in the accretion disk can remain in orbit before losing enough energy and angular momentum to move inward.
Once matter crosses the event horizon, it cannot send signals back to the outside.
The horizon is therefore a fundamental causal boundary in spacetime.

SPACETIME AND GRAVITY
General relativity describes gravity as the curvature of spacetime caused by mass and energy.
A black hole represents an extreme concentration of gravitational effects.
Near a black hole, spacetime curvature can become extremely strong.
Clocks at different gravitational potentials are measured to run at different rates by distant observers.
This effect is known as gravitational time dilation.
Light escaping a strong gravitational field can experience gravitational redshift.
The closer an emitter is to a black-hole horizon, the greater the gravitational redshift observed from far away.
These effects are consequences of general relativity rather than properties of a physical black-hole surface.
Black holes provide important environments for testing strong-field gravity.

TIDAL FORCES
Tidal forces arise because gravity can vary significantly across the size of an object.
Near a black hole, strong tidal forces can stretch an object along the direction toward the black hole.
Compression can occur in directions perpendicular to the stretching.
Extreme tidal stretching is informally called spaghettification.
For a stellar-mass black hole, tidal forces near the horizon can be very strong.
For a supermassive black hole, tidal forces at the horizon can be much weaker.
An object can therefore cross the horizon of a sufficiently massive black hole before experiencing extreme tidal disruption.
Tidal effects become stronger as the object moves closer to the central region.
The strength of tidal forces depends on the black-hole mass and the object's distance.

ROTATING BLACK HOLES
A rotating black hole is described by the Kerr solution in classical general relativity.
Rotation causes spacetime itself to be dragged around the black hole.
This effect is called frame dragging.
The region where frame dragging becomes unavoidable is called the ergosphere.
The ergosphere lies outside the event horizon of a rotating black hole.
An observer can enter the ergosphere without necessarily crossing the event horizon.
Processes involving the ergosphere can extract rotational energy from a rotating black hole.
The Penrose process is a theoretical mechanism for extracting rotational energy.
Magnetic-field processes can also extract rotational energy and help power relativistic jets.
Black-hole spin can influence the structure of nearby accretion flows.

PHOTON ORBITS AND SHADOWS
Light passing near a black hole can be strongly deflected by gravity.
This gravitational deflection produces strong gravitational lensing.
A photon sphere exists around a nonrotating Schwarzschild black hole.
The Schwarzschild photon sphere lies at 1.5 times the Schwarzschild radius.
Light can orbit temporarily on unstable paths near the photon sphere.
Some light trajectories are captured by the black hole instead of escaping.
The black-hole shadow is a dark region produced by the capture of light combined with the surrounding emission.
The shadow is not a photograph of a solid black-hole surface.
The apparent shadow depends on spacetime geometry and the surrounding emitting material.
Rotating black holes produce more complex shadow structures than nonrotating ones.

HAWKING RADIATION
Hawking radiation is a theoretical prediction combining quantum effects with curved spacetime.
It implies that black holes can emit thermal radiation.
The predicted radiation causes a black hole to gradually lose mass.
The temperature of Hawking radiation decreases as black-hole mass increases.
Astrophysical black holes are expected to have extremely low Hawking temperatures.
Their Hawking radiation is therefore far too weak to be easily detected against ordinary astrophysical backgrounds.
Hawking radiation has not been directly observed from an astrophysical black hole.
If a black hole loses enough mass, its evaporation would accelerate as its temperature rises.
The final stage of black-hole evaporation requires physics beyond the standard semiclassical description.

BLACK-HOLE INFORMATION
Quantum theory suggests that physical information should not simply disappear.
Black-hole evaporation creates a theoretical conflict between quantum mechanics and classical descriptions of black holes.
This issue is called the black-hole information paradox.
The paradox is an important problem in theoretical physics.
Proposed solutions involve ideas about quantum gravity and the encoding of information.
No single experimentally confirmed complete solution has been established.
The information paradox connects black holes with fundamental questions about spacetime and quantum mechanics.
Black holes are therefore important laboratories for theoretical studies of gravity and quantum physics.

GRAVITATIONAL WAVES
Accelerating compact objects can produce gravitational waves.
Binary black holes can lose orbital energy through gravitational-wave emission.
The orbit can shrink as gravitational radiation carries energy and angular momentum away.
Two black holes can eventually merge into a single larger black hole.
The merger produces a strong burst of gravitational waves.
The final black hole undergoes a settling phase called ringdown.
LIGO, Virgo, and KAGRA are major gravitational-wave observatories.
The first directly detected gravitational-wave event, GW150914, came from a binary black-hole merger.
Gravitational-wave observations provide information about black-hole masses, spins, and merger dynamics.

OBSERVING BLACK HOLES
Black holes are difficult to observe directly because they do not emit ordinary light from inside their horizons.
Astronomers often detect black holes through their effects on nearby matter.
X-ray binaries can reveal stellar-mass black holes accreting matter from companion stars.
The motion of visible companion stars can provide evidence for an unseen massive object.
Stars orbiting a galactic center can reveal the mass of a central black hole.
Gravitational lensing can reveal the influence of black holes on passing light.
Gravitational-wave detectors can observe mergers of black holes.
Very long baseline interferometry can image emission around supermassive black holes.

CYGNUS X-1
Cygnus X-1 is a well-studied X-ray binary system.
The system contains a black-hole candidate and a massive companion star.
Gas transferred from the companion forms a hot accretion flow.
The system produces strong X-ray emission.
Cygnus X-1 provided important evidence for the existence of stellar-mass black holes.
Its study helped establish methods for identifying black holes in binary systems.
X-ray binaries are important laboratories for studying accretion physics.
They also help researchers estimate black-hole masses and spins.

SAGITTARIUS A*
Sagittarius A* is the supermassive black hole at the center of the Milky Way.
It has a mass of roughly 4.3 million times the mass of the Sun.
Stars near Sagittarius A* follow rapid orbits around the galactic center.
Measurements of these stellar orbits provide strong evidence for a compact central mass.
Sagittarius A* is relatively faint compared with many active galactic nuclei.
The Event Horizon Telescope produced an image of the region around Sagittarius A* in 2022.
The image shows a bright emission ring surrounding a central dark shadow-like region.
The observed structure is consistent with predictions for a supermassive black hole.

M87*
M87* is the supermassive black hole at the center of the galaxy Messier 87.
Its mass is approximately 6.5 billion times the mass of the Sun.
M87* is surrounded by a bright accreting environment.
The galaxy M87 has a powerful relativistic jet extending far from its central region.
The Event Horizon Telescope released the first image of a black-hole shadow in 2019.
The image showed a bright ring-like structure around a dark central region.
The observed appearance is produced by emission near the black hole and strong gravitational lensing.
The image does not directly show the event horizon as a physical surface.

EVENT HORIZON TELESCOPE
The Event Horizon Telescope is a global network of radio observatories operating as a very long baseline interferometer.
It combines observations from widely separated telescopes to achieve extremely high angular resolution.
The EHT operates primarily at millimeter wavelengths.
Its observations can resolve structures on scales comparable to a black-hole shadow.
The EHT observed M87* and Sagittarius A*.
The M87* result was released in 2019.
The Sagittarius A* result was released in 2022.
EHT observations provide tests of strong-field gravitational physics.
They also reveal properties of the hot plasma surrounding supermassive black holes.

KEY TERMS
Black hole means a region of spacetime bounded by an event horizon from which escape is impossible.
Event horizon means the causal boundary of a black hole.
Schwarzschild radius means the event-horizon radius of a nonrotating, uncharged black hole.
Accretion means the process by which matter falls toward and becomes incorporated into a compact object.
Accretion disk means a rotating disk of matter orbiting and feeding a compact object.
Ergosphere means the region outside a rotating black hole where frame dragging is unavoidable.
Frame dragging means the dragging of spacetime caused by a rotating massive object.
Gravitational lensing means the deflection and distortion of light by gravity.
Black-hole shadow means the dark apparent region associated with photon capture near a black hole.
Gravitational waves are propagating disturbances in spacetime produced by accelerating mass-energy distributions.
Ringdown is the damped oscillation of a newly formed black hole after a merger.
Supermassive black hole means a black hole with millions to billions of solar masses.
Stellar-mass black hole means a black hole formed through stellar-scale processes.
Kerr black hole means a rotating black-hole solution of general relativity.
Schwarzschild black hole means a nonrotating, uncharged black-hole solution.
Hawking radiation means the predicted quantum thermal emission associated with black holes.

SCIENTIFIC IMPORTANCE
Black holes test general relativity in some of the strongest gravitational environments known.
Their accretion systems help researchers study high-energy plasma physics.
Their mergers provide direct observations of gravitational-wave phenomena.
Their shadows allow tests of strong gravitational lensing and compact-object geometry.
Their growth is connected to galaxy formation and evolution.
Black holes connect astrophysics with quantum theory and fundamental physics.
Observations from electromagnetic and gravitational-wave astronomy provide complementary information.
Future observations will improve measurements of black-hole populations, spins, environments, and evolution.
Black-hole research combines theory, observation, simulation, and advanced instrumentation.
Black holes remain among the most important objects for understanding extreme physics in the universe.
