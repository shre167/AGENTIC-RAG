EARTH

1. OVERVIEW
   Earth is the third planet from the Sun.
   Earth is a terrestrial planet with a solid rocky surface.
   Earth is the largest of the four terrestrial planets by diameter.
   Earth is the most massive terrestrial planet in the Solar System.
   Earth has extensive liquid water on its surface.
   Earth has a nitrogen-rich atmosphere containing oxygen.
   Earth is currently the only planet known to support life.
   Earth has one natural satellite, the Moon.
   Earth's average distance from the Sun is about 150 million kilometers.
   This distance is approximately one astronomical unit.
   Earth completes one orbit around the Sun in about 365.25 days.
   Earth rotates once approximately every 24 hours.
   Earth's rotation produces the cycle of day and night.
   Earth's orbit and axial tilt contribute to seasonal changes.
   Earth has a global magnetic field generated by processes in its interior.
   Earth is an important reference planet for comparative planetary science.

2. FORMATION
   Earth formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago.
   Earth formed from material in the early Solar System.
   Small solid bodies collided and accumulated through a process called accretion.
   Repeated collisions gradually produced a larger planetary body.
   Earth's interior became differentiated as the young planet heated and materials separated.
   Dense metallic material moved toward the center.
   Less dense rocky material formed the mantle and crust.
   Earth's early surface was much hotter than its present surface.
   Volcanic activity released gases that contributed to the developing atmosphere.
   Earth's oceans formed through a combination of sources and long-term atmospheric processes.
   The early Earth underwent major geological and chemical changes.
   Earth's formation provides a model for understanding terrestrial planet formation.

3. ORBIT
   Earth follows an elliptical orbit around the Sun.
   Earth's orbital eccentricity is relatively small.
   Earth's average orbital distance is about 149.6 million kilometers.
   Earth completes one revolution around the Sun in approximately 365.25 days.
   The extra fraction of a day is accounted for by leap-year adjustments in calendars.
   Earth travels around the Sun at an average speed of about 29.8 kilometers per second.
   Earth's orbital plane is called the ecliptic plane.
   Earth's orbit determines the length of its year.
   The distance from Earth to the Sun changes slightly during the year.
   Earth is closest to the Sun at perihelion.
   Earth is farthest from the Sun at aphelion.
   Orbital distance alone does not cause Earth's seasons.

4. ROTATION
   Earth rotates around an imaginary axis passing through its poles.
   Earth completes approximately one rotation every 24 hours relative to the Sun.
   A sidereal rotation takes approximately 23 hours and 56 minutes.
   Earth rotates from west to east.
   The rotation makes the Sun and stars appear to move across the sky.
   Earth's rotation produces alternating periods of daylight and darkness.
   Rotation also contributes to the Coriolis effect in the atmosphere and oceans.
   Earth's equatorial region moves faster than regions closer to the poles.
   The rotation axis is tilted relative to Earth's orbital plane.
   Earth's axial tilt is approximately 23.4 degrees.
   The axial tilt is a major cause of Earth's seasons.
   Earth's rotation is gradually slowing because of tidal interactions with the Moon.

5. INTERNAL STRUCTURE
   Earth has a layered internal structure.
   The crust is Earth's outermost solid layer.
   The mantle lies beneath the crust.
   The outer core lies beneath the mantle.
   The inner core is at the center of Earth.
   The crust is much thinner than the mantle.
   The mantle consists mainly of hot solid silicate rock.
   Mantle rock can flow slowly over geological timescales.
   The outer core is primarily composed of liquid iron and nickel.
   The inner core is primarily composed of solid iron and nickel.
   Earth's core is extremely hot and under immense pressure.
   Movement of liquid metal in the outer core generates Earth's magnetic field.
   Earth's internal heat drives many geological processes.

6. CRUST
   Earth's crust is divided broadly into continental and oceanic crust.
   Continental crust is generally thicker than oceanic crust.
   Oceanic crust is generally denser than continental crust.
   The crust consists mainly of silicate minerals.
   Oceanic crust is continually created at mid-ocean ridges.
   Old oceanic crust can be recycled into Earth's mantle at subduction zones.
   Continental crust can survive for very long geological periods.
   Earth's crust forms part of the lithosphere.
   The lithosphere includes the crust and the rigid uppermost mantle.
   The lithosphere is divided into tectonic plates.
   Movement of these plates changes Earth's surface over time.

7. MANTLE
   The mantle extends from beneath the crust to the outer core.
   The mantle makes up most of Earth's volume.
   Mantle material is mainly composed of silicate minerals rich in magnesium and iron.
   The mantle is mostly solid despite its high temperature.
   Heat causes very slow movement within the mantle.
   This movement contributes to plate tectonic processes.
   The upper mantle contains a region called the asthenosphere.
   The asthenosphere can deform more easily than the rigid lithosphere above it.
   Heat from Earth's interior can drive mantle convection.
   Mantle processes influence volcanism and the movement of tectonic plates.

8. CORE
   Earth's core consists mainly of iron and nickel.
   The outer core is liquid.
   The inner core is solid.
   The difference between the outer and inner core is strongly related to pressure and temperature.
   Movement of electrically conducting liquid metal in the outer core generates the geodynamo.
   The geodynamo produces Earth's global magnetic field.
   The core contains a large fraction of Earth's total mass.
   Temperatures in the core are comparable to or higher than those at the surface of the Sun.
   Pressure at Earth's center is extremely high.
   The core continues to cool slowly over geological time.
   Heat released from the cooling core contributes to Earth's internal energy budget.

9. ATMOSPHERE
   Earth has a relatively thin atmosphere compared with the size of the planet.
   Nitrogen makes up about 78 percent of dry air.
   Oxygen makes up about 21 percent of dry air.
   Argon is the third most abundant major component of dry air.
   Carbon dioxide is present in a much smaller concentration.
   Water vapor varies significantly with location and weather.
   The atmosphere provides gases required by many living organisms.
   The atmosphere helps regulate Earth's surface temperature.
   The atmosphere absorbs and scatters incoming and outgoing radiation.
   The atmosphere protects the surface from much harmful ultraviolet radiation.
   The atmosphere is divided into layers based mainly on temperature behavior.
   Major atmospheric layers include the troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere, and exosphere.

10. WEATHER AND CLIMATE
    Weather describes short-term atmospheric conditions.
    Climate describes long-term patterns of weather in a region.
    The troposphere is the lowest major atmospheric layer.
    Most weather occurs in the troposphere.
    Temperature generally decreases with altitude through much of the troposphere.
    The stratosphere lies above the troposphere.
    The ozone layer is located mainly within the stratosphere.
    The atmosphere transports heat and moisture around the planet.
    Solar heating drives many atmospheric circulation processes.
    Oceans strongly influence Earth's climate.
    Clouds affect both incoming sunlight and outgoing infrared radiation.
    Earth's climate has changed naturally throughout geological history.

11. WATER
    Water covers approximately 71 percent of Earth's surface.
    Most Earth's water is contained in the oceans.
    Only a small fraction of Earth's water is fresh water.
    Fresh water exists in glaciers, ice sheets, groundwater, lakes, rivers, soil, and the atmosphere.
    The water cycle continuously moves water between Earth's surface, atmosphere, and subsurface.
    Evaporation transfers water from surfaces into the atmosphere.
    Condensation forms clouds from water vapor.
    Precipitation returns water to Earth's surface.
    Runoff carries water across land into rivers, lakes, and oceans.
    Groundwater is water stored beneath Earth's surface.
    The oceans play a major role in Earth's climate system.
    Liquid water is an important factor in Earth's ability to support life.

12. OCEANS
    Earth has five commonly recognized ocean regions: Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern, and Arctic.
    The Pacific Ocean is the largest ocean.
    The Atlantic Ocean is the second-largest ocean.
    The Indian Ocean lies mainly between Africa, Asia, Australia, and the Southern Ocean.
    The Southern Ocean surrounds Antarctica.
    The Arctic Ocean is the smallest of the five commonly recognized oceans.
    Ocean water is salty because it contains dissolved minerals and ions.
    Ocean currents transport heat around the planet.
    Deep ocean circulation is influenced by temperature and salinity.
    Oceans absorb and store large amounts of solar energy.
    Oceans also absorb significant amounts of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere.
    Ocean processes influence weather, climate, and ecosystems.

13. MAGNETIC FIELD
    Earth has a global magnetic field.
    The magnetic field extends far into space around the planet.
    The region dominated by Earth's magnetic field is called the magnetosphere.
    The magnetosphere interacts with the solar wind.
    The solar wind consists mainly of charged particles released by the Sun.
    Earth's magnetic field deflects much of the solar wind around the planet.
    Charged particles can enter the upper atmosphere near the magnetic poles.
    These interactions can produce auroras.
    Earth's magnetic field changes gradually over time.
    The magnetic poles move and are not fixed permanently.
    Earth's magnetic field provides important protection from the space environment.

14. TECTONIC PLATES
    Earth's lithosphere is divided into moving tectonic plates.
    Tectonic plates move slowly over the underlying asthenosphere.
    Plate movement is typically measured in centimeters per year.
    There are several large tectonic plates and many smaller plates.
    Plate boundaries can be divergent, convergent, or transform.
    Divergent boundaries occur where plates move apart.
    Convergent boundaries occur where plates move toward each other.
    Transform boundaries occur where plates slide past each other.
    Plate interactions can produce mountains, earthquakes, volcanoes, and ocean trenches.
    Subduction occurs when one tectonic plate moves beneath another.
    Plate tectonics continuously reshapes Earth's surface.

15. GEOLOGICAL PROCESSES
    Earth's surface is modified by internal and external geological processes.
    Volcanism transports material from Earth's interior to the surface.
    Earthquakes occur when stored geological stress is suddenly released.
    Erosion removes and transports surface material.
    Weathering breaks down rocks at or near Earth's surface.
    Sedimentation deposits transported material in new locations.
    Mountain building can occur through tectonic plate interactions.
    Glaciers can erode and reshape landscapes.
    Rivers transport sediments and carve valleys.
    Wind can transport and deposit fine particles.
    These processes create and modify Earth's geological features.

16. MOON RELATIONSHIP
    The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite.
    The Moon orbits Earth at an average distance of about 384,400 kilometers.
    The Moon is gravitationally bound to Earth.
    The Moon is tidally locked to Earth.
    Because of tidal locking, nearly the same lunar hemisphere generally faces Earth.
    The Moon's gravity produces most of Earth's ocean tides.
    The Sun also contributes significantly to Earth's tides.
    The Moon helps stabilize Earth's axial orientation over long timescales.
    The Moon's orbital motion produces the changing lunar phases observed from Earth.
    The Earth-Moon system is important for studying planetary-satellite evolution.

17. LIFE
    Earth is the only known planet with confirmed life.
    Life exists in oceans, on land, underground, and in the atmosphere.
    Earth contains a wide diversity of organisms.
    Liquid water is widespread on Earth's surface and subsurface.
    Carbon is a fundamental element in known biological systems.
    Earth's atmosphere contains oxygen produced largely through biological processes.
    Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy in many organisms.
    Earth's biosphere interacts continuously with the atmosphere, oceans, rocks, and soil.
    Life has influenced Earth's atmosphere and surface chemistry.
    The history of life is recorded in fossils and geological materials.
    Studying Earth helps scientists identify conditions that may support life elsewhere.

18. KEY TERMS
    Earth: The third planet from the Sun and the only known planet supporting life.
    Terrestrial planet: A rocky planet with a solid surface.
    Astronomical unit: The average Earth-Sun distance, approximately 149.6 million kilometers.
    Axial tilt: The angle between a planet's rotation axis and the perpendicular to its orbital plane.
    Crust: Earth's outermost solid geological layer.
    Mantle: The thick rocky layer between Earth's crust and core.
    Outer core: Earth's liquid metallic layer surrounding the inner core.
    Inner core: Earth's solid metallic central region.
    Lithosphere: The rigid outer layer consisting of the crust and uppermost mantle.
    Asthenosphere: A mechanically weaker region beneath the lithosphere.
    Plate tectonics: The movement and interaction of Earth's lithospheric plates.
    Magnetosphere: The region around Earth dominated by its magnetic field.
    Atmosphere: The layer of gases surrounding Earth.
    Hydrosphere: The total amount of water present on Earth.
    Biosphere: The regions of Earth where life exists.
    Perihelion: The point in Earth's orbit closest to the Sun.
    Aphelion: The point in Earth's orbit farthest from the Sun.
