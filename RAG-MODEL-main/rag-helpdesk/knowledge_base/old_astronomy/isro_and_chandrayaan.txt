ISRO AND CHANDRAYAAN
ISRO stands for Indian Space Research Organisation.
ISRO is India's national space agency and is responsible for India's civilian space programme.
ISRO develops launch vehicles, spacecraft, satellites, scientific missions, and space technologies.
ISRO conducts missions for Earth observation, communication, navigation, meteorology, planetary science, and space exploration.
ISRO operates launch facilities, spacecraft control centres, research centres, and other space infrastructure.
India's space programme has developed capabilities in satellite launches, remote sensing, communications, and planetary exploration.
ISRO has launched missions to the Moon, Mars, and other destinations in space.
The Chandrayaan programme is India's series of lunar exploration missions.
Chandrayaan missions are designed to study the Moon using spacecraft instruments and scientific observations.
Lunar exploration helps scientists understand the Moon's surface, composition, interior, and geological history.

THE MOON
The Moon is Earth's natural satellite.
The Moon orbits Earth and rotates on its axis.
The Moon is tidally locked to Earth.
Tidal locking causes approximately the same lunar hemisphere to face Earth.
The Moon has no substantial atmosphere comparable to Earth's atmosphere.
Its surface is covered by regolith produced largely through impacts and surface weathering processes.
The lunar surface contains highlands, maria, craters, mountains, valleys, and other geological features.
Maria are large dark plains formed primarily by ancient volcanic activity.
The lunar highlands are generally older and more heavily cratered than the maria.
Impact craters are among the most widespread geological features on the Moon.
The Moon preserves evidence of impacts that occurred throughout Solar System history.
Polar regions contain permanently shadowed areas where sunlight does not directly reach the surface.
Water ice has been detected in some permanently shadowed lunar regions.
Lunar water resources are scientifically important and potentially relevant to future exploration.

CHANDRAYAAN PROGRAMME
Chandrayaan means "Moon craft" and refers to India's lunar exploration programme.
The Chandrayaan programme includes multiple missions with different scientific and technological objectives.
Chandrayaan-1 was India's first lunar exploration mission.
Chandrayaan-1 was launched in 2008.
Chandrayaan-1 operated in lunar orbit and carried scientific instruments from India and international partners.
The mission investigated the lunar surface and mineral composition using multiple remote-sensing techniques.
Chandrayaan-1 contributed important observations related to water and hydroxyl on the lunar surface.
The mission used instruments operating in different parts of the electromagnetic spectrum.
Chandrayaan-1 demonstrated India's capability to conduct scientific observations from lunar orbit.
The mission ended after its operational period in 2009.
Chandrayaan-1 data continue to be useful for lunar science.

CHANDRAYAAN-1 SCIENCE
Chandrayaan-1 carried instruments for imaging, mineral mapping, X-ray measurements, and other observations.
The Terrain Mapping Camera was designed to produce high-resolution images of the lunar surface.
The Moon Mineralogy Mapper was an imaging spectrometer used to study lunar mineral composition.
The High Energy X-ray Spectrometer investigated elements on the lunar surface using X-ray measurements.
The mission carried a Moon Impact Probe as a technology and scientific experiment.
The Moon Impact Probe was released from Chandrayaan-1 and impacted the lunar surface.
The probe carried instruments for studying the lunar environment during descent.
Chandrayaan-1 observations helped improve understanding of lunar surface materials.
Spectral measurements can identify minerals through characteristic absorption features.
Lunar remote sensing can reveal differences between highlands, maria, and other surface regions.

CHANDRAYAAN-2
Chandrayaan-2 was India's second lunar exploration mission.
Chandrayaan-2 was launched in July 2019.
The mission included an orbiter, a lander named Vikram, and a rover named Pragyan.
The orbiter was designed to study the Moon from lunar orbit.
The Vikram lander was designed to perform a controlled landing near the lunar south polar region.
The Pragyan rover was designed to travel across the lunar surface near the landing site.
The lander and rover were intended to perform in-situ measurements.
The Chandrayaan-2 orbiter successfully entered lunar orbit.
The orbiter has continued to provide scientific observations of the Moon.
The lander experienced an issue during the final phase of its landing attempt.
The planned surface mission of the Vikram lander and Pragyan rover was therefore not completed.
The Chandrayaan-2 orbiter remained an important scientific asset.
Its instruments continue to provide observations useful for lunar research.

CHANDRAYAAN-2 ORBITER
The Chandrayaan-2 orbiter carries several scientific instruments for lunar observation.
The Orbiter High Resolution Camera provides high-resolution images of the lunar surface.
OHRC observations can be used to study small-scale surface features.
The Imaging Infrared Spectrometer investigates the composition of lunar surface materials.
The Dual Frequency Synthetic Aperture Radar can investigate lunar surface and subsurface properties.
The radar operates at different frequencies to study characteristics of lunar materials.
The Chandrayaan-2 orbiter also carries instruments for studying lunar exosphere and elemental composition.
Orbital observations allow repeated imaging of large areas of the Moon.
Repeated measurements can help identify changes or improve maps of lunar features.
High-resolution orbital data can support landing-site analysis.
Orbiter data are useful for geological, mineralogical, and topographic studies.

CHANDRAYAAN-2 LANDER AND ROVER
Vikram was the lander component of Chandrayaan-2.
Pragyan was the rover component of Chandrayaan-2.
The lander was designed to carry scientific instruments to the lunar surface.
The rover was designed to move around the landing region and perform surface measurements.
The rover was intended to use solar power for its operations.
The rover carried instruments intended to determine the elemental composition of lunar soil.
The lander carried instruments for measuring lunar surface temperature and seismic activity.
The lander also included an instrument designed to study the lunar plasma environment.
The landing attempt demonstrated technologies needed for future controlled lunar landings.
Lessons from Chandrayaan-2 contributed to later lunar mission planning and engineering.

CHANDRAYAAN-3
Chandrayaan-3 was India's third lunar exploration mission.
Chandrayaan-3 was launched in July 2023.
The mission was designed primarily to demonstrate safe and controlled lunar landing and surface mobility.
Chandrayaan-3 consisted of a lander named Vikram and a rover named Pragyan.
The spacecraft entered lunar orbit before beginning its landing sequence.
The Vikram lander successfully performed a controlled landing on the Moon in August 2023.
The landing site was located in the lunar south polar region.
India became the first country to successfully land a spacecraft in the lunar south polar region.
Pragyan was deployed from the Vikram lander after landing.
The rover performed surface mobility and scientific measurements near the landing site.
The mission demonstrated India's capability for soft landing and rover operations on the Moon.

CHANDRAYAAN-3 LANDER
The Vikram lander carried scientific instruments for in-situ lunar measurements.
The lander measured seismic activity in the vicinity of the landing site.
The lander measured thermal properties of the lunar surface.
The lander included an instrument for measuring the plasma environment near the lunar surface.
The lander's propulsion and navigation systems supported the controlled descent.
The lander used cameras and sensors to determine its position and assess the surface during descent.
Landing requires accurate velocity reduction and navigation during the final approach.
The mission demonstrated controlled landing in a challenging lunar environment.
Surface instruments provide measurements that cannot be obtained in exactly the same way from orbit.
Lander observations complement data collected by lunar orbiters.

CHANDRAYAAN-3 ROVER
Pragyan is the rover carried by Chandrayaan-3.
The rover was deployed from the Vikram lander after the successful landing.
Pragyan was designed for short-range mobility on the lunar surface.
The rover carried instruments for measuring the elemental composition of lunar soil and rocks.
Laser-based measurements can identify elements in material near the rover.
X-ray-based measurements can also provide elemental composition information.
The rover used solar power and communicated through the lander.
Rover tracks provide a record of the rover's movement across the lunar surface.
The rover operated during the lunar daytime because its systems depended on sunlight and suitable thermal conditions.
The rover's measurements provided in-situ information about the composition of the landing region.

CHANDRAYAAN-3 LANDING SITE
The Chandrayaan-3 landing site is in the Moon's southern polar region.
Lunar polar regions are scientifically important because of their unusual lighting and thermal conditions.
Some polar locations experience prolonged illumination while nearby regions can remain permanently shadowed.
Permanently shadowed regions can preserve volatile materials because they remain extremely cold.
Water ice deposits in polar regions are important targets for lunar science.
The landing region contains geological features that can be studied using surface observations.
Images from the lander can document rocks, soil, craters, and nearby terrain.
Surface measurements can be compared with orbital observations of the same region.
Studying polar materials can improve understanding of the Moon's geological and volatile history.

LUNAR SCIENTIFIC INSTRUMENTS
A camera records images of the lunar surface and surrounding environment.
A spectrometer measures radiation across wavelengths to identify material properties.
A seismometer detects ground vibrations and seismic activity.
A thermal probe measures temperature or thermal properties of lunar materials.
A laser-induced breakdown spectrometer can determine elemental composition from laser-generated plasma.
An alpha particle X-ray spectrometer can determine elemental composition using X-ray signatures.
A radar instrument can investigate surface and subsurface characteristics.
A spectrometer can study the composition of the lunar exosphere.
Different instruments provide complementary information about the same lunar environment.
Combining measurements improves scientific interpretation of lunar surface processes.

LUNAR ORBITAL OBSERVATION
Orbital spacecraft repeatedly pass over different regions of the Moon.
Orbiter cameras can produce detailed maps of lunar terrain.
Spectrometers can map the distribution of minerals and other surface materials.
Radar instruments can investigate regions that are difficult to study using visible light.
Laser altimeters can measure surface elevation.
Gravity measurements can reveal variations in the Moon's internal mass distribution.
Orbital data can identify craters, faults, volcanic deposits, and other geological structures.
High-resolution imagery supports detailed studies of lunar landing sites.
Long-term orbital observations can improve understanding of lunar surface evolution.

LUNAR GEOLOGY
The Moon's crust formed early in its geological history.
Large impacts modified the lunar crust throughout its evolution.
Volcanic activity produced basaltic plains known as maria.
The lunar surface is continuously modified by impacts from small particles and larger bodies.
Regolith accumulates as rocks are broken down by impacts.
Lunar soil differs from ordinary terrestrial soil because it forms without biological activity.
Solar radiation and solar-wind particles interact directly with exposed lunar materials.
The Moon's geological history is recorded in its rocks, minerals, craters, and surface structures.
Lunar samples provide direct laboratory evidence about the Moon's composition and history.
Remote sensing allows similar investigations across much larger areas.

ISRO LAUNCH VEHICLES
ISRO develops launch vehicles for placing satellites and spacecraft into space.
The Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle is commonly known as PSLV.
PSLV has been used extensively for Earth-observation and other satellite missions.
The Geosynchronous Satellite Launch Vehicle is commonly known as GSLV.
GSLV is designed to launch heavier payloads into higher-energy orbits than PSLV in appropriate configurations.
The Launch Vehicle Mark-3 is a heavy-lift launch vehicle developed by ISRO.
LVM3 was used to launch the Chandrayaan-3 mission.
Launch vehicles use multiple propulsion stages to achieve the velocity required for spaceflight.
Rocket stages can use solid, liquid, or cryogenic propulsion technologies depending on the vehicle.
Launch vehicle selection depends on spacecraft mass, target orbit, trajectory, and mission requirements.

IMPORTANCE OF LUNAR EXPLORATION
The Moon provides a relatively accessible environment for planetary science.
Lunar rocks preserve information about the early history of the Earth-Moon system.
The Moon can be used to study the effects of impacts, volcanism, radiation, and space weathering.
Polar regions are important for studying the distribution of water and other volatiles.
Lunar exploration develops technologies for navigation, landing, communication, robotics, and surface operations.
Robotic lunar missions can test technologies needed for future human exploration.
Lunar observations can help scientists compare the Moon with other rocky planetary bodies.
India's Chandrayaan missions have expanded India's capabilities in planetary exploration.
Scientific data from lunar missions are used by researchers to investigate the Moon's origin and evolution.
Lunar exploration remains an important part of international planetary science.
