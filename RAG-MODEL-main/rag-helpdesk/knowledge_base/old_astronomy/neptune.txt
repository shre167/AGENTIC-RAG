NEPTUNE — OVERVIEW
Neptune is the eighth and farthest recognized planet from the Sun.
Neptune is classified as an ice giant.
It is the fourth-largest planet in the Solar System by diameter.
Neptune has a diameter of about 49,244 kilometers.
Its mass is about 17 times the mass of Earth.
Neptune has an average distance of about 30.1 astronomical units from the Sun.
One astronomical unit is the average Earth-Sun distance.
Neptune takes about 165 Earth years to complete one orbit.
Its average orbital speed is about 5.4 kilometers per second.
Neptune has an average density of about 1.64 grams per cubic centimeter.
Its blue appearance is influenced by methane in its atmosphere and atmospheric aerosols.

DISCOVERY
Neptune was discovered in 1846.
Its discovery was strongly influenced by mathematical predictions.
Astronomers noticed that Uranus's orbit did not perfectly match predictions.
The unexplained orbital differences suggested the gravitational influence of another planet.
Mathematical calculations were used to predict the location of the unknown planet.
Johann Galle and Heinrich d'Arrest observed Neptune near the predicted position.
The discovery demonstrated the power of mathematical astronomy.
Neptune became the first planet discovered through mathematical prediction.
The exact history of the prediction involved work by several astronomers.
Neptune's discovery became an important event in nineteenth-century astronomy.

ORIGIN AND FORMATION
Neptune formed about 4.5 billion years ago during Solar System formation.
It formed in the cold outer region of the young Solar System.
Its building material contained substantial volatile compounds.
Water, ammonia, and methane became important components of its interior.
Neptune formed beyond the region where terrestrial planets developed.
The snow line allowed water and other volatile materials to condense into solids.
Planetesimals accumulated through gravitational interactions and collisions.
Neptune's final position may have been affected by gravitational interactions with other planets.
Models of the early Solar System include possible migration of the giant planets.
The detailed formation history of Neptune remains an active research topic.

ORBIT
Neptune follows an elliptical orbit around the Sun.
Its orbit is nearly circular compared with many other planetary orbits.
Neptune travels at an average distance of about 4.5 billion kilometers from the Sun.
Sunlight at Neptune is much weaker than sunlight at Earth.
A Neptunian year lasts approximately 165 Earth years.
Neptune completes less than one full orbit during a human lifetime.
Its long orbital period results from its great distance from the Sun.
Neptune's orbit is affected slightly by the gravitational influence of other bodies.
Neptune and Pluto have orbital configurations that avoid close encounters.
Orbital resonance plays an important role in the long-term dynamics of some trans-Neptunian objects.

ROTATION
Neptune rotates once in roughly 16 hours.
Its rotation period is shorter than Earth's day.
Neptune's rapid rotation contributes to its atmospheric dynamics.
The planet's equatorial region rotates faster than its polar regions in atmospheric circulation.
This difference is associated with strong atmospheric winds.
Neptune has an axial tilt of about 28.3 degrees.
Its axial tilt produces seasons similar in basic geometry to those of Earth.
However, each Neptunian season lasts more than four decades.
Seasonal changes occur very slowly because of Neptune's long orbital period.
Long-term observations are therefore important for studying its seasonal atmosphere.

ATMOSPHERE
Neptune has a thick atmosphere surrounding the planet.
The atmosphere is composed primarily of hydrogen and helium.
Methane is present in smaller amounts.
Methane absorbs red light in Neptune's upper atmosphere.
This absorption contributes to Neptune's blue coloration.
The atmosphere also contains trace hydrocarbons and other compounds.
Clouds form at different levels depending on temperature and pressure.
Methane ice clouds can occur in the upper atmosphere.
Deeper cloud layers may contain ammonia and water-rich materials.
The atmosphere gradually becomes denser with increasing depth.

ATMOSPHERIC WEATHER
Neptune has some of the fastest winds measured on a planet.
Wind speeds can exceed 1,500 kilometers per hour in some regions.
Strong winds occur despite Neptune receiving very little sunlight.
Neptune's atmosphere contains large-scale storms and dark atmospheric features.
The Great Dark Spot was observed by Voyager 2 during its 1989 flyby.
The original Great Dark Spot later disappeared from observations.
Other dark storms have been observed at different times.
Bright methane clouds can form around atmospheric disturbances.
Atmospheric storms can change shape, position, and intensity.
Neptune's weather is driven by complex interactions between internal heat and atmospheric circulation.

INTERNAL HEAT
Neptune emits more energy than it receives from sunlight.
The excess energy indicates that Neptune releases significant internal heat.
Internal heat can influence atmospheric circulation.
Heat from the interior may contribute to strong winds and storms.
The exact mechanism transporting internal heat remains an area of research.
Neptune's internal heat is important when comparing it with Uranus.
Both planets are ice giants with similar overall compositions.
Neptune produces substantially more internal energy than Uranus.
Differences in internal structure may help explain their different atmospheric behavior.
Studying internal heat helps scientists understand ice giant evolution.

INTERNAL STRUCTURE
Neptune does not have a solid surface like Earth.
Its atmosphere gradually transitions into deeper fluid layers.
The outer atmosphere contains mostly hydrogen and helium.
Below the atmosphere is a dense mantle rich in volatile compounds.
Water is expected to be an important component of the mantle.
Ammonia and methane are also expected within the deeper interior.
Extreme pressure changes the physical properties of these materials.
A rocky core is expected near Neptune's center.
The core is surrounded by a much larger volatile-rich region.
The exact boundaries between Neptune's interior layers are model-dependent.

ICE GIANT MATERIAL
The word "ice" in ice giant does not mean ordinary surface ice.
Planetary scientists use the term for volatile materials such as water, ammonia, and methane.
These substances can exist in high-pressure and high-temperature states inside Neptune.
The deep interior may contain exotic phases of water.
Superionic water is one proposed high-pressure state.
In superionic water, oxygen atoms form a structure while hydrogen ions move through it.
Such states may influence Neptune's magnetic field.
Laboratory experiments help scientists investigate these extreme conditions.
Computer models combine laboratory data with planetary observations.

MAGNETIC FIELD
Neptune has a global magnetic field.
Its magnetic field is strongly tilted relative to its rotation axis.
The magnetic field is also significantly offset from the planet's center.
This unusual geometry resembles the magnetic configuration of Uranus.
The magnetic field creates a magnetosphere around Neptune.
The magnetosphere interacts continuously with the solar wind.
Solar wind conditions vary as Neptune travels around the Sun.
Charged particles can become trapped or redirected by the magnetic field.
Voyager 2 measured Neptune's magnetic environment during its flyby.
The magnetic field provides clues about electrical conductivity inside Neptune.

RINGS
Neptune has a faint system of planetary rings.
The rings are much darker and less visible than Saturn's rings.
Several distinct ring structures have been identified.
The Adams ring is one of Neptune's best-known rings.
The Adams ring contains bright arcs within the ring system.
Ring arcs are concentrated regions of material rather than uniform complete rings.
Galatea, one of Neptune's moons, helps influence the Adams ring.
Neptune's rings were observed by Voyager 2 in detail.
Earlier observations had detected some ring structures through stellar occultations.

MOONS
Neptune has 16 known moons.
Triton is by far the largest moon of Neptune.
Triton contains most of the mass of Neptune's known satellite system.
The remaining moons are much smaller than Triton.
Many smaller moons have irregular shapes.
Neptune's moons have different orbital distances and orientations.
Several small inner moons orbit within or near the ring system.
Moon interactions can affect ring particles.
Neptune's satellite system provides evidence about the planet's orbital history.
The names of Neptune's moons are largely associated with figures from Greek and Roman mythology.

TRITON
Triton is Neptune's largest moon.
It has a diameter of about 2,700 kilometers.
Triton is slightly smaller than Earth's Moon.
Triton orbits Neptune in a retrograde direction.
A retrograde orbit strongly suggests that Triton was captured rather than formed with Neptune.
Triton is therefore thought to have originated in the outer Solar System.
Its origin may be connected to the Kuiper Belt.
Triton has a young-looking and geologically active surface.
Its surface contains nitrogen, methane, and water ice.
Voyager 2 observed active geyser-like plumes on Triton.

EXPLORATION
Voyager 2 is the only spacecraft to have visited Neptune closely.
Voyager 2 performed its Neptune flyby in August 1989.
It provided the first close observations of Neptune.
The spacecraft studied Neptune's atmosphere and weather systems.
It measured Neptune's magnetic field and charged-particle environment.
Voyager 2 also observed Neptune's rings.
The spacecraft discovered several previously unknown moons.
It obtained detailed images of Triton's surface.
The flyby greatly expanded scientific knowledge of Neptune.
No spacecraft has yet entered orbit around Neptune.

SCIENTIFIC IMPORTANCE
Neptune is a key example of an ice giant.
Its atmosphere demonstrates how planetary weather can operate under weak sunlight.
Its strong winds provide information about atmospheric circulation.
Its internal heat helps scientists investigate energy transport in giant planets.
Its unusual magnetic field provides clues about its interior.
Triton provides an important example of a captured large moon.
Neptune's rings provide a laboratory for studying ring dynamics.
Neptune also helps scientists understand distant planetary systems.
Ice giants are common categories among observed exoplanets.
Understanding Neptune improves models of planets beyond the Solar System.

KEY TERMS
Ice giant: A planet containing large amounts of volatile-rich material such as water, ammonia, and methane.
Astronomical unit: A unit approximately equal to the average Earth-Sun distance.
Snow line: The region where volatile substances can condense into solid material during planet formation.
Magnetosphere: The region around a planet controlled by its magnetic field.
Retrograde orbit: An orbit in the direction opposite to the usual orbital direction of major satellites.
Ring arc: A concentrated segment of material within a planetary ring system.
Internal heat: Energy released from a planet's interior.
Triton: Neptune's largest moon and a likely captured Kuiper Belt object.
Voyager 2: The spacecraft that performed the first and only close flyby of Neptune.
