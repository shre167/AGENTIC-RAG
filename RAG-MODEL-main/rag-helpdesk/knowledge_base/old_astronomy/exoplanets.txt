EXOPLANETS — OVERVIEW
An exoplanet is a planet that orbits a star outside the Solar System.
The word exoplanet is short for extrasolar planet.
Exoplanets can orbit stars similar to or very different from the Sun.
They range from small rocky planets to enormous gas-rich planets.
Some exoplanets orbit multiple stars.
Others orbit stars that are much smaller or larger than the Sun.
The first confirmed exoplanets were discovered around a pulsar in the early 1990s.
Thousands of exoplanets have since been confirmed.
Astronomers have also identified many additional planetary candidates.
Exoplanet research studies planetary formation, evolution, atmospheres, and habitability.
The study of exoplanets connects astronomy with planetary science and astrobiology.

EXOPLANET FORMATION
Exoplanets form from material surrounding young stars.
Young stars can be surrounded by disks of gas and dust.
Planets develop through the accumulation of solid material and gas.
Rocky planets can form primarily from solid material in the inner regions.
Gas-rich planets can form farther from their stars where volatile material is available.
Planet formation is influenced by temperature, density, and chemical composition.
Gravitational interactions can cause planets to migrate from their formation locations.
Collisions between planetary bodies can alter planets during their development.
Some planetary systems contain planets very different from those in the Solar System.
Comparing many systems helps scientists understand how planetary systems form.

PROTOPLANETARY DISKS
A protoplanetary disk is a rotating disk of gas and dust around a young star.
The disk provides the raw material for planets.
Dust grains can collide and stick together during early planet formation.
Larger bodies called planetesimals can develop from accumulated solid material.
Planetesimals can gravitationally interact and form larger planetary embryos.
Planetary embryos can eventually become planets.
Temperature varies with distance from the young star.
The inner disk is generally warmer than the outer disk.
The snow line marks a region where water can condense into solid ice.
Disk chemistry influences the composition of planets that form within it.

PLANET TYPES
Exoplanets can be grouped into broad categories based on size and composition.
Terrestrial exoplanets are generally rocky and relatively small.
Super-Earths are planets larger than Earth but smaller than Neptune in many classification systems.
Mini-Neptunes are planets intermediate in size between Earth and Neptune.
Gas giants are large planets dominated by hydrogen and helium.
Hot Jupiters are gas giants orbiting extremely close to their stars.
Some exoplanets have densities much lower than Earth's.
Planet size alone does not uniquely determine composition.
Mass and radius together can provide information about bulk density.
Planet classification systems can overlap because planetary properties form a continuous range.

ROCKY EXOPLANETS
Rocky exoplanets are composed substantially of silicate rock and metallic material.
Their structures can include a metallic core, rocky mantle, and crust.
Rocky planets can exist at many distances from their host stars.
Their surface temperatures depend on stellar radiation and atmospheric properties.
Some rocky exoplanets are similar in size to Earth.
Earth-sized does not automatically mean Earth-like.
Atmospheric composition strongly affects surface conditions.
Tidal locking can occur for rocky planets close to their stars.
Strong stellar activity can affect the atmospheres of nearby rocky planets.
Rocky exoplanets are important targets in the search for potentially habitable environments.

SUPER-EARTHS
A super-Earth is generally an exoplanet larger or more massive than Earth but smaller than Neptune.
The term describes size or mass rather than confirmed surface conditions.
Super-Earths can have rocky, water-rich, or gas-enveloped compositions.
Their densities can vary significantly.
Some super-Earths orbit very close to their host stars.
Others orbit within regions where temperatures may permit liquid water under suitable conditions.
Their atmospheres can differ greatly depending on formation and evolution.
Higher gravity can affect atmospheric retention.
Studying super-Earths helps scientists understand planets between Earth and Neptune.
The Solar System does not contain a confirmed super-Earth.

HOT JUPITERS
Hot Jupiters are gas giant exoplanets with very short orbital periods.
Many complete an orbit around their stars in only a few days.
Their close distances from their stars produce high atmospheric temperatures.
Strong stellar heating can cause atmospheric expansion.
Some hot Jupiters are tidally locked to their host stars.
One hemisphere can therefore continuously face the star.
Their atmospheres can experience strong winds and temperature differences.
Hot Jupiters were among the earliest exoplanets detected around Sun-like stars.
Their existence challenged simple models of planetary formation.
They demonstrate that planets can migrate significantly after formation.

ORBITAL MIGRATION
Planetary migration occurs when a planet's orbit changes after formation.
Interactions with a protoplanetary disk can alter orbital distance.
Gravitational interactions between planets can also change their orbits.
A planet can move closer to or farther from its host star.
Migration can explain planets found extremely close to their stars.
It can also influence the arrangement of multiple-planet systems.
Migration may occur during the early stages of planetary system evolution.
Resonances can develop when migrating planets interact gravitationally.
Planetary migration is an important part of modern planet-formation models.
The exact migration history of an individual exoplanet may be difficult to determine.

DETECTION METHODS
Astronomers use several techniques to discover and study exoplanets.
The transit method detects a planet when it passes in front of its star.
The radial velocity method detects a star's motion caused by an orbiting planet.
Direct imaging attempts to detect light from a planet itself.
Gravitational microlensing can reveal planets through changes in observed starlight.
Astrometry measures small changes in a star's position caused by planets.
Each method has different strengths and limitations.
Some methods are better for finding certain planet sizes or orbital distances.
Combining detection methods can provide more complete planetary information.
Detection techniques continue to improve as telescopes become more sensitive.

TRANSIT METHOD
The transit method measures a small decrease in a star's brightness.
The decrease occurs when an exoplanet passes across the star's visible disk.
The amount of dimming depends partly on the relative sizes of the planet and star.
Repeated transits reveal the planet's orbital period.
The timing between transits can be used to determine the orbital period.
Transit depth can provide an estimate of planetary radius.
Transits can also allow atmospheric studies through transmission spectroscopy.
The method works best when the planetary orbit is aligned appropriately with Earth's viewpoint.
A planet that does not cross its star from Earth's perspective may not produce detectable transits.
Space telescopes have discovered large numbers of exoplanets using transit observations.

RADIAL VELOCITY METHOD
An orbiting planet exerts a gravitational pull on its host star.
The star responds by moving around the system's center of mass.
This motion produces changes in the star's velocity relative to Earth.
Spectrographs can detect these velocity changes through shifts in spectral lines.
The radial velocity method can estimate a planet's minimum mass.
The strength of the signal depends on planetary mass and orbital properties.
More massive planets generally produce stronger stellar motion.
Planets closer to their stars often produce more easily detectable signals.
Combining radial velocity with transit data can provide planetary density.
Long-term observations can reveal multiple planets orbiting the same star.

DIRECT IMAGING
Direct imaging attempts to observe an exoplanet's light or thermal emission.
A star is usually much brighter than its planets.
Special instruments are therefore needed to suppress starlight.
Direct imaging works best for large planets far from their stars.
Young giant planets can be especially favorable because they emit substantial infrared radiation.
Spectral measurements can reveal information about atmospheric composition.
Direct imaging can provide information that transit and radial velocity methods cannot directly provide.
The technique is technically challenging because of the contrast between stars and planets.
Coronagraphs can block or reduce the light from a host star.
Future observatories may expand direct imaging capabilities.

GRAVITATIONAL MICROLENSING
Gravitational microlensing uses the bending of light by gravity.
A foreground star can temporarily magnify the light of a more distant background star.
A planet orbiting the foreground star can create an additional short-lived signal.
This signal can reveal the presence of the planet.
Microlensing can detect planets at relatively large orbital distances.
It can also detect planets that are difficult to observe using transit methods.
Microlensing events usually occur only once for a particular alignment.
Follow-up observations may therefore be difficult.
The technique is useful for studying planetary populations at large distances.
It can detect planets that are not necessarily closely bound to bright nearby stars.

EXOPLANET ATMOSPHERES
Some exoplanet atmospheres can be studied using spectroscopy.
Spectroscopy measures how matter absorbs or emits different wavelengths of light.
During a transit, starlight can pass through the planet's atmosphere.
Molecules in the atmosphere can absorb specific wavelengths.
These absorption features can provide clues about atmospheric composition.
Possible atmospheric constituents include water vapor, carbon dioxide, methane, sodium, and other molecules.
Clouds and hazes can make atmospheric signals harder to detect.
Temperature affects atmospheric chemistry and circulation.
Stellar radiation can drive atmospheric escape.
Atmospheric observations help scientists compare exoplanets with Solar System planets.

TRANSMISSION SPECTROSCOPY
Transmission spectroscopy studies starlight filtered through an exoplanet's atmosphere.
The planet blocks slightly different amounts of light at different wavelengths.
Atmospheric molecules can create wavelength-dependent absorption features.
The resulting spectrum can provide clues about atmospheric composition.
The strength of the signal depends on atmospheric scale height and planetary properties.
Large, low-density planets can produce relatively strong atmospheric signals.
Clouds can reduce or obscure molecular absorption features.
Observations often require combining data from many transit events.
Space telescopes are especially useful because Earth's atmosphere can interfere with measurements.
Transmission spectroscopy is an important method for atmospheric characterization.

HABITABLE ZONE
The habitable zone is the region around a star where liquid water could exist on a planet's surface under suitable atmospheric conditions.
It is sometimes called the circumstellar habitable zone.
The location of the habitable zone depends strongly on stellar luminosity.
Hotter stars generally have habitable zones farther from the star.
Cooler stars generally have habitable zones closer to the star.
Being inside the habitable zone does not prove that a planet is habitable.
Atmospheric composition and pressure strongly affect surface temperature.
Planetary geology and water availability also influence habitability.
Tidal effects can be important for planets orbiting very close to small stars.
Habitability therefore depends on many interacting physical and chemical factors.

STELLAR ACTIVITY
A host star can strongly influence the environment around an exoplanet.
Stars emit radiation across a broad range of wavelengths.
Some stars produce strong ultraviolet and X-ray radiation.
Stellar flares can temporarily increase high-energy radiation.
Strong stellar activity can heat or erode planetary upper atmospheres.
Charged particles from stellar winds can interact with planetary magnetic fields.
Atmospheric loss can change the long-term habitability of a planet.
Young stars are often more active than older stars.
Red dwarf stars can produce frequent flares despite their lower luminosity.
Understanding the host star is essential when evaluating an exoplanet's environment.

TIDAL LOCKING
Tidal locking occurs when an object's rotation becomes synchronized with its orbital period.
A tidally locked planet can keep the same hemisphere facing its star.
This is more likely for planets orbiting very close to their stars.
The Moon is tidally locked to Earth.
Tidal locking does not automatically make an exoplanet uninhabitable.
Atmospheric circulation could transport heat between the permanent day and night sides.
The climate of a tidally locked planet depends on atmosphere, oceans, and surface properties.
Some planets may reach partial or other spin-orbit resonances.
Tidal effects can also heat planetary interiors.
Tidal evolution is important in compact planetary systems.

PLANETARY DENSITY
Planetary density can be estimated when both mass and radius are known.
Density provides clues about a planet's bulk composition.
A high-density planet is more likely to contain substantial rocky or metallic material.
A low-density planet may contain a large fraction of volatile material or a thick atmosphere.
Density alone cannot determine detailed internal structure.
Transit observations can provide planetary radius.
Radial velocity measurements can provide mass estimates.
Combining the two measurements allows approximate bulk density calculation.
Comparisons with Solar System planets provide useful reference points.
Density measurements are especially valuable for planets between Earth and Neptune in size.

MULTIPLE-PLANET SYSTEMS
Many stars host more than one known exoplanet.
Multiple planets can interact through mutual gravity.
Orbital resonances can occur when planetary periods have simple ratios.
Transit timing variations can reveal gravitational interactions between planets.
Planetary spacing affects long-term orbital stability.
Compact systems can contain several planets orbiting close to their host star.
The architecture of a planetary system provides clues about its formation.
Comparing multiple-planet systems with the Solar System reveals diverse outcomes.
Some systems have planets in orbital configurations not represented in our Solar System.
Multiple-planet observations improve models of planetary dynamics.

EXOPLANETS AND ASTROBIOLOGY
Astrobiology studies the possibility of life beyond Earth.
Exoplanets provide many potential environments for astrobiological investigation.
Scientists consider liquid water, energy sources, and suitable chemistry when evaluating habitability.
A planet's atmosphere can provide important clues about environmental conditions.
Potential biosignatures are chemical or physical indicators that could be associated with biological activity.
A single atmospheric molecule is generally not enough to establish life.
False positives can arise from non-biological chemical processes.
Stellar activity can also affect atmospheric chemistry.
Future observations may improve the search for atmospheric biosignatures.
The search for life on exoplanets requires multiple independent lines of evidence.

EXOPLANET EXPLORATION
Space telescopes have played a major role in exoplanet discovery.
The Kepler mission identified thousands of planetary candidates and confirmed many exoplanets.
The Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite surveys bright nearby stars for transiting planets.
The James Webb Space Telescope can study selected exoplanet atmospheres.
Ground-based observatories provide complementary radial velocity and imaging observations.
Different observatories are designed for different aspects of exoplanet research.
Combining observations from multiple instruments improves confidence in planetary properties.
Future missions may directly image some potentially Earth-like planets.
Improved instruments will expand the range of detectable exoplanets.
Exoplanet research is moving from discovery toward detailed characterization.

KEY TERMS
Exoplanet: A planet orbiting a star outside the Solar System.
Transit: The passage of an exoplanet across the visible disk of its host star.
Radial velocity: The component of a star's motion measured along the line of sight.
Super-Earth: An exoplanet larger or more massive than Earth but smaller than Neptune in common usage.
Hot Jupiter: A gas giant exoplanet with a very short orbit close to its star.
Habitable zone: The region around a star where liquid water could exist under suitable conditions.
Atmospheric spectroscopy: Analysis of wavelength-dependent light to study atmospheric composition.
Transmission spectroscopy: Study of starlight passing through an exoplanet's atmosphere.
Tidal locking: Synchronized rotation in which an object keeps the same side facing its orbital companion.
Biosignature: A possible indicator of biological activity that requires careful confirmation.
