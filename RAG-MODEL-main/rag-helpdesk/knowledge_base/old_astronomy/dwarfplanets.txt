DWARF PLANETS — OVERVIEW
A dwarf planet is a celestial body that orbits the Sun.
It has enough mass for its gravity to make it approximately round.
It has not cleared the neighborhood around its orbit.
It is not a satellite of another body.
The International Astronomical Union established this definition in 2006.
Dwarf planets are different from both planets and smaller Solar System bodies.
They can be found in different regions of the Solar System.
Several known dwarf planets orbit beyond Neptune.
Ceres is the only officially recognized dwarf planet in the inner Solar System.
The classification of distant objects can sometimes remain uncertain.
The number of possible dwarf planets is therefore larger than the confirmed group.

PLANETS AND DWARF PLANETS
Planets and dwarf planets both orbit the Sun.
Both can have enough gravity to become approximately round.
The main difference is orbital dominance.
A planet has cleared the neighborhood around its orbit.
A dwarf planet shares its orbital region with other objects.
Clearing an orbit does not mean removing every object from that region.
It refers to gravitational dominance over the surrounding orbital zone.
Earth, Mars, Jupiter, and Neptune are examples of planets.
Ceres and Pluto are examples of dwarf planets.
The distinction is based on orbital dynamics rather than simply size.

DWARF PLANET CLASSIFICATION
The dwarf planet category was introduced to describe bodies between planets and smaller objects.
Classification depends on orbital and physical characteristics.
An object must orbit the Sun to qualify.
It must not be a moon of another body.
Its own gravity must be strong enough to make it approximately round.
It must not have cleared its orbital neighborhood.
The category includes objects with very different sizes and compositions.
Some dwarf planets contain substantial amounts of ice.
Others contain significant rock and possibly metallic material.
Scientific classification can change when new observations improve our understanding.

KNOWN DWARF PLANETS
Ceres is the largest object in the main asteroid belt and a dwarf planet.
Pluto is a dwarf planet in the Kuiper Belt.
Eris is a large dwarf planet in the distant outer Solar System.
Haumea is a rapidly rotating dwarf planet beyond Neptune.
Makemake is a large icy body in the Kuiper Belt.
These five are commonly recognized as dwarf planets.
Other distant objects are considered dwarf planet candidates.
The physical properties of many distant objects are difficult to measure directly.
Diameter, mass, shape, and composition help scientists evaluate classification.
New observations can improve estimates of distant object properties.

CERES
Ceres is located in the main asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter.
It has a diameter of roughly 940 kilometers.
Ceres is the largest object in the asteroid belt.
It contains a mixture of rock and volatile-rich material.
Its gravity is strong enough to make it approximately spherical.
Water ice is believed to occur beneath its surface.
Ceres has many impact craters.
Occator Crater contains unusually bright deposits.
The bright deposits contain abundant salts.
NASA's Dawn spacecraft orbited Ceres and studied its surface and interior.

PLUTO
Pluto is a dwarf planet in the Kuiper Belt.
It was discovered in 1930 by Clyde Tombaugh.
Pluto was classified as a planet until the definition changed in 2006.
Its surface contains nitrogen, methane, and carbon monoxide ices.
Pluto has a complex surface with mountains, plains, glaciers, and craters.
The heart-shaped Tombaugh Regio is one of its most recognizable features.
Sputnik Planitia is a large nitrogen-ice-covered basin within Tombaugh Regio.
Pluto has a thin atmosphere that changes with its distance from the Sun.
The atmosphere contains nitrogen with smaller amounts of methane and carbon monoxide.
NASA's New Horizons spacecraft performed a close flyby of Pluto in 2015.

PLUTO'S ORBIT
Pluto follows an elliptical orbit around the Sun.
Its average distance from the Sun is about 39 astronomical units.
One Pluto year lasts about 248 Earth years.
Its orbit is more eccentric than the orbits of the eight planets.
Pluto's orbit is also significantly inclined relative to the main planetary orbital plane.
For part of its orbit, Pluto is closer to the Sun than Neptune.
This does not result in a collision between Pluto and Neptune.
Their orbital configuration is protected by a stable resonance.
Pluto completes two orbits for approximately every three Neptune orbits.
This relationship is known as a 2:3 orbital resonance.

PLUTO'S MOONS
Pluto has five known moons.
Charon is the largest and most massive of Pluto's moons.
Styx, Nix, Kerberos, and Hydra are the smaller moons.
Charon is unusually large compared with Pluto.
The Pluto-Charon system is often described as a binary planetary system.
Pluto and Charon orbit a common center of mass outside Pluto's center.
Charon is tidally locked with Pluto.
Pluto is also tidally locked with Charon.
The smaller moons orbit farther from the Pluto-Charon pair.
New Horizons studied Pluto and several of its moons during its flyby.

ERIS
Eris is a dwarf planet in the distant outer Solar System.
It is located in the scattered disk region beyond the Kuiper Belt.
Eris is similar in size to Pluto.
Its discovery contributed to the debate that led to the 2006 planet definition.
Eris has a highly elliptical orbit around the Sun.
Its orbital period is roughly 558 Earth years.
Eris is known to have one moon called Dysnomia.
Its surface is extremely cold because of its great distance from the Sun.
Methane ice has been detected on its surface.
Eris is important for studying distant icy bodies and dwarf planet formation.

HAUMEA
Haumea is a dwarf planet beyond Neptune.
It has an unusually elongated shape.
Its elongated shape is related to its very rapid rotation.
Haumea completes one rotation in less than four hours.
It is one of the fastest-rotating large bodies in the Solar System.
Its surface is dominated by water ice.
Haumea has two known moons.
It also has a narrow ring system.
Its rapid rotation and unusual shape make it scientifically distinctive.
Haumea's properties provide clues about collisions in the outer Solar System.

MAKEMAKE
Makemake is a dwarf planet in the Kuiper Belt.
It is one of the largest known Kuiper Belt objects.
Makemake has a diameter of roughly 1,400 kilometers.
Its surface is extremely cold and rich in volatile ices.
Methane ice is present on its surface.
Ethane and other hydrocarbons can also occur.
Makemake has a very thin atmosphere that may vary with its orbital position.
It has one known moon.
The moon is known as MK2 or S/2015 (136472) 1 in scientific designations.
Makemake helps scientists study the physical and chemical properties of Kuiper Belt objects.

DWARF PLANET SURFACES
Dwarf planet surfaces can contain large impact craters.
Ices can dominate the surfaces of distant dwarf planets.
Nitrogen ice is important on Pluto.
Methane ice occurs on several distant outer Solar System bodies.
Water ice is particularly important on Haumea.
Ceres has a rock-rich surface with hydrated minerals and salts.
Surface temperatures are generally very low on distant dwarf planets.
Seasonal heating can cause some surface ices to move or vaporize.
Impacts can expose material from beneath the surface.
Surface observations help scientists reconstruct geological histories.

DWARF PLANET INTERIORS
Dwarf planets can contain mixtures of rock and ice.
Their internal structures depend on size, composition, and thermal history.
Larger bodies can retain internal heat for longer periods.
Radioactive elements can provide a source of internal heating.
Water-rich interiors may have experienced differentiation.
Differentiation occurs when materials separate according to density.
Dense rocky material can move toward the center.
Less dense icy material can occupy outer regions.
Some dwarf planets may have had subsurface liquid water in the past.
Interior properties are often inferred from mass, size, density, and surface evidence.

KUIPER BELT DWARF PLANETS
The Kuiper Belt is a region beyond Neptune containing many icy objects.
Several large Kuiper Belt objects are classified as dwarf planets.
Pluto, Haumea, and Makemake are associated with the Kuiper Belt.
Eris is generally associated with the more distant scattered disk.
The Kuiper Belt contains objects with a wide range of sizes.
Many objects contain mixtures of rock and volatile ices.
Collisions and gravitational interactions shaped the region.
Neptune strongly influences the orbital dynamics of many Kuiper Belt objects.
Orbital resonances occur between some Kuiper Belt objects and Neptune.
The region preserves material from the early outer Solar System.

DWARF PLANETS AND ORBITAL DYNAMICS
Dwarf planets do not gravitationally dominate their orbital neighborhoods.
Their orbital regions contain many other small bodies.
Resonances can stabilize or modify their orbital paths.
Gravitational interactions can change orbital eccentricity and inclination.
Some dwarf planets have highly inclined or elongated orbits.
Orbital dynamics help explain the different populations of distant objects.
Long-term simulations are used to study their orbital evolution.
The gravitational influence of Neptune is particularly important beyond its orbit.
Close encounters can alter the trajectories of smaller bodies.
Orbital dynamics are central to understanding why dwarf planets differ from planets.

EXPLORATION
Ceres was explored extensively by NASA's Dawn spacecraft.
Dawn became the first spacecraft to orbit two extraterrestrial bodies.
It orbited Vesta before reaching Ceres.
New Horizons performed the first close flyby of Pluto.
The spacecraft reached Pluto in July 2015.
New Horizons obtained detailed images of Pluto's surface.
It studied Pluto's atmosphere, geology, composition, and moons.
Distant dwarf planets have not yet received dedicated close-up spacecraft missions.
Most information about Eris, Haumea, and Makemake comes from telescopic observations.
Future missions could provide direct measurements of distant dwarf planets.

SCIENTIFIC IMPORTANCE
Dwarf planets help scientists understand how planetary systems form.
They preserve material from regions where full-sized planets did not form.
Their surfaces can retain evidence of ancient processes.
Their orbits provide information about Solar System dynamics.
Their compositions reveal differences between formation environments.
Ceres provides information about water-rich bodies in the inner Solar System.
Pluto provides detailed evidence about the Kuiper Belt.
Eris helped establish the modern distinction between planets and dwarf planets.
Haumea provides evidence of rapid rotation and past collisions.
Makemake provides another example of a large icy Kuiper Belt body.

KEY TERMS
Dwarf planet: A round, Sun-orbiting body that has not cleared its orbital neighborhood.
Orbital neighborhood: The region around an object's orbit in which gravitational dominance is considered.
Kuiper Belt: A distant region beyond Neptune containing many icy Solar System bodies.
Scattered disk: A distant population of objects with dynamically excited orbits beyond Neptune.
Orbital resonance: A gravitational relationship involving simple ratios between orbital periods.
Tidal locking: A state in which an object's rotation period matches its orbital period around another body.
Differentiation: Separation of planetary materials into layers based on density and physical properties.
Ceres: The largest object in the main asteroid belt and a dwarf planet.
Pluto: A dwarf planet and major Kuiper Belt object.
Eris: A distant dwarf planet whose discovery contributed to the redefinition of planet classification.
