ASTEROIDS — OVERVIEW
Asteroids are small rocky or metallic bodies that orbit the Sun.
Most asteroids are much smaller than planets.
Asteroids are remnants of material left over from the formation of the Solar System.
They are classified as small Solar System bodies.
Most known asteroids are concentrated in the main asteroid belt.
The main asteroid belt lies between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter.
Asteroids can have irregular, spherical, elongated, or highly fractured shapes.
Their surfaces can contain rocks, dust, impact craters, and regolith.
Asteroids vary greatly in size, composition, brightness, and orbital properties.
Studying asteroids provides information about the early Solar System.

FORMATION
Asteroids formed from material present in the protoplanetary disk.
The protoplanetary disk surrounded the young Sun.
Small solid particles collided and gradually accumulated during Solar System formation.
Gravitational interactions influenced how these bodies grew and moved.
In the asteroid belt, Jupiter's gravity prevented many objects from forming a single planet.
Collisions repeatedly fragmented larger bodies.
Some asteroids are therefore fragments of older parent bodies.
Other asteroids may preserve relatively primitive material.
Asteroids can retain chemical information from the early Solar System.
Their compositions help scientists investigate planetary formation.
Asteroid formation and evolution occurred over billions of years.

MAIN ASTEROID BELT
The main asteroid belt is located between Mars and Jupiter.
It extends roughly from 2.1 to 3.3 astronomical units from the Sun.
The asteroid belt is not a densely packed region of rocks.
Large distances separate most individual asteroids.
A spacecraft can travel through the asteroid belt without continuously encountering objects.
The total mass of the main asteroid belt is much less than Earth's mass.
Ceres is the largest object in the main asteroid belt.
Many smaller asteroids orbit throughout the region.
Orbital resonances with Jupiter create gaps in the asteroid belt.
These resonance gaps are known as Kirkwood gaps.
The asteroid belt contains diverse populations with different compositions.

ASTEROID SIZE
Asteroids range from tiny fragments to bodies hundreds of kilometers across.
The largest asteroids are large enough to have approximately rounded shapes.
Smaller asteroids are often irregular because their gravity is weaker.
Ceres has a diameter of about 940 kilometers.
Vesta has a diameter of about 525 kilometers.
Many asteroids are only a few kilometers or less across.
Asteroid size affects gravity, surface shape, and geological evolution.
Larger asteroids can retain more internal heat.
Smaller asteroids generally cool more rapidly after formation.
Asteroid sizes are commonly estimated using observations and physical models.

COMPOSITION
Asteroids are made from different combinations of rock, metal, and carbon-rich material.
Their compositions reflect different formation environments and histories.
Some asteroids contain large amounts of silicate minerals.
Some contain significant metallic iron and nickel.
Carbon-rich asteroids contain abundant carbon-bearing compounds.
Water-bearing minerals occur on some asteroid surfaces.
Asteroids can contain hydrated minerals formed through chemical alteration.
Spectroscopy helps scientists identify minerals on asteroid surfaces.
Reflectance spectra can be used to classify asteroid types.
Composition can vary between different regions of the same asteroid.

ASTEROID TYPES
Asteroids are commonly grouped into broad spectral classes.
C-type asteroids are generally carbon-rich and relatively dark.
S-type asteroids are generally rich in silicate minerals.
M-type asteroids are associated with metallic compositions.
Spectral classification is based partly on how asteroid surfaces reflect light.
Classification does not completely describe an asteroid's internal composition.
Spacecraft observations can provide more detailed information than remote spectroscopy.
Some asteroids do not fit neatly into a single traditional class.
Different asteroid populations are more common at different distances from the Sun.
Space weathering can alter the appearance of asteroid surfaces.
Taxonomic systems continue to be refined as observations improve.

ORBITAL PROPERTIES
Asteroids orbit the Sun according to the laws of celestial mechanics.
Their orbits can be nearly circular or significantly elliptical.
Asteroid orbital inclination can vary widely.
Some asteroids have orbits that cross the paths of planets.
Gravitational encounters can change asteroid orbits over time.
Jupiter strongly influences the orbital evolution of many asteroids.
Planetary resonances can increase or decrease orbital stability.
Some asteroids become temporarily trapped in unusual orbital configurations.
Orbital calculations are used to predict future asteroid positions.
Long-term orbital prediction becomes more difficult when repeated gravitational encounters occur.

NEAR-EARTH ASTEROIDS
Near-Earth asteroids are asteroids whose orbits bring them relatively close to Earth's orbit.
Their orbital paths can cross or approach Earth's orbital region.
Near-Earth objects are monitored because some could potentially impact Earth.
Most near-Earth asteroids do not pose an immediate threat.
Astronomers use repeated observations to improve their orbital calculations.
Radar observations can provide precise information about some nearby asteroids.
Space-based and ground-based telescopes contribute to asteroid surveys.
Impact probability depends on an object's orbit, size, and future trajectory.
New observations can change calculated impact probabilities.
Monitoring improves the ability to identify potentially hazardous objects.

POTENTIALLY HAZARDOUS ASTEROIDS
A potentially hazardous asteroid is defined using orbital and size-related criteria.
Its orbit must come sufficiently close to Earth's orbital region.
It must also be large enough to be of concern if an impact occurred.
Potentially hazardous does not mean that an asteroid will hit Earth.
The designation identifies objects that deserve continued monitoring.
Astronomers calculate their future trajectories using orbital observations.
Impact risk can decrease as additional observations reduce orbital uncertainty.
Some potentially hazardous asteroids have been studied by spacecraft.
Planetary defense programs maintain catalogs of relevant near-Earth objects.
Long-term monitoring is important because asteroid orbits can evolve.

ASTEROID COLLISIONS
Asteroids frequently experience collisions over Solar System timescales.
Collisions can create craters on asteroid surfaces.
High-energy impacts can fragment an asteroid into smaller bodies.
Large collisions can produce families of asteroids with related orbits.
Asteroid families may originate from a common parent body.
Fragments from a collision can spread along similar orbital paths.
Some asteroid families have distinctive compositions.
Impact events can expose material from deeper inside an asteroid.
Collisions contribute to the continual evolution of the asteroid population.
The asteroid belt therefore contains evidence of a long history of impacts.

REGOLITH AND SURFACES
Regolith is a layer of loose material covering many asteroid surfaces.
It can contain dust, rock fragments, and impact debris.
Repeated impacts can break surface material into smaller particles.
Low gravity allows some impact-generated material to escape an asteroid.
Electrostatic processes can also move fine surface dust.
Asteroid surfaces can have large boulders and smooth regions.
Impact craters are common on many well-observed asteroids.
Some asteroids have grooves, ridges, fractures, and large depressions.
Surface properties vary strongly between different asteroid types.
Spacecraft measurements help scientists understand how asteroid surfaces behave.

ROTATION
Asteroids rotate around their own axes.
Rotation periods range from minutes to many hours or longer.
Irregular shapes can produce complex rotational behavior.
Very rapid rotation can cause loose surface material to move or escape.
Some asteroids rotate in a stable state.
Others can tumble rather than rotate around a single fixed axis.
Tumbling motion is sometimes called non-principal-axis rotation.
Collisions and gravitational effects can alter asteroid rotation.
Measurements of rotation help scientists understand asteroid structure.
Light curves are commonly used to estimate asteroid rotation periods.

LIGHT CURVES
An asteroid's brightness changes as it rotates.
These brightness changes produce a pattern called a light curve.
An irregular shape can cause periodic brightness variations.
Light curves can help determine an asteroid's rotation period.
They can also provide information about its approximate shape.
Repeated observations improve the reliability of light-curve analysis.
Brightness changes can also occur because different surface regions reflect different amounts of sunlight.
Combining light curves with other observations improves shape models.
Astronomers use photometry to measure asteroid brightness.
Light-curve analysis is an important remote-sensing technique.

ASTEROID MOONS
Some asteroids have small natural satellites.
Asteroid moons can form after collisions or through gravitational capture.
The presence of a moon can help scientists estimate an asteroid's mass.
Orbital measurements of a moon can reveal the gravity of its parent asteroid.
Some asteroid systems contain two or more bodies of comparable size.
Binary and multiple asteroid systems are scientifically valuable.
The gravitational environment around small bodies differs greatly from that around planets.
Asteroid moons can help determine internal density.
Spacecraft have visited several binary asteroid systems.
Studying these systems improves models of asteroid formation and evolution.

CERES
Ceres is the largest object in the main asteroid belt.
It is classified as a dwarf planet.
Ceres has a diameter of roughly 940 kilometers.
It contains a mixture of rock and volatile-rich material.
Water ice is believed to occur beneath its surface.
Ceres has a complex and cratered surface.
Bright deposits are visible inside some impact craters.
The bright material in Occator Crater contains abundant salts.
NASA's Dawn spacecraft orbited Ceres and studied its surface and interior.
Ceres demonstrates the transition between small bodies and fully developed planets.

VESTA
Vesta is one of the largest bodies in the main asteroid belt.
It has a diameter of roughly 525 kilometers.
Vesta is differentiated into crust, mantle, and core-like layers.
Its surface contains extensive impact features.
The enormous Rheasilvia basin covers a large region near its south pole.
Vesta's composition is related to some meteorites found on Earth.
NASA's Dawn spacecraft visited Vesta before traveling to Ceres.
Dawn mapped Vesta's surface and studied its composition.
Vesta provides evidence of substantial geological processing in an asteroid.
Its history helps scientists study early planetary differentiation.

SPACECRAFT EXPLORATION
Spacecraft have visited a number of asteroids.
Close spacecraft observations reveal surface features unavailable from Earth.
NASA's NEAR Shoemaker orbited and landed on asteroid Eros.
Japan's Hayabusa mission returned samples from asteroid Itokawa.
Japan's Hayabusa2 mission returned samples from asteroid Ryugu.
NASA's OSIRIS-REx collected samples from asteroid Bennu.
The returned samples allow laboratory analysis of asteroid material.
NASA's Dawn mission explored Vesta and Ceres.
NASA's Lucy mission is designed to study Trojan asteroids of Jupiter.
Asteroid missions improve understanding of composition, geology, and evolution.

ASTEROID SAMPLES
Sample-return missions provide direct physical material from asteroids.
Laboratories can analyze asteroid minerals at very high precision.
Samples can reveal isotopes and chemical compounds.
Some asteroid samples contain evidence of water-related alteration.
Organic molecules have been identified in some carbon-rich asteroid materials.
Sample analysis can test models developed from remote observations.
Returned material can be preserved for future scientific techniques.
Asteroid samples help connect meteorites with their parent bodies.
Laboratory measurements can reveal information unavailable through telescopes alone.
Sample-return missions are therefore important tools in planetary science.

SCIENTIFIC IMPORTANCE
Asteroids preserve evidence from the early stages of Solar System formation.
Their compositions reveal differences between formation environments.
Asteroid collisions record the history of planetary system evolution.
Near-Earth asteroids are important for planetary defense.
Asteroids may contain water and carbon-bearing materials.
These resources are relevant to future space exploration studies.
Asteroid surfaces provide natural laboratories for low-gravity geology.
Asteroid missions test technologies for navigation and autonomous operations.
Studying asteroids also improves understanding of meteorites found on Earth.
Asteroids connect planetary science, astronomy, geology, chemistry, and space engineering.

KEY TERMS
Asteroid: A small rocky or metallic body orbiting the Sun.
Main asteroid belt: The primary concentration of known asteroids between Mars and Jupiter.
Near-Earth asteroid: An asteroid whose orbit brings it close to Earth's orbital region.
Regolith: Loose dust and rock material covering a planetary or asteroid surface.
Spectroscopy: The study of how matter interacts with electromagnetic radiation.
Light curve: A record of an object's brightness as a function of time.
Kirkwood gap: A region of reduced asteroid density caused by orbital resonances with Jupiter.
Asteroid family: A group of asteroids believed to originate from a common parent body.
Potentially hazardous asteroid: A near-Earth asteroid meeting specified orbital and size criteria.
Dwarf planet: A planetary-mass body that orbits the Sun but has not cleared its orbital neighborhood.
