COSMOLOGY
Cosmology is the scientific study of the origin, evolution, structure, and large-scale properties of the universe.
Modern cosmology combines observations with theories from physics and astronomy.
The observable universe contains galaxies, stars, gas, dust, dark matter, radiation, and other forms of matter and energy.
The universe is approximately 13.8 billion years old according to current measurements.
The observable universe is larger than 13.8 billion light-years in radius because space has expanded while light was traveling.
The observable universe is the region from which light or other signals have had time to reach us.
The entire universe may be much larger than the observable universe.
Whether the entire universe is finite or infinite is not currently known.
Cosmology studies the universe on scales much larger than individual galaxies.
The standard cosmological model is commonly called Lambda-CDM.

THE BIG BANG
The Big Bang model describes the universe evolving from an extremely hot, dense early state.
The model does not describe an explosion occurring at a single location in pre-existing space.
Instead, the expansion involves space itself changing with time.
The early universe was much hotter and denser than it is today.
As the universe expanded, its average temperature decreased.
Matter eventually formed atoms after the universe cooled sufficiently.
Later, gravity caused matter to gather into stars, galaxies, and larger structures.
The Big Bang model is supported by several independent observations.
These include cosmic expansion, the cosmic microwave background, and the observed abundance of light elements.
The model describes the evolution of the observable universe but does not by itself explain every question about the ultimate beginning.

COSMIC EXPANSION
The universe is expanding on large scales.
Distant galaxies generally show redshifted spectral lines.
The relationship between galaxy distance and recession velocity is described approximately by Hubble-Lemaître's law.
Hubble-Lemaître's law can be written approximately as v = H0d for sufficiently nearby galaxies.
H0 represents the present-day Hubble constant.
d represents distance and v represents recession velocity in the simplified relation.
Cosmic expansion does not mean galaxies are necessarily moving through static space away from a central point.
On sufficiently large scales, the distances between gravitationally unbound galaxies increase as space expands.
Bound systems such as galaxies and solar systems do not expand in the same way.
The expansion rate has changed throughout cosmic history.

HUBBLE CONSTANT
The Hubble constant describes the current rate of cosmic expansion.
It is commonly expressed in kilometers per second per megaparsec.
Measurements of the Hubble constant can produce different values depending on the observational method.
Local distance-ladder measurements generally infer a higher expansion rate than some early-universe measurements.
This discrepancy is known as the Hubble tension.
The Hubble tension remains an active area of cosmological research.
Possible explanations include systematic measurement effects or new physics.
The Hubble constant is a fundamental parameter in modern cosmology.
Accurate distance measurements are important for determining the expansion rate.
The expansion rate also affects estimates of cosmic distances and ages.
Different cosmological parameters are strongly connected in cosmological models.

COSMIC MICROWAVE BACKGROUND
The cosmic microwave background is electromagnetic radiation left over from the early universe.
It was released when the universe became cool enough for electrons and nuclei to combine into neutral atoms.
This transition is called recombination.
The cosmic microwave background was emitted roughly 380,000 years after the Big Bang.
The radiation has been stretched by cosmic expansion into microwave wavelengths.
Its present temperature is approximately 2.7 kelvin.
The CMB is observed across the entire sky with remarkable uniformity.
Tiny temperature fluctuations in the CMB contain information about early density variations.
These fluctuations later grew through gravity into galaxies and larger structures.
The CMB is one of the strongest observational foundations of modern cosmology.

CMB ANISOTROPIES
CMB anisotropies are small variations in the temperature of the cosmic microwave background.
These variations are typically measured at the level of tens of microkelvin.
They reflect differences in density and gravitational potential in the early universe.
The angular pattern of CMB fluctuations contains information about cosmological parameters.
Features in the CMB power spectrum reveal information about matter, radiation, and geometry.
The positions of acoustic peaks provide constraints on the composition and evolution of the universe.
CMB polarization provides additional information about early-universe processes.
Space missions such as COBE, WMAP, and Planck produced detailed CMB measurements.
CMB observations strongly constrain the standard cosmological model.
Studying the CMB allows astronomers to observe conditions from the early universe directly through ancient radiation.

RECOMBINATION
Recombination occurred when free electrons combined with atomic nuclei to form mostly neutral atoms.
Before recombination, photons interacted frequently with free charged particles.
This interaction kept the early universe opaque to electromagnetic radiation.
After recombination, photons could travel much more freely through space.
The radiation from this transition is observed today as the cosmic microwave background.
Recombination occurred when the universe was roughly 380,000 years old.
The term recombination is historical because electrons and nuclei were combining for the first time in the early universe.
The transition changed the interaction between matter and radiation.
Small density variations present at this time became important seeds for later structure formation.
Recombination is therefore an important boundary between the early opaque universe and the later transparent universe.

EARLY UNIVERSE
The early universe contained a hot plasma of particles and radiation.
At very early times, temperatures were high enough for many particle species to exist.
As expansion continued, the universe cooled and particle interactions changed.
Quarks eventually became confined into composite particles such as protons and neutrons.
The early universe then entered an era in which light nuclei could form.
Later, neutral atoms formed during recombination.
The exact physics of the earliest moments is not completely understood.
General relativity successfully describes cosmic expansion over much of the universe's history.
Quantum gravity may be required to describe the earliest extreme conditions.
Cosmological observations provide indirect constraints on early-universe physics.

INFLATION
Inflation is a proposed period of extremely rapid expansion in the very early universe.
Inflation can explain why widely separated regions of the observable universe have similar large-scale properties.
It can also explain why the observable universe appears close to spatially flat.
Quantum fluctuations during inflation can become seeds for later cosmic structure.
These fluctuations can be stretched to enormous scales during rapid expansion.
Inflation is supported indirectly by several features of observed cosmological structure.
The specific physical mechanism responsible for inflation has not been established.
Different inflationary models propose different fields and potential energy landscapes.
Inflation remains an important theoretical framework in early-universe cosmology.
Direct confirmation of a specific inflationary model has not yet been achieved.

PRIMORDIAL NUCLEOSYNTHESIS
Big Bang nucleosynthesis describes the formation of light atomic nuclei in the early universe.
It occurred during the first few minutes after the Big Bang.
Protons and neutrons combined to form nuclei such as deuterium, helium, and small amounts of lithium.
Most ordinary matter by mass was initially composed mainly of hydrogen and helium.
The predicted abundance of light elements depends on conditions in the early universe.
Observed abundances of hydrogen and helium broadly support the Big Bang model.
The measured abundance of deuterium is particularly useful for constraining cosmological parameters.
Lithium observations show some unresolved differences with simple theoretical predictions.
Big Bang nucleosynthesis is one of the major tests of early-universe cosmology.
It connects nuclear physics with the history of cosmic expansion.

MATTER AND ANTIMATTER
Matter consists of particles such as protons, neutrons, and electrons.
Antimatter contains corresponding antiparticles with opposite quantum charges.
Matter and antimatter can annihilate when appropriate particles and antiparticles interact.
The observable universe contains far more matter than antimatter.
This imbalance is called the matter-antimatter asymmetry.
The physical process that generated this asymmetry is not completely understood.
Baryogenesis refers broadly to mechanisms that can produce an excess of baryons over antibaryons.
Conditions required for generating a matter-antimatter asymmetry are studied in particle physics and cosmology.
The observed asymmetry is an important unresolved problem in fundamental physics.
Understanding baryogenesis could explain why ordinary matter exists in large quantities today.

DARK MATTER
Dark matter is a form of matter inferred primarily through gravitational effects.
It does not emit, absorb, or reflect electromagnetic radiation strongly enough to be directly seen.
Galaxy rotation provides important evidence for dark matter.
Gravitational lensing provides additional evidence for its presence.
Galaxy clusters contain large amounts of dark matter.
Dark matter helps explain how cosmic structures form and grow.
Cold dark matter describes particles that moved relatively slowly when important structures formed.
The Lambda-CDM model assumes cold dark matter.
The identity of the dark-matter particle has not been established.
Possible dark-matter candidates include several hypothetical particle types.

DARK ENERGY
Dark energy is the name given to whatever causes the observed accelerated expansion of the universe.
It accounts for a large fraction of the universe's present energy budget in the standard cosmological model.
The simplest model of dark energy is a cosmological constant represented by the symbol Lambda.
In Lambda-CDM, Lambda represents dark energy and CDM represents cold dark matter.
Dark energy differs conceptually from dark matter.
Dark matter increases gravitational attraction and helps structure formation.
Dark energy is associated with the accelerated expansion of space on cosmic scales.
The physical nature of dark energy remains unknown.
Alternative models include evolving forms of dark energy and modified gravity.
Observations of supernovae, galaxy clustering, and the CMB constrain dark-energy models.

LAMBDA-CDM
Lambda-CDM is the standard model of modern cosmology.
Lambda represents the cosmological constant associated with dark energy.
CDM stands for cold dark matter.
The model also includes ordinary baryonic matter and radiation.
Lambda-CDM successfully explains many observations across cosmic history.
It describes the formation of large-scale structure from small early density fluctuations.
It reproduces major features of the cosmic microwave background.
It explains the broad evolution of cosmic expansion.
The model contains several measured cosmological parameters.
Some observations, including the Hubble tension and the nature of dark energy, motivate tests beyond the basic model.

COSMIC STRUCTURE
The universe is organized into structures including galaxies, groups, clusters, filaments, and voids.
These structures form part of a large-scale pattern called the cosmic web.
Dark matter provides much of the gravitational framework for this structure.
Ordinary matter falls into dark-matter-dominated gravitational wells.
Gas cools and can form stars inside galaxies.
Galaxy groups and clusters form in regions where matter is especially concentrated.
Filaments connect dense regions across the cosmic web.
Voids contain relatively few galaxies compared with surrounding regions.
Structure formation is driven mainly by gravitational growth of initial density fluctuations.
The distribution of galaxies provides information about the underlying matter distribution.

COSMIC WEB
The cosmic web is the large-scale network of matter throughout the universe.
It contains filaments, galaxy clusters, galaxy groups, and vast underdense voids.
Dark matter forms much of the underlying structure of the cosmic web.
Galaxies tend to trace the denser regions of this structure.
Filaments can contain gas and many galaxies.
Clusters often form where multiple filaments intersect.
Voids can span tens to hundreds of millions of light-years.
The cosmic web developed gradually through gravitational instability.
Large-scale galaxy surveys are used to map the cosmic web.
Its structure provides important tests of cosmological models.

COSMIC VOID
A cosmic void is a large region of space containing relatively few galaxies.
Voids are not completely empty.
They can contain diffuse gas, dark matter, and isolated galaxies.
Matter tends to move away from underdense regions toward denser structures.
Voids therefore grow as surrounding matter becomes increasingly concentrated.
The size and distribution of voids depend on the growth of cosmic structure.
Void statistics can be used to test cosmological models.
Large-scale surveys identify voids from the distribution of galaxies.
Voids provide a complementary view of the cosmic web.
Their evolution is influenced by dark energy and gravitational growth.

COSMIC TIME
Looking farther into space generally means looking farther back in time.
Light requires a finite amount of time to travel between astronomical objects.
A galaxy millions of light-years away is observed as it existed millions of years ago.
Very distant galaxies are observed as they existed billions of years in the past.
The cosmic microwave background comes from an even earlier period.
Astronomers use distant observations to reconstruct the history of the universe.
Different wavelengths reveal different stages of cosmic evolution.
The expansion of space changes the wavelength of traveling light.
Cosmological distance and lookback time are related but are not identical quantities.
Cosmic history is therefore encoded in the light reaching Earth.

COSMOLOGICAL DISTANCES
Cosmology uses several different definitions of distance.
Comoving distance accounts for cosmic expansion when describing large-scale positions.
Proper distance describes physical separation at a specified cosmic time.
Lookback time measures how long light has traveled to reach an observer.
Luminosity distance is defined using the observed brightness of an object.
Angular-diameter distance relates an object's physical size to its observed angular size.
Different distance measures are useful for different observations.
Cosmic expansion means that simple Euclidean distance formulas are not always sufficient.
Redshift is commonly used as an observable indicator of cosmological distance.
The relationship between redshift and distance depends on cosmological parameters.

COSMOLOGICAL PRINCIPLE
The cosmological principle states that the universe is approximately homogeneous and isotropic on sufficiently large scales.
Homogeneous means that matter is statistically distributed similarly from place to place.
Isotropic means that the universe looks statistically similar in different directions.
The principle does not require galaxies to be uniformly distributed on small scales.
Galaxies form clusters, filaments, and voids on smaller scales.
Homogeneity becomes a useful approximation only over sufficiently large distances.
Observations of large-scale galaxy distributions support approximate large-scale homogeneity.
The cosmological principle is a foundational assumption in many cosmological models.
It simplifies the mathematical description of the expanding universe.
Testing the principle remains an important observational goal.

GEOMETRY OF THE UNIVERSE
The geometry of the universe describes its large-scale spatial curvature.
A universe can have positive, zero, or negative spatial curvature.
Positive curvature corresponds to a closed geometry in simple cosmological models.
Zero curvature corresponds to a spatially flat geometry.
Negative curvature corresponds to an open geometry.
CMB observations indicate that the observable universe is very close to spatially flat.
Spatial flatness does not necessarily mean that the entire universe is finite or infinite.
Cosmic geometry depends on the balance of matter, radiation, dark energy, and curvature.
Measurements of the CMB and large-scale structure constrain spatial curvature.
The geometry of spacetime also includes time and is described by general relativity.

FATE OF THE UNIVERSE
The long-term evolution of the universe depends on its expansion history and physical contents.
Current observations indicate that cosmic expansion is accelerating.
If dark energy behaves like a cosmological constant, expansion is expected to continue indefinitely.
A continuously expanding universe would gradually become colder and more dilute.
This scenario is commonly associated with heat death or the Big Freeze.
Alternative dark-energy behavior could produce different futures.
A sufficiently strong future change in dark energy could alter the expansion history.
Current observations do not support a near-term cosmic recollapse.
The ultimate fate of the universe remains connected to the nature of dark energy.
Future observations will help determine whether the standard expansion scenario remains valid.

OBSERVATIONAL COSMOLOGY
Observational cosmology uses astronomical measurements to test models of the universe.
The cosmic microwave background is one of its most important datasets.
Type Ia supernovae are used as distance indicators for studying cosmic expansion.
Galaxy surveys measure positions, redshifts, and properties of millions of galaxies.
Baryon acoustic oscillations provide a standard ruler for measuring cosmic distances.
Weak gravitational lensing traces the distribution of matter.
Galaxy clusters provide information about structure growth and dark matter.
Quasar and galaxy observations probe the distant universe.
Different observations can be combined to constrain cosmological parameters.
Agreement between independent datasets is a major test of cosmological models.

KEY TERMS
Cosmology means the scientific study of the universe as a whole.
Big Bang model describes the evolution of the universe from a hot, dense early state.
Cosmic expansion means that large-scale distances between unbound regions of the universe increase with time.
Redshift is the shift of observed spectral features toward longer wavelengths.
Cosmic microwave background is relic electromagnetic radiation from the early universe.
Recombination is the epoch when electrons combined with nuclei to form neutral atoms.
Dark matter is matter inferred primarily from gravitational effects.
Dark energy is the component associated with the accelerated expansion of the universe.
Lambda-CDM is the standard cosmological model containing dark energy and cold dark matter.
Cosmic web is the large-scale network of galaxies, filaments, clusters, and voids.
Inflation is a proposed period of extremely rapid expansion in the early universe.
Big Bang nucleosynthesis describes the formation of light nuclei during the early universe.
Hubble constant describes the present-day expansion rate of the universe.
Hubble tension refers to disagreement between some independent measurements of the Hubble constant.
Observable universe is the region from which signals have had time to reach Earth.
