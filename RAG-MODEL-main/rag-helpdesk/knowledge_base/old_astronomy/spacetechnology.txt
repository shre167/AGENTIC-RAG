SPACE TECHNOLOGY
Space technology includes the engineering systems used to design, launch, operate, and communicate with spacecraft.
Spacecraft must function in vacuum, microgravity, radiation, and extreme thermal conditions.
Space systems are designed around mission requirements, available power, mass limits, and reliability.
Major spacecraft technologies include propulsion, power, communication, computing, navigation, and thermal control.
Sensors and scientific instruments allow spacecraft to measure their environment and targets.
Launch vehicles provide the initial energy needed to place spacecraft into space.
Orbital mechanics determines how spacecraft move under gravitational forces.
Space technology combines aerospace engineering, electronics, software, materials science, and physics.
Reliability is particularly important because spacecraft are difficult or impossible to repair after launch.
Technology developed for space missions can sometimes lead to useful applications on Earth.

SPACECRAFT ARCHITECTURE
A spacecraft is generally divided into a platform or bus and one or more payloads.
The spacecraft bus provides common services required by the mission.
Typical bus subsystems include power, thermal control, communications, attitude control, and onboard computing.
The payload performs the primary scientific, commercial, or technological mission function.
Spacecraft structures support components and maintain mechanical integrity during launch.
Electrical wiring distributes power and connects spacecraft subsystems.
Onboard computers coordinate spacecraft operations and process data.
Software allows spacecraft systems to execute commands and respond to changing conditions.
Subsystem interfaces must be carefully designed to prevent conflicts.
Spacecraft architecture depends strongly on mission destination and operational requirements.

SPACECRAFT STRUCTURES
The spacecraft structure provides mechanical support for onboard components.
Structures must survive vibration and acceleration during launch.
Launch environments can produce strong acoustic, mechanical, and shock loads.
Materials must provide adequate strength while minimizing spacecraft mass.
Aluminum alloys and composite materials are commonly used in spacecraft structures.
Deployable structures can increase the size of solar arrays or antennas after launch.
Structural analysis predicts stresses, vibrations, and possible failure modes.
Thermal expansion must also be considered when designing spacecraft structures.
Instrument mounting must minimize unwanted vibration and mechanical disturbance.
Structural testing is performed before launch to verify mechanical integrity.

PROPULSION
Spacecraft propulsion provides controlled changes in velocity and trajectory.
Chemical propulsion uses the energy released by chemical reactions to produce thrust.
Electric propulsion accelerates propellant using electrical energy.
Chemical thrusters can provide high thrust for relatively short periods.
Electric propulsion generally provides low thrust but can operate for long periods.
Specific impulse is a measure of propulsion efficiency related to propellant consumption.
Hydrazine and other propellants have historically been used in spacecraft propulsion systems.
Modern spacecraft may use lower-toxicity propellants and advanced propulsion technologies.
Propulsion systems can perform orbit insertion, trajectory correction, attitude control, and station keeping.
The required propulsion system depends on spacecraft mass, mission duration, and trajectory.

ELECTRIC PROPULSION
Electric propulsion uses electrical energy to accelerate charged particles or heat propellant.
Ion thrusters accelerate ions using electric fields.
Hall-effect thrusters use electric and magnetic fields to accelerate plasma.
Electric propulsion typically produces much lower thrust than chemical propulsion.
Its high efficiency makes it useful for long-duration orbital maneuvers.
A spacecraft using electric propulsion may accelerate continuously for weeks or months.
Solar panels can supply electrical power for many electric-propulsion systems.
Electric propulsion can significantly reduce the propellant mass required for some missions.
It is particularly useful for satellite station keeping and deep-space trajectory changes.
Electric propulsion is an important technology for future high-efficiency space missions.

ROCKET ENGINES
Rocket engines generate thrust by accelerating exhaust in the opposite direction of motion.
The basic principle follows conservation of momentum.
A chemical rocket carries both fuel and oxidizer when operating outside Earth's atmosphere.
The combustion chamber converts chemical energy into hot high-pressure gases.
A nozzle accelerates exhaust gases to produce directed thrust.
Liquid rocket engines can control thrust and may be restarted depending on design.
Solid rocket motors generally have simpler mechanical designs but limited throttling capability.
Cryogenic engines use very cold propellants such as liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen.
Engine performance depends on combustion conditions, nozzle design, propellant properties, and mass flow.
Rocket-engine reliability is critical because launch vehicles operate under extreme conditions.

PROPULSION STAGES
Many launch vehicles use multiple rocket stages.
Each stage contains its own propulsion and propellant systems.
A stage is discarded after its useful propellant is consumed.
Discarding empty stages reduces the mass that subsequent stages must accelerate.
Upper stages provide the velocity needed to reach the final target orbit or trajectory.
Some upper stages can perform multiple burns.
Restartable engines allow an upper stage to execute separate orbital maneuvers.
Stage separation mechanisms must operate accurately and reliably.
The number and design of stages depend on launch requirements.
Staging is one of the major methods used to improve rocket performance.

POWER SYSTEMS
Spacecraft require electrical power for computers, communication systems, sensors, heaters, and propulsion.
Solar photovoltaic cells convert sunlight into electrical energy.
Solar arrays are commonly used by spacecraft operating near the Sun.
Rechargeable batteries provide power when solar energy is unavailable.
Spacecraft can enter eclipse when Earth or another body blocks sunlight.
Battery capacity must be sufficient to support the spacecraft during expected eclipse periods.
Power-management systems distribute electrical energy among spacecraft subsystems.
Electrical loads are prioritized according to mission requirements.
Radioisotope power systems can provide long-term energy far from the Sun.
Power-system degradation over time must be considered during mission design.

SOLAR CELLS
Solar cells convert incoming sunlight into electrical power through the photovoltaic effect.
Spacecraft solar cells are designed for high efficiency and long-term operation.
Solar arrays can be folded during launch and deployed after reaching space.
The available solar power decreases with increasing distance from the Sun.
Radiation can gradually degrade solar-cell performance.
Temperature also affects solar-cell efficiency.
The spacecraft attitude determines how much sunlight reaches the solar arrays.
Sun-tracking or spacecraft pointing strategies can maximize available power.
Solar arrays must be designed to survive launch loads and the space environment.
Power generation is a fundamental constraint for many spacecraft missions.

RADIOISOTOPE POWER
Radioisotope power systems generate electricity from the heat produced by radioactive decay.
They are useful when sunlight is weak or unavailable.
Radioisotope thermoelectric generators convert heat into electrical energy using thermoelectric materials.
RTGs can provide power for many years.
They are especially useful for missions to the outer Solar System.
RTGs do not require sunlight and can operate during long periods of darkness.
Their power output gradually decreases as the radioactive material decays.
Radioisotope systems are different from nuclear fission reactors.
They provide relatively low continuous power but high reliability.
Several deep-space missions have used radioisotope power systems.

THERMAL CONTROL
Spacecraft thermal control keeps components within their permitted temperature ranges.
Space has no atmosphere to provide ordinary convective cooling.
Heat is primarily transferred within spacecraft through conduction and radiated into space.
Radiators emit excess heat as infrared radiation.
Multilayer insulation reduces unwanted heat transfer.
Heaters can prevent components from becoming too cold.
Thermal coatings can control how spacecraft surfaces absorb and emit radiation.
Spacecraft may experience large temperature differences between sunlight and shadow.
Sensitive instruments often require tightly controlled temperatures.
Thermal design must account for both launch and long-term space operations.

ATTITUDE CONTROL
Attitude control determines and maintains spacecraft orientation.
Spacecraft orientation is important for pointing antennas, cameras, solar panels, and scientific instruments.
Reaction wheels change spacecraft orientation by transferring angular momentum.
Control-moment gyroscopes can provide larger attitude-control torques on some spacecraft.
Small thrusters can also rotate or stabilize a spacecraft.
Magnetorquers use interactions with Earth's magnetic field to control satellite attitude.
Star trackers determine spacecraft orientation by comparing observed stars with a known star catalog.
Sun sensors can determine the direction of the Sun.
Gyroscopes measure changes in rotational motion.
Attitude-control systems combine sensors, computers, and actuators.

GUIDANCE AND NAVIGATION
Guidance determines where a spacecraft should go.
Navigation estimates the spacecraft's current position and velocity.
Control systems apply the required changes to follow the desired trajectory.
Inertial measurement units can measure acceleration and rotation.
Ground stations can determine spacecraft trajectories using radio tracking.
Optical navigation can use images of planets, moons, or stars.
Autonomous navigation reduces dependence on continuous communication with Earth.
Navigation accuracy is especially important during planetary encounters and landings.
Guidance algorithms can calculate corrections when the spacecraft deviates from its planned path.
Reliable navigation is essential for rendezvous, docking, landing, and orbital insertion.

COMMUNICATION SYSTEMS
Spacecraft communication systems exchange information between spacecraft and ground stations.
Radio waves are widely used for space communication.
Antennas determine the direction and efficiency of transmitted and received signals.
High-gain antennas provide narrow beams and high communication efficiency.
Low-gain antennas provide broader coverage but generally lower data rates.
Transmitters convert electrical information into radio-frequency signals.
Receivers recover information from incoming radio signals.
Communication links are affected by distance, antenna orientation, frequency, and available power.
Deep-space links require highly sensitive ground antennas and careful signal processing.
Communication bandwidth determines how quickly science and engineering data can be transmitted.

DATA HANDLING
Spacecraft generate both scientific data and engineering telemetry.
Onboard computers process, store, and organize data before transmission.
Data storage allows spacecraft to retain information when communication with Earth is unavailable.
Compression can reduce the amount of data that must be transmitted.
Lossless compression preserves all original information.
Lossy compression reduces data size by allowing some information to be discarded.
Data packets include information needed to identify and reconstruct transmitted data.
Error-detection methods help identify corrupted information.
Onboard data handling must operate reliably with limited computing resources.
Data-management design depends on instrument output, storage capacity, and communication bandwidth.

ONBOARD COMPUTING
Spacecraft computers control many automated functions.
Flight software manages spacecraft subsystems and executes mission commands.
Spacecraft computers must tolerate radiation and temperature variations.
Radiation can cause temporary or permanent electronic faults.
Error detection and correction can improve computer reliability.
Watchdog systems can reset software when abnormal behavior is detected.
Redundant computers can provide backup processing capability.
Software updates can sometimes be transmitted after launch.
Autonomous software is important when communication delays prevent immediate human intervention.
Software verification and testing are major parts of spacecraft development.

RADIATION
Spacecraft operate in an environment containing energetic particles and electromagnetic radiation.
Solar radiation can affect spacecraft surfaces and electronics.
Solar energetic particles can produce radiation hazards during solar activity.
Earth's radiation belts contain trapped energetic charged particles.
Galactic cosmic rays originate outside the Solar System and can affect spacecraft electronics.
Radiation can cause single-event effects in electronic components.
Long-term radiation exposure can degrade materials and semiconductor performance.
Radiation shielding can reduce exposure to sensitive components.
Shielding increases spacecraft mass and therefore must be optimized.
Radiation testing helps engineers select components suitable for space missions.

MICROMETEOROIDS AND DEBRIS
Small natural particles in space are called micrometeoroids.
Artificial fragments orbiting Earth are generally referred to as space debris.
High relative velocities can make even small particles dangerous to spacecraft.
Impacts can damage solar panels, antennas, windows, or spacecraft structures.
Spacecraft in low Earth orbit can be exposed to large populations of debris.
Collision-avoidance systems can help protect important satellites.
Tracking networks monitor many larger objects in Earth orbit.
Protective shielding can reduce damage from some small impacts.
Space-debris mitigation includes reducing the creation of new orbital debris.
Responsible mission design considers disposal or deorbiting after operational life.

ORBITAL MANEUVERS
An orbital maneuver changes spacecraft velocity to modify its orbit.
A small velocity change can produce a large change in orbital position over time.
Thrusters or rocket engines can provide the required delta-v.
Delta-v is the change in velocity available from a propulsion system.
Orbit raising increases the altitude of an orbit.
Orbit lowering decreases orbital altitude.
Plane changes alter the orientation of an orbital plane.
Rendezvous missions require precise matching of position and velocity.
Station keeping maintains a spacecraft near its assigned orbital location.
Fuel-efficient orbital maneuver planning is important because spacecraft propellant is limited.

SATELLITE SYSTEMS
A satellite is an object intentionally placed into orbit around a celestial body.
Artificial satellites can provide communication, navigation, weather, science, and Earth-observation services.
Satellite orbits are selected according to mission requirements.
Low Earth orbit satellites are relatively close to Earth's surface.
Medium Earth orbit is commonly used by navigation satellite constellations.
Geostationary satellites remain above approximately the same longitude.
Satellite constellations use multiple spacecraft to provide broader or continuous coverage.
Ground stations communicate with satellites and monitor their health.
Satellite operators perform orbit and attitude maintenance throughout the mission.
End-of-life procedures reduce the long-term accumulation of orbital debris.

EARTH OBSERVATION TECHNOLOGY
Earth-observation satellites use sensors to measure properties of Earth's surface and atmosphere.
Optical cameras collect reflected sunlight in visible wavelengths.
Multispectral instruments measure several selected wavelength bands.
Hyperspectral instruments can measure many narrow spectral bands.
Synthetic aperture radar can observe Earth using active microwave signals.
Radar can operate through clouds and can observe during both day and night.
Thermal infrared sensors measure emitted radiation related to surface temperature.
Earth-observation data can support agriculture, disaster response, weather analysis, and environmental monitoring.
Spatial resolution describes the smallest ground features that can be distinguished.
Temporal resolution describes how frequently an area can be observed.

SPACE OPTICS
Optical systems collect and focus electromagnetic radiation.
Mirrors or lenses can direct incoming light toward detectors.
Space telescopes avoid atmospheric distortion for many observations.
Reflecting telescopes use mirrors rather than large lenses as their primary collecting optics.
Detector sensitivity affects the faintness of objects that can be observed.
Optical alignment must remain within tight tolerances.
Thermal changes can alter the shape or alignment of optical components.
Large space telescopes often use deployable structures to fit inside launch vehicles.
Infrared instruments require specialized thermal designs.
Optical engineering directly affects the quality of scientific observations.

SENSORS AND INSTRUMENTS
Sensors convert physical phenomena into measurable electrical or digital signals.
Scientific instruments are designed around specific measurement objectives.
Spectrometers separate electromagnetic radiation according to wavelength.
Cameras record spatial information about targets.
Radar systems transmit signals and measure returned echoes.
Magnetometers measure magnetic fields.
Accelerometers measure changes in linear motion.
Radiation detectors measure energetic particles or electromagnetic radiation.
Instrument calibration converts detector output into scientifically meaningful measurements.
Instrument selection depends on target properties, wavelength, sensitivity, resolution, and available spacecraft resources.

SPACE ROBOTICS
Space robotics includes robotic systems designed to operate in space or on planetary surfaces.
Robotic arms can manipulate equipment, samples, or spacecraft components.
Rovers use wheels or other mobility systems to travel across planetary surfaces.
Robotic systems may operate with significant autonomy.
Communication delays make direct manual control difficult for distant planetary missions.
Cameras and other sensors provide information needed for navigation.
Autonomous navigation allows a rover to avoid hazards and plan movement.
Robotic systems must tolerate dust, radiation, temperature changes, and mechanical wear.
Planetary robots can perform scientific measurements in environments difficult for humans to access.
Robotics is an important technology for future lunar and planetary exploration.

LANDING TECHNOLOGY
Landing systems safely deliver spacecraft to the surface of a planetary body.
Atmospheric bodies can use aerodynamic drag, parachutes, or powered descent.
Bodies with little atmosphere require different techniques such as powered braking.
Lunar landers generally rely on rocket propulsion for controlled descent.
Terrain-relative navigation can help spacecraft identify safe landing regions.
Altimeters measure distance from the surface during descent.
Landing systems must reduce horizontal and vertical velocity before contact.
Hazard detection can identify rocks, slopes, craters, or other dangerous terrain.
Landing legs or other structures absorb impact forces.
Autonomous landing systems are valuable when communication delays prevent real-time control.

SPACE DOCKING
Docking allows two spacecraft to connect physically in orbit.
Rendezvous operations first bring two spacecraft into nearby trajectories.
Relative navigation determines the position and velocity of one spacecraft relative to another.
The spacecraft must carefully control its approach speed.
Docking mechanisms align and connect the spacecraft.
Sensors can include cameras, radar, lidar, and relative-position measurements.
Docking is used to transfer crew, cargo, fuel, or equipment.
Automated docking can reduce the need for continuous manual control.
Docking requires accurate guidance, navigation, and attitude control.
Crewed docking missions require additional safety and abort procedures.

REUSABLE SPACE TECHNOLOGY
Reusable launch systems are designed to recover and fly some hardware on multiple missions.
Reusability can reduce the hardware cost per launch when refurbishment is efficient.
Reusable stages require controlled atmospheric reentry and landing.
Thermal protection is needed to survive high temperatures during reentry.
Landing systems can use engines, aerodynamic surfaces, parachutes, or combinations of technologies.
Reusable spacecraft must be designed for repeated structural and thermal loads.
Turnaround time affects the practical benefits of reusability.
Reliability must remain high across repeated flights.
Not every launch vehicle is designed for reusability.
Reusability is one approach to reducing the cost of access to space.

SPACE TECHNOLOGY CHALLENGES
Mass is one of the most important constraints in spacecraft design.
Increasing spacecraft mass generally increases launch requirements.
Power availability limits the operation of instruments and onboard systems.
Thermal environments can vary greatly during a mission.
Radiation can degrade electronics and materials.
Communication delays complicate operations far from Earth.
Spacecraft cannot normally be repaired after launch.
Components therefore require extensive testing before deployment.
Engineers must balance performance, reliability, cost, and development time.
Space technology advances through repeated testing, missions, failures, and engineering improvements.

KEY TERMS
Spacecraft means a vehicle or platform designed to operate in space.
Spacecraft bus means the collection of subsystems that provide common spacecraft services.
Payload means the mission-specific equipment that performs the primary objective.
Propulsion means systems used to change spacecraft velocity or generate thrust.
Delta-v means the change in velocity available or required for a maneuver.
Specific impulse is a measure of rocket-propulsion efficiency.
Attitude means the orientation of a spacecraft.
Reaction wheel means an actuator that changes spacecraft orientation by exchanging angular momentum.
Telemetry means engineering information transmitted from a spacecraft.
Ground station means a terrestrial facility used to communicate with spacecraft.
Thermal control means methods used to maintain spacecraft temperature within safe limits.
Radiation shielding means material protection used to reduce radiation exposure.
Electric propulsion means propulsion that uses electrical energy to accelerate propellant.
Gravity assist means changing spacecraft trajectory using the gravity and motion of a celestial body.
Autonomous navigation means spacecraft navigation performed partly without continuous ground control.
Space debris means artificial objects and fragments remaining in orbit around Earth.
