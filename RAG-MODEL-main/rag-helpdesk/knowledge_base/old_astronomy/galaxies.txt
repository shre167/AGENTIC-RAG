GALAXIES
A galaxy is a gravitationally bound system containing stars, gas, dust, dark matter, and other astronomical objects.
Galaxies range from small dwarf systems to enormous galaxies containing hundreds of billions of stars.
Gravity is the primary force holding a galaxy together on large scales.
Most of the mass of a galaxy is thought to be contained in dark matter.
Stars, gas, and dust form the visible or baryonic component of a galaxy.
Galaxies can contain star clusters, nebulae, stellar remnants, planets, and black holes.
The Milky Way is the galaxy containing our Solar System.
Galaxies are major building blocks of the large-scale structure of the universe.
Galaxies can interact, merge, grow, and change their internal structure over cosmic time.
The study of galaxies is called galactic astronomy or galaxy evolution research.

GALAXY STRUCTURE
Many large galaxies contain a central region called the bulge.
A galactic disk is a flattened rotating structure containing stars, gas, and dust.
Spiral galaxies commonly have prominent disks and central bulges.
The outer region surrounding a galaxy is called its halo.
A galactic halo contains stars, globular clusters, hot gas, and large amounts of dark matter.
The dark-matter halo extends much farther than the bright visible disk.
The central region of many galaxies contains a supermassive black hole.
Galaxies can have different proportions of bulge, disk, gas, and stellar components.
The distribution of matter influences how stars and gas move within a galaxy.
Galaxy structure can provide clues about a galaxy's formation and evolutionary history.

TYPES OF GALAXIES
The main broad morphological classes include spiral, elliptical, and irregular galaxies.
Spiral galaxies have flattened disks and spiral structures around their central regions.
Elliptical galaxies generally have smooth, rounded or elongated stellar distributions.
Irregular galaxies lack a simple regular structure.
Lenticular galaxies have disks but generally lack prominent spiral arms.
Dwarf galaxies are relatively small and can belong to several morphological categories.
Galaxy classification describes visible structure rather than every physical property.
A galaxy can change morphology through interactions and mergers.
Some galaxies show features that make classification difficult.
Modern galaxy studies use both morphology and physical measurements.

SPIRAL GALAXIES
Spiral galaxies contain rotating stellar and gaseous disks.
Their spiral arms are regions where stars, gas, and dust are concentrated.
Spiral arms are associated with enhanced star formation.
Many spiral galaxies contain a central bulge.
Some spiral galaxies have a bar crossing their central region.
The Milky Way is classified as a barred spiral galaxy.
Spiral galaxies generally contain significant amounts of cold gas and dust.
Their disks can continue forming new stars over long periods.
The rotation of a spiral galaxy is influenced strongly by its dark-matter halo.
Interactions with other galaxies can distort or enhance spiral structure.

ELLIPTICAL GALAXIES
Elliptical galaxies generally have smooth stellar distributions without prominent spiral arms.
They range from relatively small dwarf ellipticals to giant elliptical galaxies.
Many elliptical galaxies contain older stellar populations.
Large elliptical galaxies often contain relatively little cold gas compared with star-forming spirals.
Star formation in many elliptical galaxies is weak or suppressed.
The stars in elliptical galaxies can follow a wide range of orbital directions.
Some elliptical galaxies form through mergers involving other galaxies.
Giant elliptical galaxies can occupy the centers of galaxy clusters.
Their shapes range from nearly spherical to highly elongated.
Elliptical galaxies provide important evidence about galaxy mergers and evolution.

IRREGULAR GALAXIES
Irregular galaxies do not fit cleanly into spiral, elliptical, or lenticular categories.
Their structures can be asymmetric or strongly disturbed.
Gravitational interactions can produce irregular shapes.
Some irregular galaxies contain substantial amounts of gas and active star formation.
Dwarf irregular galaxies are common in the nearby universe.
The Large Magellanic Cloud has an irregular or Magellanic-type structure.
The Small Magellanic Cloud is another nearby irregular galaxy.
Irregular galaxies can provide laboratories for studying star formation in low-mass systems.
Their evolution can be strongly influenced by nearby massive galaxies.
Tidal forces can stretch and distort irregular galaxies.

LENTICULAR GALAXIES
Lenticular galaxies are commonly classified as S0 galaxies.
They have a central bulge and a disk but lack prominent spiral arms.
Their disks can contain relatively little cold gas.
Star formation is generally lower than in typical spiral galaxies.
Lenticular galaxies can occur in dense environments such as galaxy clusters.
Environmental processes can remove gas from a galaxy.
A spiral galaxy can potentially evolve toward a lenticular state after losing much of its cold gas.
Their structure makes them intermediate in appearance between spiral and elliptical galaxies.
Lenticular galaxies demonstrate that galaxy morphology forms a broader evolutionary sequence.
Detailed observations are needed to determine how individual lenticular galaxies formed.

THE MILKY WAY
The Milky Way is a barred spiral galaxy.
The Solar System lies in the Milky Way's disk.
The Sun is located in a region of the disk called the Orion Arm or Orion Spur.
The Milky Way contains hundreds of billions of stars, although the exact number is uncertain.
Its visible disk spans roughly 100,000 light-years in diameter.
The galaxy contains a central bulge surrounded by a bar.
A large dark-matter halo surrounds the visible galaxy.
The center of the Milky Way contains the supermassive black hole Sagittarius A*.
The Milky Way is part of a small collection of galaxies called the Local Group.
The Milky Way interacts gravitationally with nearby galaxies including the Andromeda Galaxy.

ANDROMEDA GALAXY
The Andromeda Galaxy is also known as Messier 31 or M31.
Andromeda is the nearest large spiral galaxy to the Milky Way.
It is located roughly 2.5 million light-years from Earth.
Andromeda is a large spiral galaxy with a central bulge and disk.
It contains a supermassive black hole at its center.
The galaxy has satellite galaxies orbiting around it.
Andromeda and the Milky Way are the two dominant large galaxies in the Local Group.
Their gravitational interaction will change both galaxies over cosmic time.
Current models predict a future major interaction between the Milky Way and Andromeda.
The exact details and timing of their future encounter remain uncertain.

LOCAL GROUP
The Local Group is a gravitationally bound collection of galaxies.
The Milky Way and Andromeda are its two largest galaxies.
The Local Group contains numerous dwarf galaxies.
The Triangulum Galaxy is another major spiral galaxy in the Local Group.
Satellite galaxies orbit larger galaxies within the group.
The gravitational influence of the Local Group extends across millions of light-years.
Galaxies in the Local Group interact through gravity.
Dwarf galaxies can lose gas and stars through tidal interactions.
Studying nearby galaxies provides detailed information about galaxy formation.
The Local Group is part of the larger cosmic web.

GALAXY CLUSTERS
A galaxy cluster is a gravitationally bound collection of many galaxies.
Clusters can contain hundreds or thousands of galaxies.
Most of the mass of a cluster is dark matter.
Hot intracluster gas can account for a significant fraction of the ordinary matter.
The hot gas emits strongly in X-rays.
Galaxy clusters are among the largest gravitationally bound structures in the universe.
Galaxies inside clusters interact with one another and with the cluster environment.
Ram-pressure stripping can remove gas from galaxies moving through hot cluster gas.
Cluster environments can influence star formation and galaxy morphology.
The mass of clusters can be estimated using galaxy motions, X-rays, and gravitational lensing.

GALAXY GROUPS
A galaxy group contains fewer galaxies than a typical massive galaxy cluster.
The Local Group is an example of a galaxy group.
Galaxy groups are commonly dominated by one or a few large galaxies.
Dwarf satellite galaxies can orbit the dominant galaxies.
Gravity keeps the group members associated over long periods.
Interactions between group members can alter their shapes and star-formation activity.
Galaxy groups can eventually merge into larger structures.
Groups are important environments for studying galaxy interactions.
Their lower densities distinguish them from massive clusters.
Galaxy groups contribute to the hierarchical growth of cosmic structure.

GALAXY FORMATION
Galaxies formed as matter became concentrated under gravity in the early universe.
Small density variations in the early universe provided seeds for later structure.
Dark matter collapsed into gravitationally bound halos.
Ordinary matter fell into these halos and formed gas-rich structures.
Cooling gas could condense and form stars.
Stars and stellar systems gradually built larger galaxies.
Galaxy formation is a continuing process rather than a single event.
Mergers and gas accretion can significantly change a galaxy.
Feedback from stars and black holes can influence how efficiently galaxies form stars.
Modern galaxy formation models combine dark matter, gas physics, star formation, and feedback.

GALAXY MERGERS
A galaxy merger occurs when two galaxies interact strongly and eventually combine.
Mergers are driven by gravitational forces.
During a merger, stellar orbits can be significantly rearranged.
Gas clouds can collide and become compressed.
Gas compression can trigger bursts of star formation.
Major mergers can transform the structure of disk galaxies.
Minor mergers occur when a large galaxy interacts with a much smaller galaxy.
Mergers can contribute to the growth of massive elliptical galaxies.
Central supermassive black holes can eventually merge after their host galaxies combine.
Galaxy mergers are important mechanisms in hierarchical galaxy evolution.

STAR FORMATION IN GALAXIES
Stars form primarily from cold, dense molecular gas.
Molecular clouds can collapse when gravity overcomes internal support.
Regions of active star formation are often associated with bright nebulae.
Young massive stars emit ultraviolet radiation that can ionize surrounding gas.
Supernova explosions return energy and heavy elements to the surrounding interstellar medium.
Star formation can therefore influence the gas from which later generations of stars form.
The rate of star formation varies strongly between different galaxies.
Spiral galaxies can maintain ongoing star formation across their disks.
Some galaxies undergo short periods of extremely intense star formation called starbursts.
Galaxy evolution depends partly on the balance between gas supply, star formation, and feedback.

GALACTIC ROTATION
Stars and gas in a galaxy can orbit its center under gravity.
The orbital speed of stars depends on the distribution of mass within the galaxy.
Spiral galaxies often show nearly flat rotation curves at large distances from their centers.
The observed rotation speeds imply more mass than can be explained by visible matter alone.
This discrepancy is one of the major lines of evidence for dark matter.
Dark matter is thought to form an extended halo around galaxies.
Gas motions can also be used to measure a galaxy's gravitational potential.
Galaxy rotation provides information about both visible and unseen mass.
The Milky Way's rotation has been measured using stars and gas.
Rotation patterns can also reveal bars, spiral structures, and other internal features.

DARK MATTER IN GALAXIES
Dark matter does not significantly interact with electromagnetic radiation.
It is therefore not directly visible through ordinary telescopes.
Its gravitational effects reveal its presence around galaxies.
Dark-matter halos provide gravitational wells that help galaxies assemble.
The amount of dark matter strongly affects galaxy rotation.
Gravitational lensing can independently reveal dark-matter distributions.
Galaxy clusters contain large quantities of dark matter.
The nature of dark matter remains an open question in particle physics and cosmology.
Dark matter is different from ordinary gas, dust, stars, and planets.
Understanding dark matter is essential for explaining the large-scale formation of galaxies.

INTERSTELLAR MEDIUM
The interstellar medium is the material occupying the space between stars.
It contains gas, dust, cosmic rays, magnetic fields, and radiation.
Hydrogen is the most abundant element in the interstellar medium.
Gas can exist in ionized, atomic, or molecular forms.
Molecular clouds are important sites of star formation.
Dust grains can absorb and scatter visible light.
Dust can also emit thermal radiation at infrared wavelengths.
Supernovae inject energy and heavy elements into the interstellar medium.
Stellar winds can also shape surrounding gas.
The interstellar medium connects stellar evolution with future generations of stars.

GALACTIC NUCLEI
The central region of a galaxy is called its nucleus.
Many galaxies contain supermassive black holes in their nuclei.
A galaxy with a very luminous active nucleus is called an active galactic nucleus.
Active galactic nuclei are powered by accretion onto central supermassive black holes.
The accretion disk can emit large amounts of electromagnetic radiation.
Some active galactic nuclei produce powerful relativistic jets.
Quasars are extremely luminous active galactic nuclei.
Not all galaxies with central black holes are currently active.
The activity level depends strongly on the availability and inflow of gas.
Black-hole activity can influence the evolution of the host galaxy.

GALAXY OBSERVATION
Astronomers observe galaxies across the electromagnetic spectrum.
Visible light reveals stars, dust structures, and galaxy morphology.
Infrared observations can detect cool dust and reveal stars hidden by dust.
Radio observations can trace neutral hydrogen and other gas.
Ultraviolet observations are useful for identifying young massive stars.
X-ray observations can reveal hot gas, binary systems, and active galactic nuclei.
Spectroscopy provides information about chemical composition, temperature, and motion.
Redshift measurements can estimate how rapidly a galaxy is receding because of cosmic expansion.
Gravitational lensing can magnify and distort distant galaxies.
Combining multiple wavelengths provides a more complete picture of galaxy evolution.

GALAXY REDSHIFT
Galaxy redshift describes the shift of spectral features toward longer wavelengths.
Cosmological redshift occurs because space expands as light travels through the universe.
For sufficiently distant galaxies, redshift is related to cosmic expansion.
Redshift can be measured from known spectral lines.
Astronomers use redshift to estimate distances and study the history of cosmic expansion.
Higher redshift generally corresponds to observing galaxies at earlier cosmic times.
Very distant galaxies are therefore seen as they existed billions of years ago.
Galaxy surveys use redshift to construct three-dimensional maps of cosmic structure.
Redshift is different from ordinary Doppler shifts caused solely by local motion.
The interpretation of redshift depends on the scale and physical context of the observation.

GALAXY EVOLUTION
Galaxies evolve through star formation, gas accretion, mergers, and internal processes.
Gas can enter galaxies from the surrounding cosmic environment.
Stars consume gas and return some material through stellar winds and supernovae.
Black-hole feedback can heat or expel gas.
Galaxy mergers can rearrange stars and trigger new star formation.
Environmental effects can remove gas from galaxies in groups and clusters.
The balance of these processes determines a galaxy's size, structure, and star-formation rate.
Galaxies observed at different distances represent different stages of cosmic history.
Distant galaxy surveys therefore provide a record of galaxy evolution.
No single mechanism explains the evolution of every galaxy.

LARGE-SCALE STRUCTURE
Galaxies are distributed across the universe in a large-scale pattern called the cosmic web.
The cosmic web contains filaments, galaxy groups, clusters, and large underdense regions.
Dark matter provides much of the underlying gravitational structure.
Galaxies preferentially form along dense regions of the cosmic web.
Clusters are commonly located where several filaments intersect.
Voids are large regions containing relatively few galaxies.
The distribution of galaxies preserves information about the growth of cosmic structure.
Galaxy surveys map this structure using positions and redshifts.
The cosmic web developed through gravitational growth from early density fluctuations.
Galaxy formation is therefore connected to the evolution of the entire universe.

KEY TERMS
Galaxy means a gravitationally bound system of stars, gas, dust, and dark matter.
Bulge means the dense central stellar component of many galaxies.
Disk means the flattened rotating component found in spiral and lenticular galaxies.
Halo means the extended region surrounding a galaxy containing dark matter and other material.
Spiral galaxy means a galaxy with a disk and prominent spiral structure.
Elliptical galaxy means a galaxy with a smooth, generally ellipsoidal stellar distribution.
Irregular galaxy means a galaxy lacking a simple regular morphological structure.
Galaxy group means a relatively small gravitationally bound collection of galaxies.
Galaxy cluster means a massive gravitationally bound collection containing many galaxies.
Interstellar medium means the gas, dust, radiation, magnetic fields, and cosmic rays between stars.
Active galactic nucleus means a luminous galactic center powered by accretion onto a central black hole.
Quasar means an extremely luminous type of active galactic nucleus.
Galaxy merger means a strong gravitational interaction that can lead to galaxies combining.
Cosmic web means the large-scale network of filaments, clusters, groups, and voids containing galaxies.
Galaxy redshift means the shift of observed spectral features toward longer wavelengths.
