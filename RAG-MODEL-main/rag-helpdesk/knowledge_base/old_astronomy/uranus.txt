URANUS — OVERVIEW
Uranus is the seventh planet from the Sun.
Uranus is classified as an ice giant.
It is the third-largest planet in the Solar System by diameter.
Uranus has a diameter of about 50,724 kilometers.
Uranus has a mass about 14.5 times that of Earth.
Its average distance from the Sun is about 19.2 astronomical units.
One astronomical unit is the average Earth-Sun distance.
Uranus takes about 84 Earth years to complete one orbit.
Its average orbital speed is about 6.8 kilometers per second.
Uranus has a low average density of about 1.27 grams per cubic centimeter.
Its pale blue-green appearance is mainly caused by methane in its atmosphere.

ORIGIN AND FORMATION
Uranus formed about 4.5 billion years ago during Solar System formation.
It formed from material present in the young solar system.
Its formation occurred far beyond the region where Earth formed.
Low temperatures allowed volatile compounds to become abundant in its building material.
Water, methane, and ammonia became important components of its interior.
Uranus formed in the outer Solar System beyond the snow line.
The snow line is the region where volatile compounds could condense into solid material.
Growth of Uranus involved collisions and accumulation of planetesimals.
Its present position may have been influenced by early gravitational interactions.
The detailed migration history of Uranus remains an area of planetary research.

ORBIT
Uranus follows an elliptical orbit around the Sun.
Its orbit is much farther from the Sun than Earth's orbit.
Sunlight at Uranus is much weaker than sunlight received by Earth.
Uranus travels around the Sun at an average distance of about 2.9 billion kilometers.
Its long orbital period produces extremely long seasons.
A single Uranian year lasts about 84 Earth years.
Each season can last roughly 21 Earth years.
Uranus completes only a small fraction of its orbit during a human lifetime.
Its orbital motion is governed mainly by the Sun's gravity.
The gravitational influence of other planets also produces small orbital changes.

ROTATION AND AXIAL TILT
Uranus rotates once approximately every 17 hours and 14 minutes.
Its rotation axis is tilted by about 97.8 degrees.
This extreme tilt makes Uranus appear to rotate on its side.
The extreme axial tilt produces unusual seasonal illumination.
During part of its orbit, one pole can face the Sun for many years.
During another part of the orbit, the opposite pole receives prolonged sunlight.
Near the equinoxes, sunlight reaches both hemispheres more evenly.
The unusual rotation is one of Uranus's defining characteristics.
Uranus rotates in the retrograde sense when its tilt is measured conventionally.
Its extreme tilt may have resulted from a major ancient collision or multiple impacts.

ATMOSPHERE
Uranus has a thick atmosphere surrounding its interior.
The atmosphere is composed mainly of hydrogen and helium.
Methane is present in smaller quantities.
Methane absorbs red wavelengths of visible light.
This absorption contributes to Uranus's blue-green color.
The atmosphere also contains trace amounts of other hydrocarbons.
Different atmospheric layers have different temperatures and pressures.
Uranus has clouds and atmospheric haze.
Clouds can contain methane ice at suitable altitudes and temperatures.
The upper atmosphere is extremely cold.

ATMOSPHERIC CONDITIONS
Temperatures near Uranus's visible cloud tops can fall to about -224 degrees Celsius.
Uranus has one of the coldest planetary atmospheres known in the Solar System.
Its atmosphere contains strong winds.
Wind speeds can reach hundreds of kilometers per hour.
Atmospheric bands and cloud structures can change over time.
Seasonal sunlight affects atmospheric activity.
The planet's extreme tilt produces unusual seasonal circulation.
Large-scale atmospheric circulation transports heat and material.
Methane absorption and atmospheric haze affect observations of the planet.
Observations show that Uranus is not completely inactive despite its cold environment.

INTERNAL STRUCTURE
Uranus does not have a solid surface like Earth.
Its atmosphere gradually transitions into deeper fluid layers.
The outer atmosphere is dominated by hydrogen and helium.
Below the atmosphere is a dense mantle rich in volatile compounds.
Water is expected to be a major component of the deep interior.
Ammonia and methane are also expected to occur in the interior.
Increasing pressure and temperature change the properties of these materials.
A rocky core is expected near the center of Uranus.
The transition between atmospheric and deeper layers is gradual.
The interior is responsible for much of Uranus's mass and density.

ICE GIANT INTERIOR
The term ice giant refers to Uranus and Neptune.
In planetary science, "ice" includes substances such as water, ammonia, and methane.
These materials can exist in unusual high-pressure states inside a planet.
The deep interior of Uranus is extremely hot and highly compressed.
Water may become electrically conductive under extreme interior conditions.
Laboratory experiments study high-pressure water states relevant to ice giants.
Superionic water is one proposed state that may occur deep inside Uranus.
Such exotic states could help explain the planet's magnetic field.
Interior models are based on observations, physics, and computer simulations.
The exact internal structure of Uranus is not directly observable.

RINGS
Uranus has a system of narrow, dark planetary rings.
Thirteen known main rings surround Uranus.
The rings are generally much less bright than Saturn's rings.
Most Uranian rings are narrow and relatively dark.
The epsilon ring is one of the most prominent Uranian rings.
Uranus's rings were discovered through stellar occultation observations in 1977.
A stellar occultation occurs when a planet or ring passes in front of a star.
The rings blocked starlight at specific times during the observations.
Voyager 2 later observed the rings in greater detail.
Some rings contain particles ranging from dust-sized material to larger fragments.

MOONS
Uranus has 28 known moons.
Its major moons include Titania, Oberon, Ariel, Umbriel, and Miranda.
The major moons are composed largely of mixtures of rock and ice.
Titania is the largest moon of Uranus.
Oberon is the second-largest major Uranian moon.
Ariel is one of the brightest major Uranian moons.
Umbriel has a notably dark surface.
Miranda is famous for its unusually varied and fractured terrain.
The moons interact gravitationally with Uranus and the ring system.
Most major Uranian moons have names associated with literary characters.

MIRANDA
Miranda is one of Uranus's most geologically unusual moons.
It has a diameter of roughly 470 kilometers.
Its surface contains large regions with different textures and brightness.
Miranda has enormous fault systems and steep cliffs.
It contains distinctive regions called coronae.
Coronae are large complex terrains associated with geological deformation.
Miranda may have experienced strong internal geological activity in the past.
Its unusual surface suggests a complicated geological history.
One of Miranda's cliffs is among the highest known planetary cliffs.
Voyager 2 obtained the most detailed close-up observations of Miranda.

MAGNETOSPHERE
Uranus has a planetary magnetic field.
Its magnetic field is unusually tilted relative to its rotation axis.
The magnetic axis is tilted by about 59 degrees relative to the rotation axis.
The magnetic field is also significantly offset from the planet's center.
This geometry produces an unusual magnetosphere.
The magnetosphere is the region dominated by Uranus's magnetic field.
Solar wind interacts with the magnetosphere.
The interaction changes as Uranus rotates and moves around the Sun.
Uranus's unusual magnetic field may provide clues about its interior.
Voyager 2 made important measurements of Uranus's magnetosphere.

EXPLORATION
Voyager 2 is the only spacecraft to have visited Uranus closely.
Voyager 2 performed its Uranus flyby in January 1986.
The spacecraft passed Uranus at a distance of roughly 81,500 kilometers from its cloud tops.
Voyager 2 studied the atmosphere, rings, moons, and magnetic environment.
It discovered several previously unknown moons.
It also discovered additional ring structures.
The spacecraft revealed detailed surface features on several moons.
Voyager 2 provided the first close observations of Uranus.
No spacecraft has yet entered orbit around Uranus.

FUTURE EXPLORATION
Uranus remains a major target for future planetary exploration.
An orbiter combined with an atmospheric probe has been proposed for Uranus.
A dedicated mission could study the atmosphere for an extended period.
An orbiter could investigate the rings and moons repeatedly.
A probe could directly measure atmospheric composition and structure.
Long-duration observations could reveal seasonal atmospheric changes.
A future mission could investigate Uranus's unusual magnetic field.
Measurements of the interior could improve models of ice giants.
Uranus exploration could also improve understanding of similar exoplanets.
Mission concepts and priorities can change as space agencies evaluate future programs.

SCIENTIFIC IMPORTANCE
Uranus is important for understanding ice giant formation.
Its unusual axial tilt provides a natural laboratory for studying extreme seasons.
Its rings help scientists study small-particle dynamics around planets.
Its moons provide evidence of geological and orbital evolution.
Its atmosphere helps researchers study circulation under extreme conditions.
Its magnetic field provides information about planetary interiors.
Uranus can be compared with Neptune to understand differences between ice giants.
Ice giants are common-sized planets in exoplanet observations.
Studying Uranus helps connect Solar System science with exoplanet science.
Uranus remains one of the least directly explored major planets.

KEY TERMS
Ice giant: A planet dominated by volatile-rich material such as water, ammonia, and methane.
Axial tilt: The angle between a planet's rotation axis and a reference perpendicular to its orbital plane.
Retrograde rotation: Rotation opposite to the conventional direction used for most planets.
Atmosphere: The gaseous outer layer surrounding a planet.
Magnetosphere: The region controlled by a planet's magnetic field.
Stellar occultation: An event in which a planetary body blocks light from a background star.
Corona: A large distinctive geological terrain found on Miranda.
Volatile compounds: Substances that can vaporize relatively easily under planetary conditions.
Superionic water: A high-pressure state of water proposed to occur inside ice giants.
Voyager 2: The spacecraft that performed the first and only close flyby of Uranus.
